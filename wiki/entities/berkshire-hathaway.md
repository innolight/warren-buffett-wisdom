# Berkshire Hathaway Inc.

Diversified holding company chaired by [[warren-buffett]], with [[charlie-munger]] as vice chairman and partner in managing the controlled companies. By 1984, the headline metric is per-share book value, which serves as a conservative proxy for per-share intrinsic value — the measurement Buffett identifies as the one that really counts.

## History

Formed in 1955 by the merger of Berkshire Fine Spinning Associates and Hathaway Manufacturing — both New England textile mills. In 1948, the pro forma combined entity earned $18M after tax and employed 10,000 across a dozen mills; in the decade following the merger it produced an aggregate loss of $10M, and by 1964 net worth had fallen from $53M to $22M.

Corporate control changed in 1965. The insurance era began in early 1967 with the purchase of [[national-indemnity]] and its sister National Fire and Marine for ~$8.6M, funded by capital generated from the textile division.

Two structural events in this 1980–1984 stretch reshape the corporate form:

- **December 31, 1980** — divestiture of the Illinois National Bank, forced by the Bank Holding Company Act. Berkshire offered shareholders a proportional exchange option: 24 of ~1,300 shareholders took the basic exchange; 39 took overexchanges; Buffett took the residual. (Gene Abegg, the Bank's founder/operator, died July 1980. Buffett's obituary: "From the time we first met, Gene shot straight 100% of the time.")
- **Mid-1983** — Berkshire/Blue Chip merger completes. Shareholder count roughly doubles (~1,000 → ~2,900). Crucially, the merger was conducted as a business-value-for-business-value exchange (see [[stock-as-acquisition-currency]]).

Notably, share count grew only from 1,137,778 (1965) to 1,146,909 (1983, post-Blue Chip merger) — under 1% net dilution over 19 years while adding everything outside the original textile business. The 1983 letter highlights this directly.

## Owner's manual (1983)

The 1983 letter publishes the **13 owner-orientation principles** that become Berkshire's governing framework — covering corporate-form philosophy, capital allocation, debt policy, retention testing, share issuance, candor in reporting, and refusal to discuss investment activity. See [[owner-orientation]] for the full discussion.

## Why it matters

Canonical case study in capital allocation across the 1980–1984 stretch:

- **Insurance underwriting was poor** — combined ratios deteriorating into the 120s+ — but **float was reinvested into equity holdings** that compounded extraordinarily (GEICO, Washington Post, General Foods). The 1981–82 net-worth gains came primarily from non-controlled equity appreciation, not operating earnings.
- **Berkshire entered structured settlements and loss-reserve assumption businesses** in 1983–84, leveraging unmatched balance-sheet strength as the operational asset. Premier financial strength becomes a competitive advantage in long-tail insurance lines.
- **Wholly-owned operating businesses expand:** [[nebraska-furniture-mart]] acquired 1983; [[buffalo-evening-news]] becomes the dominant Buffalo paper after the Courier-Express closes in 1982.
- **Acquisition discipline visibly applied** — Buffett confesses to nearly completing a major acquisition in 1981 that, on reflection, would have left shareholders worse off. The deal failed for unrelated reasons; "two blank pages depicting this blown deal would be the appropriate centerfold."

## Operating units as of 1984

- **Insurance Group** — [[national-indemnity]] flagship, homestate operations, reinsurance (now including structured settlements and loss-reserve assumptions under Don Wurster; Columbia Insurance as the reinsurance unit). Mike Goldberg took over most insurance oversight from Buffett during 1982.
- **[[nebraska-furniture-mart]]** — 90%-owned since 1983; Mrs. B and family.
- **[[sees-candies]]** — wholly owned via former Blue Chip ownership; Chuck Huggins.
- **[[buffalo-evening-news]]** — wholly owned via former Blue Chip ownership; Stan Lipsey publisher from 1983.
- **[[wesco-financial]]** — 80% owned; Munger chairman from late 1983.
- **Textiles** — still operating but radically reduced; the Waumbec subsidiary terminated in 1980. **Shut down July 1985**; remaining equipment auctioned in early 1986, with net proceeds less than zero. See [[berkshire-1985-letter]] and [[tailwinds-vs-headwinds]] for the post-mortem.
- **Blue Chip Stamps** — merged into Berkshire mid-1983.
- **[[scott-fetzer]]** — acquired right after year-end 1985 for ~$320M plus ~$90M of assumed debt; ~$700M of sales across 17 businesses, led by World Book; Ralph Schey CEO. Projected to push 1986 Berkshire revenues over $2B (more than 2× 1985).

## 1985 — the Halley's Comet year

The 1985 letter inaugurates a new posture in the writing: Buffett begins formally telegraphing that past compound rates will not repeat. Two reasons cited:

- **Market opportunity has compressed.** "We cannot find significantly-undervalued equities to purchase for our insurance company portfolios. The current situation is 180 degrees removed from that existing about a decade ago."
- **Size dampens.** No high-return company Buffett can identify, once it has amassed $1B of equity capital, has subsequently sustained 20%+ ROE for ten years while reinvesting substantially all earnings. The pre-tax earnings requirement to sustain 15% annual growth: $5.7B over the coming ten years (vs. $3.9B the year before — a 48% increase, mathematically tracking the capital base).

Four structural events of 1985–86:

- **Cap Cities/ABC commitment.** 3M shares at $172.50 = $517.5M, with [[capital-cities]] CEO Tom Murphy granted Berkshire's voting rights for an extended period and Berkshire restricted on sale of the block. Same friendly-holder model used previously at [[geico]] and [[washington-post]].
- **Scott & Fetzer acquisition.** See [[scott-fetzer]].
- **Fireman's Fund quota-share.** 7% participation in all FFIC in-force business as of September 1, 1985, four-year term. All written through National Indemnity; two-sevenths passed to a new Wesco-organized subsidiary, Wes-FIC. Followed Jack Byrne to FFIC.
- **General Foods sold to Philip Morris.** $338M pre-tax realized gain.

Also: Berkshire's insurance premium volume more than tripled in 1985 off a long stagnation. The drivers — flight to quality after large 1985 insurer insolvencies, and Berkshire's willingness to write net lines large enough to be useful to brokers — are treated in [[commodity-business-economics]] and on the source page. Combined ratio improved from 134 (1984) to 111 (1985) but loss-reserve development remained adverse, "as it did in the several years preceding."

A further structural shift noted in 1985: **Berkshire's market price had stopped trading at a discount to intrinsic value** and was occasionally at a modest premium. Buffett flags this as "bad news for the new or prospective owner" — the elimination of the discount means future returns to a new entrant cannot match future business performance unless the discount stays absent or widens to a premium.
