# Nebraska Furniture Mart

Home-furnishings retailer in Omaha, founded by [[rose-blumkin]] (Mrs. B) in 1937 with $500 of savings. Berkshire acquired 90% in 1983 for $55M, leaving 10% with the Blumkin family and optioning 10% to key younger family managers. The single Omaha store generates more furniture, carpet, and appliance volume than all of its Omaha competitors combined — and more than any other home-furnishings store in the United States.

## Why it matters

The canonical Berkshire **low-cost operator** entity. Where [[geico]] is the low-cost archetype in insurance and [[sees-candies]] is the pricing-power archetype in consumer products, NFM is the pure low-cost retail archetype. The economics are extraordinary precisely because the discipline is extreme.

A few comparison points from the 1984 letter:

- **Gross margin:** about half that of Levitz (44.4%) — i.e., NFM operates on ~22% gross margins.
- **Operating expenses:** 16.5% of sales vs. Levitz's 35.6%.
- **Customer savings:** NFM saves its customers an estimated $30M+ annually relative to typical-markup competitors.
- **Geographic reach:** the savings draw customers from far outside Omaha — growth comes from territory expansion, not just local market share.

The disciplines that produce the economics (per Buffett's 1984 distillation of "Blumkin secrets"):

1. Apply unusual energy.
2. Define area of special competence with extraordinary realism, and act decisively within it.
3. Ignore enticing propositions outside that area.
4. Treat counterparties with integrity.

Mrs. B's compressed version: **"Sell cheap and tell the truth."**

## History

- **1917:** Mrs. Blumkin emigrates from Russia at age 23.
- **1937:** Opens NFM with $500 of saved capital from selling used clothing. Named after the American Furniture Mart in Chicago.
- **Early years:** Furniture and carpet manufacturers refused to sell to her under pressure from established Omaha retailers. She circumvented; was sued under Fair Trade laws; won. After one trial, sold the judge $1,400 worth of carpet.
- **1983:** Berkshire acquires 90% for $55M.
- **1983 (one store):** sales >$100M.
- **1984:** sales $115M; Mrs. B receives an honorary doctorate from NYU at age 91.

## The acquisition deal

The transaction itself is the canonical case study in trust-as-deal-economics: "NFM had never had an audit and we did not request one; we did not take an inventory nor verify the receivables; we did not check property titles. We gave Mrs. B a check for $55 million and she gave us her word. That made for an even exchange." ([[berkshire-1984-letter]])
