# Salomon Inc

Investment bank (parent of Salomon Brothers). The Berkshire investment was a $700M 9% convertible preferred purchased in 1987 — at the time, the largest single capital commitment in Berkshire's history. The position is also the source of the most consequential operational intervention of Buffett's career: his service as Interim Chairman during the August 1991 government-bond-trading-scandal crisis.

## Why it matters

Three reasons make Salomon canonical despite the modest investment return:

1. **The 1991 crisis intervention is the canonical Buffett-on-reputation case study.** When Salomon faced existential threat after the Treasury auction scandal, Buffett took over as Interim Chairman for ten months, conducted a public restoration of credibility with regulators (the SEC, the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, the U.S. Treasury, the U.S. Attorney, and the DoJ Antitrust Division), and operationalized a culture-of-compliance reset that almost certainly saved the firm. The 1992 letter: "It is no exaggeration to say that without the combined efforts of Salomon executives Deryck Maughan, Bob Denham, Don Howard, and John Macfarlane, the firm very probably would not have survived." The episode also seeded Buffett's most-quoted internal directive — *lose money for the firm and I will be understanding; lose a shred of reputation for the firm and I will be ruthless* — though the canonical wording appears in subsequent (post-1995) letters.

2. **The convertible-preferred form as Buffett's instrument for an industry he does not understand.** The 1987 letter is explicit:

   > We, of course, have no special insights regarding the direction or future profitability of investment banking. By their nature, the economics of this industry are far less predictable than those of most other industries in which we have major commitments. This unpredictability is one of the reasons why our participation is in the form of a convertible preferred. ([berkshire-1987-letter](../sources/berkshire-1987-letter.md))

   The structure delivers a senior-claim fixed income with an equity option — appropriate compensation for ambiguity about long-term industry economics. This pattern was repeated in 1989 (Gillette, USAir, Champion) and 1991 (First Empire, American Express PERC).

3. **The case for the bet was the people, not the business.** Buffett invested in John Gutfreund (then CEO), not in investment banking as an industry:

   > What we do have a strong feeling about is the ability and integrity of John Gutfreund, CEO of Salomon Inc. Charlie and I like, admire and trust John. We first got to know him in 1976 when he played a key role in GEICO's escape from near-bankruptcy.

   The 1991 scandal showed the limits of even an admired CEO when the institution's compliance culture is inadequate. Gutfreund resigned in 1991. The lesson Buffett extracts in 1995, after the dust has settled: "I had 'no special insights regarding the direction or future profitability of investment banking.' Even the most charitable commentator would conclude that I have since proved my point." ([berkshire-1995-letter](../sources/berkshire-1995-letter.md))

## Structure of the preferred

- $700M cost, October 1987.
- 9% dividend.
- Convertible after three years into Salomon common at $38 per share.
- Mandatory redemption 20% annually 1995–1999 if not converted.

The 1987 letter assesses the position as "mostly medium-term fixed-income" with a conversion kicker. By 1988, Charlie Munger's Wesco letter values the preferred at slightly above cost. The 1991 crisis temporarily impaired the value but the preferred terms (senior to common, with negotiated compounding-rate protections) held up.

The first scheduled $140M redemption occurred on October 31, 1995. The 1995 letter reports the redemption ("Some press reports labeled this a sale, but a senior security that matures is not 'sold.'") and Berkshire retains four more conversion opportunities through 1999.

The 1995 retrospective ranks the Salomon preferred as having "modestly outperformed the fixed-income securities for which it was a substitute" — a passing grade as a fixed-income decision, but a failing grade on the embedded conversion option:

> The Dow Industrials have doubled since I committed to buy the preferred, and the brokerage group has performed equally as well. That means my decision to go with Salomon because I saw value in the conversion option must be graded as very poor. ([berkshire-1995-letter](../sources/berkshire-1995-letter.md))

## The August 1991 crisis

Salomon disclosed in August 1991 that traders had submitted false bids in U.S. Treasury auctions. The disclosure threatened the firm's regulatory standing, including its primary-dealer status. With the firm at risk of losing its license to do business, the board asked Buffett to take over as Interim Chairman; he served from August 18, 1991 until June 1992.

The 1991 letter is restrained about the episode:

> Berkshire's operating managers are so outstanding that I knew I could materially reduce the time I was spending at the company and yet remain confident that its economic progress would not skip a beat … My job is merely to treat them right and to allocate the capital they generate. Neither function is impeded by my work at Salomon.

Buffett notes the appointment of Deryck Maughan as the operational head and refers shareholders to a separate letter in Salomon's own annual report. Ron Olson (of Munger, Tolles & Olson) is credited with the legal coordination across the multiple regulatory authorities. By the time of the 1992 letter, the firm had been pulled back from the brink and ranked second among 311 in *Fortune*'s "improved reputation" survey; Salomon Brothers reported record pre-tax earnings.

## The lessons Buffett does not state explicitly until later

Several inferences from the Salomon episode that appear in the 1987-1995 letters and become more pointed in subsequent reports:

- An investment banker's product is judgment, and judgment cannot be insulated from compensation incentives that reward transactions over restraint.
- Compliance culture is set from the top; a single tolerated misbehavior compounds across an organization.
- The economics of an industry whose key resource is the moment-to-moment trustworthiness of its people are inherently fragile.

The 1989 zero-coupon-bond essay's broader critique of investment-banking ethics ("we believe that they should perform a gatekeeping role, guarding investors against the promoter's propensity to indulge in excess") is a direct extension. The "bartender morality" line from 1989 reads in retrospect as the framework Buffett had developed but Salomon had not.

## Exit: merger into Travelers Group (1997)

In 1996, Berkshire partially monetized the Salomon position by issuing $500M of five-year notes exchangeable into Salomon common at 17.65 shares per $1,000 note — proceeds of $447.1M, with a 3% yield to maturity if held to term. See [berkshire-1996-letter](../sources/berkshire-1996-letter.md).

The full exit came in 1997, when Salomon merged with Travelers Group. Berkshire retained the resulting position as Travelers common (23.7M shares; $604.4M cost; $1.279B market at yearend 1997). Deryck Maughan and Bob Denham were credited in [berkshire-1997-letter](../sources/berkshire-1997-letter.md) with saving Salomon after 1991 and restoring multi-year vitality, with Buffett describing "long-suffering shareholders rewarded." The Travelers position was subsequently sold.

Buffett's relief at the conclusion was audible. He had spent ten months as Interim Chairman navigating the 1991 Treasury auction scandal — an engagement he described as "a play … I would have enjoyed except that I had an unfortunate seat. It faced the stage." His 1997 formulation on the difficulty of extricating himself from both Salomon and USAir: "How can I miss you if you won't go away?" (quoting a country song).

The lesson Buffett drew: operating in an industry whose product is a non-proprietary promise, whose economics are opaque, and whose culture depends on individual integrity under aggressive compensation incentives is doubly difficult. The economics set the incentive structure; the incentive structure corrodes the culture. The Salomon episode validated his original confession in [berkshire-1987-letter](../sources/berkshire-1987-letter.md) — "no special insights regarding the direction or future profitability of investment banking" — and reinforced the discipline of staying within [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md). The financial return was modest (the conversion option "must be graded as very poor" — [berkshire-1995-letter](../sources/berkshire-1995-letter.md)); the reputational and time cost of the crisis intervention was significant.
