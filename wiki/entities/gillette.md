# Gillette

Razor and blade producer; Berkshire's second great global consumer-franchise position after Coca-Cola, and a recurring companion in Buffett's writing on durable competitive advantage.

## Why it matters

Gillette serves a structurally similar role in Buffett's thinking to [[coca-cola]]: a global consumer brand with dominant share (>60% of the world razor/blade market by value, per the 1993 letter), substantial pricing power, and modest incremental capital needs. Together the two are repeatedly treated as the paired exemplars of the modern franchise — the businesses Buffett returns to whenever he is making a point about moats, the inadequacy of beta-as-risk, or the difference between business risk and stock-price risk.

The 1993 articulation is the clearest summary:

> Worldwide, Coke sells about 44% of all soft drinks, and Gillette has more than a 60% share (in value) of the blade market. Leaving aside chewing gum, in which Wrigley is dominant, I know of no other significant businesses in which the leading company has long enjoyed such global power. Moreover, both Coke and Gillette have actually increased their worldwide shares of market in recent years. The might of their brand names, the attributes of their products, and the strength of their distribution systems give them an enormous competitive advantage, setting up a protective moat around their economic castles. ([[berkshire-1993-letter]])

## The 1989 preferred and the 1991 conversion

Berkshire's Gillette position began as a $600M convertible preferred (8 3/4% coupon, ten-year mandatory redemption, $50 conversion price) purchased in July 1989. The structure was one of the four convertible preferreds Buffett bought in 1989-91 (along with Salomon, USAir, and Champion).

In April 1991 Gillette called the preferred. Berkshire converted into 12,000,000 common shares (later 24,000,000 after a 2-for-1 split; 48,000,000 after a second split by 1995).

The 1995 retrospective on the entire convertible-preferred program ranks Gillette as the singular success — "our best holding has been Gillette" — and admits the structural mistake:

> Ironically, though, this is also the purchase in which I made my biggest mistake — of a kind, however, never recognized on financial statements. We paid $600 million in 1989 for Gillette preferred shares that were convertible into 48 million (split-adjusted) common shares. Taking an alternative route with the $600 million, I probably could have purchased 60 million shares of common from the company. … If I had negotiated for common rather than preferred, we would have been better off at yearend 1995 by $625 million, minus the "excess" dividends of about $70 million. ([[berkshire-1995-letter]])

The lesson Buffett extracts: when buying into a business whose long-term economics you are highly confident about, the equity participation almost always dominates the income-coupon participation. The convertible structure pays a fixed-income kicker that is small relative to the foregone equity upside. See also [[mistakes-of-omission]] for the related category.

## Colman Mockler

[[colman-mockler]] was Gillette's CEO from 1975 until his death in January 1991, a few weeks before the preferred conversion. The 1990 letter's obituary section is unusually personal:

> I deeply regret having to end this section of the report with a note about my friend, Colman Mockler, Jr., CEO of Gillette, who died in January. No description better fitted Colman than "gentleman" — a word signifying integrity, courage and modesty. Couple these qualities with the humor and exceptional business ability that Colman possessed and you can understand why I thought it an undiluted pleasure to work with him.

A *Forbes* cover story published days before Mockler's death is cited approvingly: Gillette's success comes "not from marketing savvy (though it exhibits that talent repeatedly) but has instead resulted from its devotion to quality. This mind-set has caused it to consistently focus its energies on coming up with something better, even though its existing products already ranked as the class of the field." Quality-driven product orthodoxy as an enduring competitive feature.

Buffett joined the Gillette board with the 1989 preferred purchase.

## The Sensor era

Mockler's tenure included the Sensor razor launch (1989-90), which became the canonical illustration of Gillette's ability to introduce premium-priced product upgrades that customers willingly bought. The 1989 letter: "If you haven't tried Gillette's new Sensor razor, go right out and get one." The empirical signature of a real franchise: each premium upgrade is accepted by the existing customer base, raising the per-customer revenue while preserving share.
