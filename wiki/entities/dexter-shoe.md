# Dexter Shoe

A New England shoe manufacturer, based in Dexter, Maine, acquired by Berkshire Hathaway in 1993 for 25,203 Berkshire A shares. It became Buffett's most candid self-described mistake — not merely because the business deteriorated, but because the currency used to pay for it compounded the error. The business is a canonical case study on two interlocking fronts: (1) how quickly a moat in commodity manufacturing can evaporate against low-cost foreign competition, and (2) why paying with undervalued shares for any acquisition — let alone a deteriorating one — is doubly destructive.

## History

Berkshire acquired Dexter in 1993, paying 25,203 Berkshire A shares. The domestic shoe industry was already under pressure from imports, but Buffett judged the business to have a durable competitive position. That judgment proved wrong. By the late 1990s, 93% of U.S. shoe consumption was imported, and low-cost foreign manufacturing had rendered Dexter's competitive position essentially worthless.

In 1997 Buffett alluded to the cost of stock-financed acquisitions in aggregate without naming Dexter explicitly — see [berkshire-1997-letter](../sources/berkshire-1997-letter.md) for the "Confession" essay. By 1999 he acknowledged the operational deterioration, attributing it to industry economics rather than management failures — see [berkshire-1999-letter](../sources/berkshire-1999-letter.md). The explicit reckoning came in 2000: all remaining accounting goodwill from Dexter was charged off in a single write-down, and Buffett stated plainly that he had made a mistake both in the purchase and in the choice of currency — see [berkshire-2000-letter](../sources/berkshire-2000-letter.md).

By 2001 Buffett enumerated three distinct errors: the purchase itself, paying in Berkshire stock, and procrastinating on operational changes once the deterioration became obvious. The Dexter operations were eventually folded into H.H. Brown — see [berkshire-2001-letter](../sources/berkshire-2001-letter.md).

## Why it matters

**The moat lesson.** Dexter appeared to have a workable competitive position in a niche of domestic shoe manufacturing. It did not. The lesson is not that every manufacturer is vulnerable — it is that commodity manufacturing offers no natural barrier against a competitor with structurally lower labor costs. An economic moat requires either a cost advantage, a switching-cost advantage, or a demand-side advantage (brand/network effects). Dexter had none that could withstand a global labor-cost differential of the magnitude that emerged in the 1990s.

**The stock-as-acquisition-currency lesson.** Paying for an acquisition with shares is an exchange: the buyer gives away a fractional claim on its own business. If those shares are undervalued — as Berkshire shares consistently were and are — the buyer is surrendering something worth more than the market price. When the acquisition also turns out to be a bad purchase, both errors compound: cheap shares for a worthless business. Buffett described this as his worst acquisition precisely because the second mistake (the currency) magnified the first (the target). See [stock-as-acquisition-currency](../concepts/stock-as-acquisition-currency.md).

The combined lesson: overpaying with undervalued equity is not just an error in acquisition analysis. It is an error in understanding what currency you are using.
