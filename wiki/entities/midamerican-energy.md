# MidAmerican Energy

Regulated electric and natural gas utility acquired by [[berkshire-hathaway]] starting in 2000 (stake negotiated in 1999), becoming the seed of what eventually became Berkshire Hathaway Energy — one of the largest energy infrastructure platforms in the United States. MidAmerican marked Berkshire's decisive entry into regulated, capital-intensive infrastructure businesses and established a new capital-deployment model suited to Berkshire's growing size.

## History

- **1999 (deal announced).** Walter Scott, a Berkshire director, introduced the opportunity at a conference in California they both attended. Berkshire committed approximately $2 billion structured to avoid triggering the Public Utility Holding Company Act of 1935 (PUHCA): an 11% fixed-income security plus common and exchangeable preferred stock giving Berkshire just under 10% of voting power but approximately 76% of the economic interest. Walter Scott retained voting control; [[dave-sokol]] was identified as the operating leader. Buffett explicitly flagged in the 1999 letter that further utility commitments could be large — see [[berkshire-1999-letter]].
- **2000.** Berkshire's 76% ownership closes. MidAmerican generates its first meaningful earnings contribution to Berkshire.
- **2001.** MidAmerican adds Yorkshire Electricity (UK distribution). Pre-tax earnings contribution grows substantially.
- **2002.** Two major pipeline acquisitions: Kern River (Wyoming to Southern California) and Northern Natural Gas (16,600-mile system, Southwest to Midwest — the original InterNorth pipeline that was renamed Enron after a 1985 merger with Houston Natural Gas). Together, MidAmerican transports approximately 8% of all U.S. natural gas. HomeServices of America (a MidAmerican subsidiary) becomes the second-largest U.S. residential real estate brokerage. See [[berkshire-2002-letter]].
- **2003.** Berkshire holds 80.5% economic interest (9.9% voting). The UK platform adds Northern Electric (Yorkshire + Northern Electric together serve 3.7 million UK customers). MidAmerican Energy serves 689,000 Iowa electric customers. Kern River and Northern Natural carry approximately 7.8% of U.S. natural gas transport. HomeServices reaches $48.6 billion in transactions. See [[berkshire-2003-letter]].
- **2005.** PUHCA (the 1935 Public Utility Holding Company Act) repealed August 8, 2005 — the structural constraint that had forced the complex preferred/voting arrangement since 1999. On February 9, 2006, Berkshire converted its MidAmerican preferred into voting common shares, raising its stake to **83.4%** of common stock and votes (the 80.5% economic figure reflects dilution from outstanding employee options certain to be exercised). PacifiCorp — serving 1.6 million electric customers across six western states — cleared all regulatory approvals and was expected to close shortly after yearend. Berkshire's investment: $3.4B in MidAmerican common, supplemented by $1.7B of MidAmerican borrowing. PacifiCorp adds +$3.3B to MidAmerican revenues and +$14.1B to assets. HomeServices of America: 19,200 agents across 18 locally-branded firms; $64B in transactions in 2005, up 6.5%. 2005 earnings applicable to Berkshire: $523M. See [[berkshire-2005-letter]].
- **2010.** Berkshire's stake 89.8%. Wind generation reaches 2,909 MW by year-end 2011 — more than any other regulated electric utility in the country; total wind investment committed: $5.4B. Iowa electricity rates are unchanged since MidAmerican became Iowa's major provider in 1999; the state's other major electric utility has raised prices more than 70% over the same period, and in markets where both serve side-by-side, MidAmerican customers' bills are substantially lower. Northern Natural Gas pipeline advances to second in Mastio customer-satisfaction rankings (Kern River holds #1), completing a transformation from dead last (43rd of 43) at the 2002 acquisition to the top tier eight years later. Net earnings $1.238B; earnings applicable to Berkshire $1.131B. See [[berkshire-2010-letter]].
- **2009.** Berkshire's stake at 89.5%. MidAmerican's ten-year record of paying no dividends continues — all earnings reinvested into operations and expansion. Over the prior three years, Iowa and Western utilities earned $2.5B combined while investing $3B in wind generation facilities — deliberately spending more on infrastructure than the businesses earned in that period, consistent with the regulatory compact. Cologne Re (the reinsurance operation previously partially owned) achieves 100% MidAmerican ownership. [[bnsf]] is announced as a future addition to this "regulated utility" reporting segment, given the railroad's similar economics: essential service, heavy capital requirements, regulated returns, long planning horizons. Buffett flags that this segment will deploy "many tens — yes, tens — of billions of dollars of incremental equity capital" over the coming decades. See [[berkshire-2009-letter]].
- **2008.** Record earnings for MidAmerican. Berkshire's stake now 87.4% (diluted). MidAmerican became **#1 among U.S. regulated utilities in wind generation capacity** — $1.8B invested in wind in 2008 alone, while Iowa and Pacific utility operations generated only $1.1B combined in pre-tax earnings. The willingness to invest more in infrastructure than the operations earn is the explicit regulatory compact: fair returns in exchange for genuine capital commitment. Pipeline quality transformed: Kern River Gas Transmission ranked #1 (up from #9 at 2002 acquisition) and Northern Natural Gas ranked #3 (up from #39) in Mastio customer-satisfaction surveys among the 44 rated U.S. pipelines — a complete cultural reversal in six years. Iowa electricity prices held flat since MidAmerican became Iowa's major provider in 1995 — continuing through Berkshire's 2000 acquisition and committed through 2013 — achieved through efficiency and planning rather than rate-seeking. PacifiCorp (acquired 2006) transformed: wind capacity grew from 33MW to 794MW; 98 committees reduced to 28; electricity generation increased with 2% fewer employees. See [[berkshire-2008-letter]].

## Why it matters

**Berkshire's entry into regulated capital-intensive businesses.** Regulated utilities earn defined returns on their rate bases — the capital invested in the infrastructure. The larger the rate base, the larger the permitted earnings. For a company like Berkshire facing the challenge of deploying ever-growing capital at reasonable returns, regulated utilities are an ideal receptacle: they absorb nearly unlimited capital, they earn predictable (if modest) returns set by regulators, and they are permanent businesses. The 1999 investment was Buffett's first recognition that this model suited Berkshire's scale constraints.

**The "allowed returns" model.** Unlike a consumer brand or an insurance company whose economics depend on competitive pricing and customer choices, a regulated utility earns a return authorized by a state or federal regulator. That return is constrained from above (regulators prevent monopoly pricing) and protected from below (regulators allow cost recovery). The result is a business with very low variance in returns — the opposite of Berkshire's insurance operations, but complementary to them as a capital-deployment vehicle.

**The PUHCA structural constraint as an investing lesson.** PUHCA of 1935 imposed strict limits on holding-company ownership of utilities. Berkshire's initial stake was architected around this constraint: maximum economic exposure, minimum voting control. This required financial creativity — separating economic interest from voting rights using fixed-income and exchangeable preferred instruments. The structure is a reminder that regulatory constraints on capital structure can be navigated without surrendering economic return.

**The "social compact" and regulated infrastructure.** The 2009 letter introduces an explicit framing for how Berkshire thinks about regulated businesses: a two-sided compact between the enterprise and the public. Utilities and railroads provide essential services; in return, regulators allow a fair return on the capital deployed. Both sides must honor the arrangement or both suffer. This framing links MidAmerican directly to [[bnsf]] — the two businesses share not just economic characteristics but a philosophical relationship with society and regulation that justifies Berkshire's commitment of permanent capital at moderate returns. See [[inversion]] for the avoidance-framing version of the same principle.

**Dave Sokol and Greg Abel as the operating model.** Sokol evaluated and executed the original acquisition, then ran the operation. Greg Abel managed the pipeline acquisitions and UK integration. This partnership exemplified Berkshire's operating philosophy: find skilled operators, give them capital and autonomy, stay out of the way. The relationship also showed Buffett's ability to evaluate operator quality quickly — the initial investment decision was based on Sokol's track record and character, assessed before closing.

**Seed of a long-duration capital platform.** The 1999 investment was explicitly framed as a potential platform. Over the following years, MidAmerican absorbed billions of dollars of incremental Berkshire capital through pipeline acquisitions, UK utility expansion, and HomeServices growth. The business became the proof-of-concept that regulated infrastructure could absorb Berkshire-scale capital at satisfactory returns.

## The partnership model

"We view MidAmerican as a partnership among Berkshire, Walter Scott, and two terrific managers, Dave Sokol and Greg Abel. It's unimportant how many votes each party has; we will make major moves only when we are unanimous in thinking them wise." ([[berkshire-2005-letter]])

This framing captures how Berkshire operates large subsidiaries: voting control is less important than the quality of the partnership. Even after the PUHCA repeal gave Berkshire formal majority control, Buffett characterized the governance as requiring unanimity among the four parties — reflecting an owner-partnership orientation over a principal-agent one.
