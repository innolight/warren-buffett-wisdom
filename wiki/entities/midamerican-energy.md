# MidAmerican Energy

Regulated electric and natural gas utility acquired by [berkshire-hathaway](berkshire-hathaway.md) starting in 2000 (stake negotiated in 1999), becoming the seed of what eventually became Berkshire Hathaway Energy — one of the largest energy infrastructure platforms in the United States. MidAmerican marked Berkshire's decisive entry into regulated, capital-intensive infrastructure businesses and established a new capital-deployment model suited to Berkshire's growing size.

## History

- **1999 (deal announced).** Walter Scott, a Berkshire director, introduced the opportunity at a conference in California they both attended. Berkshire committed approximately $2 billion structured to avoid triggering the Public Utility Holding Company Act of 1935 (PUHCA): an 11% fixed-income security plus common and exchangeable preferred stock giving Berkshire just under 10% of voting power but approximately 76% of the economic interest. Walter Scott retained voting control; [dave-sokol](../people/dave-sokol.md) was identified as the operating leader. Buffett explicitly flagged in the 1999 letter that further utility commitments could be large — see [berkshire-1999-letter](../sources/berkshire-1999-letter.md).
- **2000.** Berkshire's 76% ownership closes. MidAmerican generates its first meaningful earnings contribution to Berkshire.
- **2001.** MidAmerican adds Yorkshire Electricity (UK distribution). Pre-tax earnings contribution grows substantially.
- **2002.** Two major pipeline acquisitions: Kern River (Wyoming to Southern California) and Northern Natural Gas (16,600-mile system, Southwest to Midwest — the original InterNorth pipeline that was renamed Enron after a 1985 merger with Houston Natural Gas). Together, MidAmerican transports approximately 8% of all U.S. natural gas. HomeServices of America (a MidAmerican subsidiary) becomes the second-largest U.S. residential real estate brokerage. See [berkshire-2002-letter](../sources/berkshire-2002-letter.md).
- **2003.** Berkshire holds 80.5% economic interest (9.9% voting). The UK platform adds Northern Electric (Yorkshire + Northern Electric together serve 3.7 million UK customers). MidAmerican Energy serves 689,000 Iowa electric customers. Kern River and Northern Natural carry approximately 7.8% of U.S. natural gas transport. HomeServices reaches $48.6 billion in transactions. See [berkshire-2003-letter](../sources/berkshire-2003-letter.md).
- **2005.** PUHCA (the 1935 Public Utility Holding Company Act) repealed August 8, 2005 — the structural constraint that had forced the complex preferred/voting arrangement since 1999. On February 9, 2006, Berkshire converted its MidAmerican preferred into voting common shares, raising its stake to **83.4%** of common stock and votes (the 80.5% economic figure reflects dilution from outstanding employee options certain to be exercised). PacifiCorp — serving 1.6 million electric customers across six western states — cleared all regulatory approvals and was expected to close shortly after yearend. Berkshire's investment: $3.4B in MidAmerican common, supplemented by $1.7B of MidAmerican borrowing. PacifiCorp adds +$3.3B to MidAmerican revenues and +$14.1B to assets. HomeServices of America: 19,200 agents across 18 locally-branded firms; $64B in transactions in 2005, up 6.5%. 2005 earnings applicable to Berkshire: $523M. See [berkshire-2005-letter](../sources/berkshire-2005-letter.md).
- **2010.** Berkshire's stake 89.8%. Wind generation reaches 2,909 MW by year-end 2011 — more than any other regulated electric utility in the country; total wind investment committed: $5.4B. Iowa electricity rates are unchanged since MidAmerican became Iowa's major provider in 1999; the state's other major electric utility has raised prices more than 70% over the same period, and in markets where both serve side-by-side, MidAmerican customers' bills are substantially lower. Northern Natural Gas pipeline advances to second in Mastio customer-satisfaction rankings (Kern River holds #1), completing a transformation from dead last (43rd of 43) at the 2002 acquisition to the top tier eight years later. Net earnings $1.238B; earnings applicable to Berkshire $1.131B. See [berkshire-2010-letter](../sources/berkshire-2010-letter.md).
- **2011.** Net earnings $1.331B; earnings applicable to Berkshire $1.204B. Wind capacity reaches 3,316 MW by end of 2012 — still the most of any regulated electric utility. Total wind investment committed: $6B. Two solar projects announced: a 100%-owned project in California and a 49%-owned project in Arizona, totaling approximately $3B to construct. MidAmerican serves 2.5 million U.S. customers (largest supplier in Iowa, Utah, and Wyoming). Pipelines transport 8% of U.S. natural gas. Greg Abel and [dave-sokol](../people/dave-sokol.md)'s legacy confirmed: MidAmerican all-in on capital commitment in exchange for regulated returns. See [berkshire-2011-letter](../sources/berkshire-2011-letter.md).
- **2013.** MidAmerican acquires **NV Energy** for $5.6B — NV Energy supplies electricity to about 88% of Nevada's population. This broadens MidAmerican's regulated customer base into Nevada and "offers many possibilities for large investments in renewable energy." Buffett: "NV Energy will not be MidAmerican's last major acquisition." Net earnings $1,636M; earnings applicable to Berkshire $1,470M (up from $1,323M in 2012). Wind generation capacity: MidAmerican now accounts for **7% of all U.S. wind generation capacity** (up from 6% in 2012). MidAmerican "retained more dollars of earnings — by far — than any other American electric utility" in 2013, which is the key to funding its renewable-energy investment program. Regulated retail customers now served in eleven states. When all current renewable projects are completed, the portfolio will have cost $15B total. Greg Abel leads MidAmerican; Matt Rose (BNSF) and Abel jointly cited as extraordinary managers. See [berkshire-2013-letter](../sources/berkshire-2013-letter.md).
- **2009.** Berkshire's stake at 89.5%. MidAmerican's ten-year record of paying no dividends continues — all earnings reinvested into operations and expansion. Over the prior three years, Iowa and Western utilities earned $2.5B combined while investing $3B in wind generation facilities — deliberately spending more on infrastructure than the businesses earned in that period, consistent with the regulatory compact. Cologne Re (the reinsurance operation previously partially owned) achieves 100% MidAmerican ownership. [bnsf](bnsf.md) is announced as a future addition to this "regulated utility" reporting segment, given the railroad's similar economics: essential service, heavy capital requirements, regulated returns, long planning horizons. Buffett flags that this segment will deploy "many tens — yes, tens — of billions of dollars of incremental equity capital" over the coming decades. See [berkshire-2009-letter](../sources/berkshire-2009-letter.md).
- **2008.** Record earnings for MidAmerican. Berkshire's stake now 87.4% (diluted). MidAmerican became **#1 among U.S. regulated utilities in wind generation capacity** — $1.8B invested in wind in 2008 alone, while Iowa and Pacific utility operations generated only $1.1B combined in pre-tax earnings. The willingness to invest more in infrastructure than the operations earn is the explicit regulatory compact: fair returns in exchange for genuine capital commitment. Pipeline quality transformed: Kern River Gas Transmission ranked #1 (up from #9 at 2002 acquisition) and Northern Natural Gas ranked #3 (up from #39) in Mastio customer-satisfaction surveys among the 44 rated U.S. pipelines — a complete cultural reversal in six years. Iowa electricity prices held flat since MidAmerican became Iowa's major provider in 1995 — continuing through Berkshire's 2000 acquisition and committed through 2013 — achieved through efficiency and planning rather than rate-seeking. PacifiCorp (acquired 2006) transformed: wind capacity grew from 33MW to 794MW; 98 committees reduced to 28; electricity generation increased with 2% fewer employees. See [berkshire-2008-letter](../sources/berkshire-2008-letter.md).

## 2014: Renamed BHE; AltaLink acquisition; renewables leadership

In 2014, MidAmerican Energy was renamed **Berkshire Hathaway Energy (BHE)** — signaling its transition from a single state utility to a major national energy platform.

Key 2014 developments:

- **AltaLink acquisition** ($3 billion CAD): an electric transmission system serving approximately 85% of Alberta's population. This extended BHE's international reach (already including UK utilities) and broadened the earnings base across additional regulatory jurisdictions.
- **Renewables scale:** BHE now accounts for **6% of U.S. wind generation capacity** and **7% of U.S. solar generation capacity** — from a standing start ten years prior.
- **Geographic breadth:** regulated retail customers in **eleven states** — broader than any other U.S. utility. Two large pipelines deliver 8% of U.S. natural gas consumption. UK and Philippines electric businesses in addition.
- **100% earnings retention:** BHE retained more dollars of earnings in 2014 than any other American electric utility. This retention policy — unusual among utilities, which typically pay substantial dividends — is the engine of BHE's investment program. Regulators view it as an advantage: BHE has capital to invest in infrastructure without needing to come to capital markets.
- **Structural advantage: earnings diversity.** BHE's multiple regulatory jurisdictions (eleven U.S. states, UK, Philippines, Canada) mean no single regulatory body can materially harm the company. This diversity plus the inherent advantage of a strong parent (Berkshire) has allowed BHE and its subsidiaries to significantly lower their cost of debt, benefiting both owners and customers.
- **Financial results:** net earnings $2,095M (up from $1,636M); earnings applicable to Berkshire $1,882M (up from $1,470M). Renewables projects underway will bring the total committed renewables portfolio to $15B when complete.

Buffett's long-term framing: "We will continue to buy and build utility operations throughout the world for decades to come." ([berkshire-2014-letter](../sources/berkshire-2014-letter.md))

## 2015: Renewables leadership and the rate-price social compact

By 2015, Berkshire Hathaway Energy had become the dominant U.S. utility investor in renewable energy.

**Renewables scale:** BHE owns and operates 4,423 megawatts of wind generation — **six times the generation of the runner-up utility** — representing **7% of the country's wind generation capacity**. BHE also accounts for **6% of U.S. solar generation capacity**. Total renewables investment: $16B committed. In Iowa, wind-generated megawatt-hours equaled **47% of all megawatt-hours sold to retail customers** in 2015; committed projects will take that to **58% in 2017**. BHE made additional commitments at the Paris Climate Change Conference.

**Rate leadership.** BHE's average Iowa retail rate was 6.8¢ per KWH — vs. Alliant (the other major Iowa utility) at 9.5¢, neighboring states averaging 9.0¢–9.7¢, and a national average of 10.4¢. This 35% price advantage against the national average is the economic expression of the social compact: maximize capital investment in capacity and efficiency, earn regulatory goodwill, maintain customer loyalty.

**Operational efficiency evidence.** When BHE acquired its Iowa utility in 1999, it employed 3,700 people and produced 19 million megawatt-hours. By 2015: 3,500 employees and 29 million megawatt-hours — major output gain with a headcount reduction, achieved without a single rate increase in 16 years, while industry rates rose 44%. PacifiCorp (acquired 2006): employees fell from 6,750 to 5,700 while generation rose from 52.6M to 56.3M megawatt-hours; accident rate fell from 3.4 to 0.85 per 100 employees. BHE now ranks in the industry's top decile for safety.

**No dividends, still.** BHE has never paid a dividend to Berkshire since Berkshire assumed ownership. No investor-owned utility comes close to BHE's reinvestment rate — a structural feature that enables perpetual expansion of the rate base.

**Financial results:** Net earnings $2,370M (up from $2,095M in 2014); earnings applicable to Berkshire $2,132M (up from $1,882M). BHE and BNSF together invested $11.6B in plant and equipment in 2015. 2016 outlook: Buffett expects increased after-tax earnings at BHE. ([berkshire-2015-letter](../sources/berkshire-2015-letter.md))

## 2023: The regulatory compact breaks — Buffett's admitted "costly mistake"

The 2023 letter marks the sharpest reversal in tone toward BHE in the entire series, and one of Buffett's most direct admissions of a capital-allocation error. BHE was the year's "even more severe earnings disappointment." The operating businesses — electric utilities and gas pipelines — "performed about as expected," but "the regulatory climate in a few states has raised the specter of zero profitability or even bankruptcy," citing the actual bankruptcy of California's largest utility (PG&E) and a current threat in Hawaii (Hawaiian Electric).

The diagnosis goes to the foundation of the model documented throughout this page. For over a century, electric utilities raised capital on "a state-by-state promise of a fixed return on equity," letting them make massive forward-looking investments in capacity that would be needed years later. Buffett notes the regulators' own logic was a **"margin of safety" approach** — build ahead of demand so the lights stay on — that "seemed sensible to regulators, investors and the public." "Now, the fixed-but-satisfactory-return pact has been broken in a few states, and investors are becoming apprehensive that such ruptures may spread." Climate change compounds the worry: forest-fire losses (Berkshire booked a best-estimate charge) are rising with more frequent convective storms, and underground transmission — long avoided because of "staggering costs" — may be required.

The admission: **"I did not anticipate or even consider the adverse developments in regulatory returns and, along with Berkshire's two partners at BHE, I made a costly mistake in not doing so."** This recasts the social-compact thesis as conditional on regulators honoring their side: when a few states break the fixed-return pact, the entire premise that justified committing permanent capital at moderate returns is thrown into question. Buffett warns the endgame for the industry "may be ominous" — certain utilities "might no longer attract the savings of American citizens and will be forced to adopt the public-power model" (as Nebraska chose in the 1930s). Yet he reaffirms that both BHE and Berkshire "are structured to survive negative surprises" — while adding the discipline line: "we will not knowingly throw good money after bad." BHE's multi-state Western transmission project, initiated in 2006 and still years from completion, will eventually serve 10 states comprising 30% of the continental U.S. acreage. ([berkshire-2023-letter](../sources/berkshire-2023-letter.md))

This is the regulated-infrastructure counterpart to a [risk-vs-volatility](../concepts/risk-vs-volatility.md) lesson: the durability of the "allowed returns" model depends on a political/regulatory equilibrium that is not guaranteed, and the failure mode is not volatility but permanent impairment if the compact ruptures.

## Why it matters

**Berkshire's entry into regulated capital-intensive businesses.** Regulated utilities earn defined returns on their rate bases — the capital invested in the infrastructure. The larger the rate base, the larger the permitted earnings. For a company like Berkshire facing the challenge of deploying ever-growing capital at reasonable returns, regulated utilities are an ideal receptacle: they absorb nearly unlimited capital, they earn predictable (if modest) returns set by regulators, and they are permanent businesses. The 1999 investment was Buffett's first recognition that this model suited Berkshire's scale constraints.

**The "allowed returns" model.** Unlike a consumer brand or an insurance company whose economics depend on competitive pricing and customer choices, a regulated utility earns a return authorized by a state or federal regulator. That return is constrained from above (regulators prevent monopoly pricing) and protected from below (regulators allow cost recovery). The result is a business with very low variance in returns — the opposite of Berkshire's insurance operations, but complementary to them as a capital-deployment vehicle.

**The PUHCA structural constraint as an investing lesson.** PUHCA of 1935 imposed strict limits on holding-company ownership of utilities. Berkshire's initial stake was architected around this constraint: maximum economic exposure, minimum voting control. This required financial creativity — separating economic interest from voting rights using fixed-income and exchangeable preferred instruments. The structure is a reminder that regulatory constraints on capital structure can be navigated without surrendering economic return.

**The "social compact" and regulated infrastructure.** The 2009 letter introduces an explicit framing for how Berkshire thinks about regulated businesses: a two-sided compact between the enterprise and the public. Utilities and railroads provide essential services; in return, regulators allow a fair return on the capital deployed. Both sides must honor the arrangement or both suffer. This framing links MidAmerican directly to [bnsf](bnsf.md) — the two businesses share not just economic characteristics but a philosophical relationship with society and regulation that justifies Berkshire's commitment of permanent capital at moderate returns. See [inversion](../concepts/inversion.md) for the avoidance-framing version of the same principle.

**Dave Sokol and Greg Abel as the operating model.** Sokol evaluated and executed the original acquisition, then ran the operation. Greg Abel managed the pipeline acquisitions and UK integration. This partnership exemplified Berkshire's operating philosophy: find skilled operators, give them capital and autonomy, stay out of the way. The relationship also showed Buffett's ability to evaluate operator quality quickly — the initial investment decision was based on Sokol's track record and character, assessed before closing.

**Seed of a long-duration capital platform.** The 1999 investment was explicitly framed as a potential platform. Over the following years, MidAmerican absorbed billions of dollars of incremental Berkshire capital through pipeline acquisitions, UK utility expansion, and HomeServices growth. The business became the proof-of-concept that regulated infrastructure could absorb Berkshire-scale capital at satisfactory returns.

## The partnership model

"We view MidAmerican as a partnership among Berkshire, Walter Scott, and two terrific managers, Dave Sokol and Greg Abel. It's unimportant how many votes each party has; we will make major moves only when we are unanimous in thinking them wise." ([berkshire-2005-letter](../sources/berkshire-2005-letter.md))

This framing captures how Berkshire operates large subsidiaries: voting control is less important than the quality of the partnership. Even after the PUHCA repeal gave Berkshire formal majority control, Buffett characterized the governance as requiring unanimity among the four parties — reflecting an owner-partnership orientation over a principal-agent one.
