# MidAmerican Energy

Regulated electric and natural gas utility acquired by [[berkshire-hathaway]] starting in 2000 (stake negotiated in 1999), becoming the seed of what eventually became Berkshire Hathaway Energy — one of the largest energy infrastructure platforms in the United States. MidAmerican marked Berkshire's decisive entry into regulated, capital-intensive infrastructure businesses and established a new capital-deployment model suited to Berkshire's growing size.

## History

- **1999 (deal announced).** Walter Scott, a Berkshire director, introduced the opportunity at a conference in California they both attended. Berkshire committed approximately $2 billion structured to avoid triggering the Public Utility Holding Company Act of 1935 (PUHCA): an 11% fixed-income security plus common and exchangeable preferred stock giving Berkshire just under 10% of voting power but approximately 76% of the economic interest. Walter Scott retained voting control; [[dave-sokol]] was identified as the operating leader. Buffett explicitly flagged in the 1999 letter that further utility commitments could be large — see [[berkshire-1999-letter]].
- **2000.** Berkshire's 76% ownership closes. MidAmerican generates its first meaningful earnings contribution to Berkshire.
- **2001.** MidAmerican adds Yorkshire Electricity (UK distribution). Pre-tax earnings contribution grows substantially.
- **2002.** Two major pipeline acquisitions: Kern River (Wyoming to Southern California) and Northern Natural Gas (16,600-mile system, Southwest to Midwest — the original InterNorth pipeline that was renamed Enron after a 1985 merger with Houston Natural Gas). Together, MidAmerican transports approximately 8% of all U.S. natural gas. HomeServices of America (a MidAmerican subsidiary) becomes the second-largest U.S. residential real estate brokerage. See [[berkshire-2002-letter]].
- **2003.** Berkshire holds 80.5% economic interest (9.9% voting). The UK platform adds Northern Electric (Yorkshire + Northern Electric together serve 3.7 million UK customers). MidAmerican Energy serves 689,000 Iowa electric customers. Kern River and Northern Natural carry approximately 7.8% of U.S. natural gas transport. HomeServices reaches $48.6 billion in transactions. See [[berkshire-2003-letter]].

## Why it matters

**Berkshire's entry into regulated capital-intensive businesses.** Regulated utilities earn defined returns on their rate bases — the capital invested in the infrastructure. The larger the rate base, the larger the permitted earnings. For a company like Berkshire facing the challenge of deploying ever-growing capital at reasonable returns, regulated utilities are an ideal receptacle: they absorb nearly unlimited capital, they earn predictable (if modest) returns set by regulators, and they are permanent businesses. The 1999 investment was Buffett's first recognition that this model suited Berkshire's scale constraints.

**The "allowed returns" model.** Unlike a consumer brand or an insurance company whose economics depend on competitive pricing and customer choices, a regulated utility earns a return authorized by a state or federal regulator. That return is constrained from above (regulators prevent monopoly pricing) and protected from below (regulators allow cost recovery). The result is a business with very low variance in returns — the opposite of Berkshire's insurance operations, but complementary to them as a capital-deployment vehicle.

**The PUHCA structural constraint as an investing lesson.** PUHCA of 1935 imposed strict limits on holding-company ownership of utilities. Berkshire's initial stake was architected around this constraint: maximum economic exposure, minimum voting control. This required financial creativity — separating economic interest from voting rights using fixed-income and exchangeable preferred instruments. The structure is a reminder that regulatory constraints on capital structure can be navigated without surrendering economic return.

**Dave Sokol and Greg Abel as the operating model.** Sokol evaluated and executed the original acquisition, then ran the operation. Greg Abel managed the pipeline acquisitions and UK integration. This partnership exemplified Berkshire's operating philosophy: find skilled operators, give them capital and autonomy, stay out of the way. The relationship also showed Buffett's ability to evaluate operator quality quickly — the initial investment decision was based on Sokol's track record and character, assessed before closing.

**Seed of a long-duration capital platform.** The 1999 investment was explicitly framed as a potential platform. Over the following years, MidAmerican absorbed billions of dollars of incremental Berkshire capital through pipeline acquisitions, UK utility expansion, and HomeServices growth. The business became the proof-of-concept that regulated infrastructure could absorb Berkshire-scale capital at satisfactory returns.
