# Blue Chip Stamps

Trading-stamp company turned holding entity, a Berkshire affiliate from the 1970s through mid-1983. Berkshire's equity interest grew from ~36.5% in 1977 to ~60% by 1980, then to 100% via the 1983 merger that folded Blue Chip's subsidiaries directly into Berkshire.

## History

A trading-stamp business in long-term volume decline — by the time of the 1984 letter, Buffett notes that sales volume had shrunk to about 5% of its size at Berkshire's entry. The stamp business itself was not the asset; the cash flow it threw off, plus the corporate vehicle, were used to acquire what became some of Berkshire's most important holdings.

Key Blue Chip subsidiaries during the Berkshire-affiliate era:
- [sees-candies](sees-candies.md) (99%) — acquired 1972
- [wesco-financial](wesco-financial.md) (80%) — acquired
- [buffalo-evening-news](buffalo-evening-news.md) (100%) — acquired 1977

[charlie-munger](../people/charlie-munger.md) was Chairman of Blue Chip through the merger period.

## The 1983 merger

Mid-1983 — Berkshire and Blue Chip merged, structured by Buffett's stated standard as a *business-value-for-business-value* exchange (see [stock-as-acquisition-currency](../concepts/stock-as-acquisition-currency.md)). Less than 0.1% of shares of either company voted against; no appraisal requests. Berkshire shares outstanding moved from 1,137,778 (1965) to 1,146,909 (1983) — under 1% net dilution over 19 years.

## Why it matters

Two reasons:

1. **A vehicle for permanent compounding via marginal businesses.** Blue Chip's diminishing trading-stamp business generated cash that funded great acquisitions (See's, Buffalo News). The lesson, as Buffett notes in the 1984 letter's dividend-policy discussion, is that "by committing available funds to much better businesses were we able to overcome these origins."
2. **A clean example of business-value-for-business-value merger.** The 1983 merger is the exception that proves the rule in Buffett's 1982 [stock-as-acquisition-currency](../concepts/stock-as-acquisition-currency.md) essay — most stock-funded mergers destroy value; this one did not because both sides were valued by the same intrinsic-value yardstick.
