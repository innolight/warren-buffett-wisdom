# General Re

General Re (General Reinsurance Corporation) was the largest U.S. property-casualty reinsurer when Berkshire acquired it in 1998 for approximately $22 billion — the largest deal in Berkshire's history at that time. The acquisition added roughly $15 billion of float and seemed to complete Berkshire's transformation into a global insurance powerhouse. Instead, it became one of Buffett's most instructive failures: a case study in cultural due diligence, the hidden cost of underwriting laxity, the compounding penalty of stock-as-acquisition-currency, and the near-impossibility of exiting a derivatives book once entered.

## History

**The acquisition (1998).** Berkshire closed the deal on December 21, 1998. General Re brought 100% ownership of General Reinsurance Corporation and an 82% stake in Cologne Re, the oldest reinsurer in the world, together operating in 124 countries. Berkshire paid in Berkshire shares — a fact Buffett immediately flagged in the 1998 letter: "most of that 48.3% gain [in per-share book value] came from our issuing shares in acquisitions." Because Berkshire stock traded at a large premium to book value, issuing shares inflated book value per share even though no per-share intrinsic value was immediately created. Ron Ferguson, General Re's CEO, was retained to run the operation; Berkshire took over General Re's investment portfolio but explicitly stayed out of underwriting. See [berkshire-1998-letter](../sources/berkshire-1998-letter.md).

The strategic rationale was clear. A publicly-held reinsurer is paid to absorb the volatility that primary insurers want to shed — yet is itself penalized by markets for that same volatility, forcing it to lay off good business or accept worse terms. Berkshire's permanent capital and "Fort Knox of capital" credit strength dissolved that paradox: General Re could now retain more of what it wrote, expand internationally, and stop sacrificing profitable business to protect quarterly earnings. Buffett described it as handing outstanding management freedom they had never had. Yearend 1998 float jumped from $7.4 billion to $22.8 billion.

**The reckoning (2001).** September 11th exposed what Buffett had not seen at deal close: General Re had violated two of his three cardinal underwriting rules. It had been pricing property coverage from historical experience rather than current exposure, thereby assuming terrorism risk for free. It had allowed an aggregation of nuclear, chemical, and biological (NCB) risk large enough that, had terrorists detonated nuclear devices in a U.S. city, the company would have been destroyed. As Buffett wrote in [berkshire-2001-letter](../sources/berkshire-2001-letter.md), General Re's reserve shortfall at that point was approximately $800 million — a sum buried in prior-year "rented suits" that had caused under-pricing, inflated incentive compensation, and premature tax payments. Cost of float in 2001 hit 12.8%, Berkshire's worst since 1984. Joe Brandon was appointed CEO in September 2001; Tad Montross became president.

**The cleanup (2002).** The 2002 letter brought Buffett's fullest admission: "I was dead wrong. Gen Re's culture and practices had substantially changed and unbeknownst to management — and to me — the company was grossly mispricing its current business." A $1.31 billion catch-up reserve charge was taken. NCB aggregation was reduced to tolerable levels. Brandon and Montross reset the underwriting mandate: write only properly-priced business, whatever the effect on volume; size simply does not count. By yearend 2002, General Re and National Indemnity were the only two AAA-rated reinsurers among world majors. See [berkshire-2002-letter](../sources/berkshire-2002-letter.md).

**Gen Re Securities and the derivatives overhang (2002–2003).** General Re came with a derivatives dealer, Gen Re Securities, that Buffett and Munger recognized as dangerous at the time of the 1998 merger. They tried to sell it and failed, then began winding it down. The pace was slow. As Buffett confessed in [berkshire-2003-letter](../sources/berkshire-2003-letter.md): "Charlie would have moved swiftly to close down Gen Re Securities — no question about that. I, however, dithered. As a consequence, our shareholders are paying a far higher price than was necessary to exit this business." The wind-down cost roughly $272 million pre-tax across 2002–2003 under benign market conditions, with no credit losses. Even so, by yearend 2003 the book still held 7,580 contracts with 453 counterparties, down from 23,218 and 884 at the start. The 2002 letter had codified why: "the reinsurance and derivatives businesses are similar: Like Hell, both are easy to enter and almost impossible to exit."

By 2003, General Re's underwriting business was genuinely fixed — producing $145 million of underwriting profit after years of losses — and the float it contributed ($23.7 billion at yearend 2003) was earning its keep. The lesson, however, was written at significant cost.

**Turnaround confirmed (2012).** In the 2012 letter Buffett describes General Re in the same terms he reserves for Berkshire's best insurance operations: Tad Montross had "observed all four of the insurance commandments," and "General Re's huge float has been better than cost-free under his leadership." He added that he expected it "on average" to continue so. International life reinsurance — part of the original 1998 acquisition thesis — achieved "consistent and profitable growth since we acquired the company in 1998." The 2012 contribution: float of $20.128 billion and an underwriting profit of $355 million. The arc from "I was dead wrong" (2002) to explicit positive exemplar alongside [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md)'s BH Reinsurance (2012) is the complete General Re story as it stands in Buffett's public record.

**"General Re is now a gem" (2013).** The 2013 letter delivers the bluntest possible verdict on the rehabilitation: "It can be remembered that soon after we purchased General Re, the company was beset by problems that caused commentators — and me as well, briefly — to believe I had made a huge mistake. That day is long gone. General Re is now a gem." ([berkshire-2013-letter](../sources/berkshire-2013-letter.md)) Tad Montross's observance of all four underwriting disciplines is cited as the reason. International life reinsurance — "grown consistently and profitably since we acquired the company in 1998" — is specifically highlighted as a long-dated success within the original acquisition thesis. 2013 results: float $20,013M (modestly down from $20,128M in 2012); underwriting profit $283M.

## Why it matters

**1. Cultural due diligence cannot be substituted with financial due diligence.** Buffett studied General Re for decades before the acquisition and saw consistent underwriting discipline and conservative reserving. He detected none of this degrading in the deal process — and he was wrong. The culture had shifted silently before the merger closed. No amount of financial modeling catches a change in institutional norms. For acquirers, this is the enduring lesson: assets transfer cleanly; culture does not. The market-share-over-profit drive that infected General Re's underwriting force — "it is difficult for able, hard-driving professionals to curb their urge to prevail over competitors" — is an instance of [institutional-imperative](../concepts/institutional-imperative.md) that due diligence rarely surfaces.

**2. Float purchased with underwriting losses is debt, not free capital.** The acquisition rationale centered on adding billions of float to Berkshire's permanent-capital structure. That logic holds — but only if the float is obtained at low or negative cost. When under-reserving causes under-pricing, which causes further under-reserving, the float's apparent cost is a fiction. The $800 million reserve catch-up in 2001 and the $1.31 billion charge in 2002 represented costs that had been deferred, not avoided. Float that carries a 12.8% annual cost (as in 2001) is worse than a bond. The General Re episode is the sharpest illustration in Berkshire's history that float quality, not float quantity, is what matters — and that quality is only visible in hindsight. See [float](../concepts/float.md).

**3. "I was dead wrong" as a model of accountability.** Buffett did not attribute the General Re failures to September 11th, the soft reinsurance market, or prior management. He owned the error personally and specifically. This matters beyond the instance: the 2002 admission is a template for how an acquirer should respond when an acquisition exposes its own judgment to have been flawed. Accountability here is not merely virtuous — it is informative. The precise diagnosis (mispricing, NCB aggregation, reserve inadequacy) gave readers a map of what had gone wrong, and the corrective actions gave a standard against which to evaluate whether the fix was real.

**4. The compounding cost of stock as acquisition currency.** Berkshire issued shares trading at a large premium to acquire General Re, then spent years repairing the acquired business. The cost of the share issuance does not diminish as the business underperforms — it compounds. Each Berkshire share issued in 1998 represented a perpetual claim on Berkshire's future earnings; that claim persisted through every year of underwriting losses, reserve charges, and derivatives write-downs that followed. This is the specific mechanism that makes stock-financed acquisitions of businesses that later disappoint so punishing: unlike a cash deal, where the sunk cost is fixed, a share issuance is a perpetual liability. See [stock-as-acquisition-currency](../concepts/stock-as-acquisition-currency.md).

**5. The derivatives roach motel.** Gen Re Securities illustrated at small scale what Buffett described at large scale in the canonical derivatives essay of [berkshire-2002-letter](../sources/berkshire-2002-letter.md): entering a derivatives book is easy; exiting one is not. Counterparties, contract tenors, and collateral arrangements create stickiness that no amount of managerial will fully dissolves. Even in an orderly, benign market — no credit losses, no financial crisis — the Gen Re Securities wind-down took years and cost hundreds of millions. The lesson is architectural: never acquire a derivatives book you do not intend to run, because "intent to exit" is not a strategy. The dithering confession carries weight beyond self-criticism: it shows that even a fully-informed decision-maker, who recognized the problem at deal close, could not simply will his way to a swift exit.
