# Fruit of the Loom

Fruit of the Loom is an American apparel company best known for men's and boys' underwear. Berkshire Hathaway acquired it out of bankruptcy in 2002, after management loaded the company with debt following CEO John Holland's 1996 retirement and drove it into insolvency. The acquisition is a case study in buying durable brand value stripped of a ruinous financial structure — the brand and its operating capability survived the corporate failure intact, and the acquisition illustrates Berkshire's philosophy of permanent ownership at a sensible price.

## History

Buffett's personal connection to Fruit of the Loom stretches back to 1955. While working at Graham-Newman Corporation, he watched his bosses Ben Graham, Jerry Newman, and Howard "Micky" Newman orchestrate the purchase of Union Underwear Company — then only a licensee of the Fruit of the Loom name — by Graham-Newman-controlled Philadelphia and Reading Coal and Iron. The price was $15 million, of which $2.5 million was paid in cash (drawn from Union's own balance sheet) and a notable $9 million of the remaining balance was structured as non-interest-bearing notes payable from 50% of earnings above $1 million. Union later bought the licensor of the Fruit of the Loom name outright and was eventually merged into Northwest Industries. At its peak, Fruit earned annual pre-tax earnings exceeding $200 million — see [berkshire-2001-letter](../sources/berkshire-2001-letter.md).

John Holland ran Fruit's operations in its most productive years. In 1996 he retired, and the management that followed loaded the company with debt, partly to fund a series of disappointing acquisitions. Bankruptcy followed. Holland was rehired and undertook a major operational reworking — reducing employment from roughly 40,000 to 23,000, untangling chaotic deliveries, cutting costs, and repairing key customer relationships — before Berkshire stepped in.

Berkshire entered via the bankruptcy proceedings in 2001. The offer to creditors carried no financing conditions, even though it had to remain outstanding for many months. Buffett imposed one unusual condition: John Holland had to be available to continue as CEO. "To us, John and the brand are Fruit's key assets" — [berkshire-2001-letter](../sources/berkshire-2001-letter.md). The acquisition closed in April 2002 — see [berkshire-2002-letter](../sources/berkshire-2002-letter.md).

Micky Newman, Buffett's 81-year-old former Graham-Newman boss, assisted on the bankruptcy bid, closing a half-century loop. "What goes around truly does come around" — [berkshire-2001-letter](../sources/berkshire-2001-letter.md).

By 2003, under Holland's continued leadership, Fruit held 42.3% of men's and boys' underwear sold by mass marketers (Walmart, Target, K-Mart) and had grown its share of the women's and girls' market to 13.9%, up from 11.3% in 2002. Buffett described Fruit's three core assets as: "a 148-year-old universally-recognized brand, a low-cost manufacturing operation, and John Holland, its CEO" — [berkshire-2003-letter](../sources/berkshire-2003-letter.md).

## Why it matters

**Durable brand value outlives failed corporate structure.** Fruit of the Loom entered bankruptcy not because the brand or underlying product was broken but because management overlaid a sound business with excessive debt and misallocated capital into poor acquisitions. The brand — and the operational knowledge to run it — survived. Berkshire's acquisition illustrates a key principle: the price paid to acquire a business in distress is not necessarily distressed if the source of the distress was financial engineering rather than competitive failure.

**Distressed debt as acquisition route.** Berkshire's path into Fruit began with purchases of Fruit of the Loom's public and bank debt at approximately 50 cents on the dollar, which then became the route into whole-company ownership — see [berkshire-2001-letter](../sources/berkshire-2001-letter.md). This reflects a broader Berkshire pattern: distressed-debt positions as options on control of durable assets.

**Capital allocation as the decisive variable.** The business Berkshire acquired was fundamentally the same business Holland had managed to annual pre-tax earnings exceeding $200 million before the 1996 overleveraging. The difference between that outcome and bankruptcy was not the brand, the product, or the market — it was the financial decisions made by management after Holland's departure. What poor [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md) destroys, capital discipline can restore.

**Not quite a turnaround.** Berkshire generally avoids turnarounds on the grounds that they rarely work — see [turnarounds-seldom-turn](../concepts/turnarounds-seldom-turn.md). Fruit is more accurately a financial restructuring: the underlying business was sound, the competitive position intact, and the right operator (Holland) had already been brought back before Berkshire arrived. The distress was structural, not operational. This distinction matters when applying the [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md): Buffett insisted Holland remain as CEO precisely because the operating knowledge was present — what was needed was Berkshire's balance sheet and permanent-ownership philosophy to replace the failed financial structure.
