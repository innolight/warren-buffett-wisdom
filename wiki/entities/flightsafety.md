# FlightSafety International

Aviation pilot-training company acquired by [[berkshire-hathaway]] in late 1996 for approximately $1.5 billion — then the largest acquisition in Berkshire's history and the first that was explicitly capital-intensive. Founded in 1951 by [[al-ueltschi]] and built over four decades into the world's dominant provider of flight-simulator training, FlightSafety is the canonical Berkshire case study for a competitive moat built through massive, hard-to-replicate physical infrastructure.

## History

- **1951.** Al Ueltschi founded FlightSafety while still working at Pan Am (for Juan Trippe), starting with $10,000. He had earlier piloted Charles Lindbergh. The business premise: airlines and corporate pilots require certified simulator training; nobody else was supplying it at scale.
- **1996.** Berkshire acquired FlightSafety for ~$1.5B. The deal was sourced through Richard Sercer, a Tucson aviation consultant who was a long-time shareholder of both FlightSafety and Berkshire, routed via Bob Denham at Salomon. By then the company operated 41 training locations with 175 simulators (ranging from Cessna 210s to Boeing 747s), priced up to $19M each. Roughly half of revenue came from corporate pilots; the rest from airlines and military. The deal structure minimized Berkshire share issuance: 51% of sellers took cash, 41% took A shares, and 8% took B shares — see [[berkshire-1996-letter]].
- **Post-acquisition.** The company continued under Ueltschi's leadership. The simulator fleet expanded to 222 units by 1999. Sister subsidiary [[netjets]] (acquired 1998) sent its entire pilot workforce of ~650 for biannual training at FlightSafety, creating an intra-Berkshire cross-subsidy.
- **2001.** Commercial aviation training was temporarily depressed following September 11th. Ueltschi remained CEO in his mid-eighties. The long-term business was unaffected.

## Why it matters

**Moat through capital-intensity as barrier.** Each professional-grade flight simulator costs up to $19M. A competitive training network requires hundreds of them, spread across dozens of locations, with the aircraft-certification relationships and regulatory approvals that take years to build. A new entrant cannot replicate the platform without spending billions and waiting years. This makes the competitive position remarkably durable — Buffett explicitly flagged the capital intensity in the 1996 letter not as a concern but as the explanation for the business's defensibility.

**Regulatory demand creates recurring, non-discretionary revenue.** Pilots of commercial aircraft and corporate jets are required by aviation authorities to complete periodic simulator training at certified facilities. This demand is non-optional and recurs on a fixed schedule, providing the revenue predictability Buffett prizes.

**The first major capital-intensive Berkshire acquisition.** Before FlightSafety, Berkshire's operating subsidiaries were mostly capital-light businesses — insurance companies, candy shops, newspapers, furniture retailers. The 1996 letter made a point of acknowledging the departure. It demonstrated that Buffett was willing to pay for quality businesses whose moats were built from sunk capital, not just from brands or low costs.

**Founder-operator template.** Ueltschi built the company over four decades and showed no interest in leaving. This fits the Berkshire [[acquisition-criteria]] pattern: find an owner who has made the business their life's work, buy it at a sensible price, and leave the founder in place.

## 2007: The good-business case study

The 2007 letter uses FlightSafety as the canonical "good business" archetype in the [[great-good-gruesome]] framework — excellent but not extraordinary, contrasted against See's Candies.

Since Berkshire's 1996 acquisition with pre-tax earnings of $111M on $570M net fixed assets, through 2007:

| Metric | Amount |
| --- | ---: |
| Depreciation charged | $923M |
| Capital expenditures | $1.635B |
| Net fixed assets (2007) | $1.079B |
| Pre-tax earnings (2007) | $270M (+$159M) |
| Incremental capital invested | ~$509M |

The ratio — $159M in added earnings on $509M of incremental capital — is a respectable return but not extraordinary. The capital requirement is structural: simulator technology must track aircraft model introductions, and each new simulator costs $12M or more. There is no See's-like ability to simply extract earnings without reinvestment.

Buffett's framing: "There is, to follow through on our example, nothing shabby about earning $82 million pre-tax on $400 million of net tangible assets. But that equation for the owner is vastly different from the See's situation." The good-business lesson: competitive moats and good returns are necessary but not sufficient for great economics. Capital-light compounding is the distinguishing feature of the great tier.
