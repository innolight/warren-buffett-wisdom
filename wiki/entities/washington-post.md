# The Washington Post Company

Newspaper and media company. Berkshire's first commercial connection (per the 1983 letter) was Buffett's at age 13 — paper-route delivery. The Berkshire investment of the modern era was established mid-1970s and held permanently. 1,868,600 shares at yearend 1977 ($10.6M cost, $33.4M market); same share count through 1984 ($10.6M cost, $150.0M market). The position is one of the cleanest illustrations in the early letters of long-term compounding through partial-ownership of a great business.

## The "triple-dip" (1985)

The 1985 letter gives WashPost its definitive Berkshire treatment as a case study in compounding through partial ownership. Buffett identifies three reinforcing tailwinds operating simultaneously on a non-selling shareholder's position:

1. **Business value soared upward.** Underlying WPC economics improved through the 1973–85 stretch.
2. **Per-share business value increased considerably faster because of stock repurchases.** Kay Graham executed large share repurchases in 1973–74 specifically when the market had marked WPC stock to ~20 cents on the dollar of intrinsic value.
3. **Discount narrowed, so the stock price outpaced the gain in per-share business value.** Market re-recognition compounded onto the per-share-value gain.

Counterfactual any of the half-dozen "investment-favorite" media names of mid-1973 would have produced ~$40–60M today; Buffett attributes the additional ~$160M of the position's actual outcome "in very large part" to Kay Graham's repurchase decisions and operating skill.

> What we had thought ridiculously cheap a year earlier had become a good bit cheaper as the market, in its infinite wisdom, marked WPC stock down to well below 20 cents on the dollar of intrinsic value. ([[berkshire-1985-letter]])

Buffett's 1985 caveat is the natural sequel: with WPC market cap now above $1.8B versus $100M in 1973, "there is no way that the value can progress from that level at a rate anywhere close to the rate possible when the company's valuation was only $100 million." Multiple re-rating is a one-way ratchet that, once paid, cannot pay again.

Buffett left the WPC board early in 1986 because of the [[capital-cities]] purchase; the position is held going forward to the extent FCC rules allow.

## Why it matters

Three reasons WashPost is a recurring touchstone in the 1980–84 letters:

1. **The "nostalgia" investment lesson.** Buffett's 1982 quip: "nostalgia should be weighted heavily in stock selection." Both of Berkshire's largest unrealized gains in 1982 (Washington Post and GEICO) were companies with which Buffett had formed his first commercial connections in his youth. "The table quantifies the rewards for even long-delayed corporate fidelity."

2. **Share-repurchase case study.** Cited in 1984 among Berkshire's largest holdings whose managements have engaged in "significant share repurchases at times when wide discrepancies existed between price and value." Berkshire's percentage ownership compounded without additional capital outlay — a clean illustration of [[share-repurchases]] as wealth-enhancement for non-selling shareholders.

3. **Dominant-newspaper economics.** Phil Graham (the Post's late publisher) is quoted in the 1984 letter on newspapers as "a first rough draft of history." Like [[buffalo-evening-news]], WashPost is an example of a dominant local franchise with the structural advantages explored in [[moat]].
