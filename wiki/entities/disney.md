# Disney (The Walt Disney Company)

Berkshire became a substantial Disney shareholder in early 1996 through the merger in which Disney acquired [[capital-cities]] for cash and stock. Berkshire's 20 million Cap Cities shares were exchanged for a combination of Disney stock and cash; Buffett notes some additional Disney market purchases as well. This is Berkshire's first significant equity holding in a pure-play entertainment franchise.

## Why it matters

Two reasons make Disney a Buffett page rather than a footnote:

1. **The merger origin is the canonical "ABWA" case.** The 1995 letter retells the genesis: Michael Eisner and Buffett crossed paths on Wildflower Lane in Sun Valley on July 14, 1995, right after Eisner had given a Disney presentation at Herbert Allen's investment-bank conference. Buffett was on his way to a golf game with Tom Murphy (Cap Cities CEO). The three-minute conversation that ensued — with both sides indicating willingness to bend on the stock/cash mix that had previously blocked the deal — set the merger in motion. A contract was put together in three days.

   The episode is the operational illustration of "acquisitions by walking around," Buffett's playful counter-term to "management by walking around." Major deals can originate from incidental encounters when the principals are positioned to act on them.

2. **Buffett's history with Disney as a personal compounding lesson.** Buffett Partnership Ltd. bought Disney in 1966 at a split-adjusted $0.31 per share — "a company selling at only five times rides." Buffett sold in 1967 at $0.48. By yearend 1995, Disney was $66. The 1995 letter's framing: "your Chairman was up to the task of nullifying" the brilliant original decision.

   This is a [[mistakes-of-omission]] retrospective, similar in shape to the Cap Cities 1978–80 sale and the Coca-Cola 50-year wait. Each is a personal admission that the cost of selling — or never buying — a wonderful business is invisible on the financial statements and is potentially the largest cost an investor pays.

## The transaction mechanics

Cap Cities shareholders had a choice:

- Exchange each Cap Cities share for one Disney share plus $65 cash.
- Or request all cash or all stock (subject to allocation depending on other shareholders' choices and decisions made by Disney).

Berkshire requested all stock. The 1995 letter (written in March 1996) reports that allocation results were not yet known but that Berkshire would receive "something over 20 million Disney shares." Additional Disney shares were purchased in the market.

## On Tom Murphy

The same 1995 letter, in the context of the Disney transaction, includes Buffett's most concentrated tribute to Murphy:

> To put it simply, he is as fine an executive as I have ever seen in my long exposure to business. Equally important, he possesses human qualities every bit the equal of his managerial qualities. … When I say that I like to be associated with managers whom I would love to have as a sibling, in-law, or trustee of my will, Murph is the exemplar of what I mean.
>
> If Murph should elect to run another business, don't bother to study its value — just buy the stock. And don't later be as dumb as I was two years ago when I sold one-third of our holdings in Cap Cities for $635 million (versus the $1.27 billion those shares would bring in the Disney merger). ([[berkshire-1995-letter]])
