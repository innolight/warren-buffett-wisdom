# National Indemnity Company

Auto and general liability insurer headquartered in Omaha. Acquired by [berkshire-hathaway](berkshire-hathaway.md) in early 1967 together with sister company National Fire and Marine for approximately $8.6M total — the entry point into insurance, and the company around which Berkshire's float-driven model was built.

## History

In 1967, the two sister companies had $22M of combined premium volume. By 1977, Berkshire's aggregate insurance premium volume — National Indemnity plus all subsequent additions — had reached $151M, an almost 600% increase achieved without issuing any Berkshire shares.

Expansion came through three channels: (1) growth in National Indemnity's traditional liability lines; (2) a new homestate insurance group (Cornhusker 1970, Lakeland 1971, Texas United 1972, Iowa 1973, Kansas Fire 1977); (3) cash acquisitions (Home and Automobile 1971, Kerkling Reinsurance / now Central Fire and Casualty 1976, Cypress Insurance yearend 1977).

## Why it matters

National Indemnity is Buffett's canonical case for **underwriting discipline as the cost of float**. The 1977 letter credits chief underwriter Phil Liesche with "an extraordinary underwriting achievement" — large volume gains paired with excellent margins as competitors retreated after the 1974–75 crisis — and uses the company to articulate the discipline of accepting reduced volume in soft markets, an early form of the [institutional-imperative](../concepts/institutional-imperative.md) argument.

National Indemnity also illustrates the [float](../concepts/float.md) mechanic: insurance reserves plus retained earnings drove Berkshire's investments at cost from $134.6M (1975) to $252.8M (1977), with pre-tax net investment income climbing $8.4M → $12.3M.

## Notable mistakes acknowledged (1977 letter)

- Surety operation initiated 1969
- 1973 expansion of Home & Auto's urban auto marketing into Miami
- An "unresolved aviation fronting arrangement"
- Worker's Comp operation in California (under reorganization)

Buffett's framing: "It is comforting to be in a business where some mistakes can be made and yet a quite satisfactory overall performance can be achieved."

## 1985-1995 — the platform for the super-cat franchise

National Indemnity is also the corporate vehicle through which Berkshire's super-cat reinsurance franchise is written from 1989 onward. Key milestones:

- **1985:** Berkshire's premium volume more than triples; large flight-to-quality from sophisticated insureds. The Fireman's Fund quota-share written through National Indemnity.
- **1986:** Combined ratio improved to 103 from 111. Premium volume now driven by "large risks" ($1-3M+ single policies) and the FFIC contract.
- **1988-89:** Ajit Jain joins; super-cat reinsurance debut post-Hurricane Hugo and California earthquake.
- **1992:** Combined ratio 84.2 in the National Indemnity traditional book (run by Don Wurster). Hurricane Andrew super-cat loss $125M but well-managed.
- **1995:** Three-year traditional-book average combined ratio 85.6. Don Wurster, Rod Eldred, Brad Kinstler, Ajit Jain, John Kizer named as the operating leadership.

The 1995 super-cat examples (illustrating the diversified non-traditional book): Mike Tyson life insurance, Lloyd's mortality coverage on 225 "names," Chinese satellite launch+orbit. See [super-cat-insurance](../concepts/super-cat-insurance.md).

## The NICO cultural model

The 2004 letter contains Buffett's most complete account of *why* National Indemnity's underwriting discipline has been maintained across four CEOs since 1940, when most insurers cannot sustain it even for four years.

**The fear-factor dynamic.** When insurance prices are soft and volume is declining, employees at most companies face a logical trap: they know inadequately-priced business will eventually cause problems, but they also know declining volume leads to layoffs. The institutional self-interest resolves in predictable fashion — rationalize the bad pricing, tell oneself the market will recover, preserve the organization and distribution network. The [institutional-imperative](../concepts/institutional-imperative.md) runs through individual fear of job loss. ([berkshire-2004-letter](../sources/berkshire-2004-letter.md))

**The no-layoff policy as the countermeasure.** NICO has always promised its workforce that no one will be fired because of declining volume, however severe the contraction. This removes the self-interest argument entirely. Employees need not rationalize inadequate pricing to protect themselves; they can evaluate each policy on its merits. NICO is not labor-intensive — as the 25-year table shows, it can live with excess overhead. It cannot live with underpriced business and the breakdown in underwriting discipline that accompanies it. ([berkshire-2004-letter](../sources/berkshire-2004-letter.md))

**The overstaffing discipline.** A no-layoff policy creates its own constraint: the company must avoid overstaffing when times are good, since it cannot shrink easily during bad times. Tom Murphy (Capital Cities CEO) illustrated this to Buffett: hiring an assistant at $20,000/year is really a $3M decision once you factor in raises, benefits, and additional costs over a lifetime of likely employment. One unnecessary hire is not a marginal cost — it is a long-duration commitment. ([berkshire-2004-letter](../sources/berkshire-2004-letter.md))

**Cultural fortitude as the operational result.** NICO has had four CEOs since its formation in 1940; none have bent on underwriting discipline. Living day after day with dwindling volume — while competitors boast growth and receive Wall Street's applause — requires a culture that genuinely does not equate business volume with business success. "It takes real fortitude—embedded deep within a company's culture—to operate as NICO does." ([berkshire-2004-letter](../sources/berkshire-2004-letter.md))

**Don Wurster (CEO since 1989).** Buffett's portrait: "The current managerial star — make that superstar — at NICO is Don Wurster, who has been running things since 1989. His slugging percentage is right up there with Barry Bonds' because, like Barry, Don will accept a walk rather than swing at a bad pitch." By 2004, Wurster had accumulated $950M of float at NICO that Buffett expected to prove negative-cost over time. As insurance prices fell and NICO's volume declined, Buffett noted he would "applaud him ever more loudly." ([berkshire-2004-letter](../sources/berkshire-2004-letter.md))

**25-year performance table (1980–2004).** Written premium ran from $79.6M (1980) to a trough around $54.5M (1999) during soft-market years when NICO let business walk rather than price inadequately, then recovered to $605.6M (2004) as the hard market arrived. Underwriting margins were positive in most years across the 25-year span — a sustained record nearly unmatched in commercial insurance. Float accumulated by Wurster at NICO alone reached $950M by 2004. ([berkshire-2004-letter](../sources/berkshire-2004-letter.md))
