# NetJets

Fractional-ownership operator of business jets, acquired by Berkshire in August 1998 for $725M (half cash, half stock) under its prior name **Executive Jet Aviation (EJA)**. Founded as Executive Jet Aviation; the brand "NetJets" refers to the fractional-ownership program through which the business operates. CEO **Rich Santulli** invented the fractional-ownership industry in 1986 and ran the company through and beyond the Berkshire acquisition. The earlier Berkshire letters (1998–2000) use "Executive Jet" or "EJA" as the company name; from 2002 onward the letters use "NetJets" as the canonical name, matching the consumer brand that survived.

## Overview

NetJets sells fractional interests in business jets and operates the fleet for its owners. The unit economic anchor: a **1/8th share entitles the owner to 100 hours of flying time annually**, with a plane (or another at least as good) made available on a few hours' notice at any of 5,500 U.S. airports. Dead-head hours don't count against the allotment; hours can be averaged across five years. Owners also pay monthly management fees and hourly flight charges — the recurring revenue base. The retail framing in [[berkshire-1998-letter]] is unusually concrete: "In effect, calling up your plane is like phoning for a taxi."

## Why it matters

NetJets is the canonical Berkshire case study in a **category-creating business with a capital-light, customer-owns-the-equipment model**. Two durable themes:

1. **Category creator with structural lead.** Santulli created the industry in 1986. By 1997, EJA accounted for 31% of all corporate jets ordered globally; in 2002, NetJets' industry share by value was 75% — "clients purchased or leased planes from us that were valued at triple those recorded by our three competitors combined" ([[berkshire-2002-letter]]). Network effects compound: more planes flying means lower positioning costs and faster availability, which attracts more owners.
2. **Capital profile inverted from the simulator-training peer.** In the 1999 letter Buffett deliberately pairs NetJets with FlightSafety International (FSI) — Berkshire's other aviation-services subsidiary — to contrast two opposite capital structures within the same parent. FSI must own its productive assets (222 simulators at up to $15M each; $215M of capex in 1999); NetJets does not: "At EJA, in contrast, the customer owns the equipment, though we, of course, must invest in a core fleet of our own planes to ensure outstanding service" ([[berkshire-1999-letter]]). The takeaway: leadership in two adjacent industries can carry profoundly different capital intensities, and the right managerial frame ("how much capital does this business consume per dollar of revenue?") is more important than the surface similarity of "aviation services."

A third theme — the **multi-manufacturer competitive positioning** — has the cleanest articulation Buffett ever gave for an open-architecture distribution moat:

> NetJets is like a physician who can recommend whatever medicine best fits the needs of each patient; our competitors, in contrast, are producers of a "house" brand that they must prescribe for one and all. ([[berkshire-1998-letter]])

EJA offered jets from Boeing, Gulfstream, Falcon, Cessna and Raytheon; competitors owned by single manufacturers could only sell their parents' aircraft. The "buy a fraction, get a fleet" framing in [[berkshire-1999-letter]] extends the point: a customer owning 1/16ths of three different jets gets 50 hours apiece in each, a virtual fleet for a fraction of the cost of one whole plane.

## History

- **1986.** Rich Santulli creates the fractional-ownership industry as Executive Jet Aviation, "by visualizing an important new way of using planes" ([[berkshire-1998-letter]]).
- **1995.** Buffett personally buys a 1/4 share of a Hawker 1000 (200 hours/year) for his family, introduced by Frank Rooney of [[scott-fetzer|H.H. Brown]]. Family logs 900 hours on 300 trips by 1998. Buffett does a customer testimonial ad for EJA before any thought of acquisition.
- **May 1998.** Santulli calls Buffett to discuss selling. A $725M deal (equal cash and stock) is quickly struck.
- **August 7, 1998.** Acquisition closes. 1,000+ customers; 163 aircraft (including 23 "core" aircraft owned or leased by EJA itself for peak demand); ~650 pilots, trained twice yearly at sister-subsidiary FlightSafety International.
- **1999.** Recurring revenues (monthly management + hourly flight fees) up 46%. EJA supply-constrained at ~8% of all business jets manufactured worldwide. European expansion begins ("very expensive — but we will spend what it takes" — [[berkshire-1999-letter]]).
- **2000.** Recurring revenue up another 49%. Margins slim due to European-buildout costs. World leadership in aircraft, service quality and safety reasserted.
- **2001.** Small loss (vs. small profit in 2000): "We made a little money in the U.S., but these earnings were more than offset by European losses" ([[berkshire-2001-letter]]). Brief post-9/11 order spurt; then back to normal. Buffett family flies "in excess of 800" fractional hours that year — likely more than any other family in the program.
- **2002.** Revenues set a record; company again loses money — small U.S. profit offset by European losses. Industry share 75% by aircraft value. "The bald fact is that airplanes are costly to operate." ([[berkshire-2002-letter]]) General Electric noted as NetJets' largest customer.
- **2003.** $41M pre-tax loss: a modest U.S. operating profit, more than offset by a $32M aircraft-inventory loss and continued European losses. Industry share rises to 70% of net sales by value. European management-and-flying revenues had grown 88% / 61% / 77% in 2001–03, but the red ink had not yet been stemmed. "Any company that forsakes Europe, as all of our competitors have done, is destined for second-tier status" ([[berkshire-2003-letter]]).
- **2004–2008.** Losses persist. Debt balloons from $102M at the time of the 1998 acquisition to $1.9B by April 2009. Over eleven years of Berkshire ownership the aggregate pre-tax loss reaches $157M. Without Berkshire's guarantee of NetJets' debt, the company would have been insolvent. Rich Santulli remains CEO throughout, maintaining the safety and service standards that defined the brand.
- **August 2009.** [[dave-sokol]] — the builder and operator of [[midamerican-energy]] — is named CEO. Buffett's self-assessment on the situation: "It's clear that I failed you in letting NetJets descend into this condition. But, luckily, I have been bailed out."
- **Yearend 2009.** NetJets records a $711M loss for the full year, but the Sokol-driven restructuring is already taking hold: debt reduced to $1.4B and the company is "now solidly profitable." Safety and service standards are explicitly cited as unchanged — Rich Santulli's legacy, and the reason Buffett and his family continue to use NetJets for "almost all of our flying," paying exactly the same prices as any other owner. See [[berkshire-2009-letter]].

## Fractional-ownership economics

The 1998 letter is the canonical exposition:

- Purchase a fraction (e.g. 1/8th) of one of EJA's available jet models.
- 1/8th = 100 hours/year of flying time. Dead-head hours don't count. Hours can be averaged over five years.
- Monthly management fee plus hourly flight charge (for the time the customer actually flies).
- A few hours' notice books the plane — or another at least as good — at any of 5,500 U.S. airports.
- "Buy a fraction, get a fleet": owning 1/16ths of three jets gives 50 hours each across three different aircraft profiles. ([[berkshire-1999-letter]])

The thesis on scale: "The company with the most planes in the air worldwide will be able to offer its customers the best service" ([[berkshire-1999-letter]]). By 2002 NetJets had "more than 300 planes constantly on the go in the U.S.," producing positioning-cost advantages competitors with smaller fleets could not match ([[berkshire-2001-letter]]).

## The capital-profile contrast with FlightSafety

The 1999 letter pairs the two aviation services as a deliberate teaching case:

| | FlightSafety International | NetJets (EJA) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Asset ownership | Owns the simulators ($15M each; 222 of them) | Customer owns the planes; EJA holds a core peak-load fleet |
| 1999 capex | $215M | Modest by comparison |
| Margin requirement | High (high capital per dollar of revenue) | Modest pre-tax margins can yield good ROE |
| Throughput constraint | One trainee per simulator at a time | Aircraft delivery from manufacturers (~8% of world output) |

Both are leaders in their fields under founding-entrepreneur CEOs (Al Ueltschi and Rich Santulli). Buffett's point: leadership matters, but capital intensity dictates how much margin a business must extract to clear its return-on-capital hurdle.

## The multi-manufacturer moat

EJA's two largest competitors were each subsidiaries of single aircraft manufacturers, restricted to selling only their parent's planes. EJA offered Boeing, Gulfstream, Falcon, Cessna and Raytheon. The 1998 letter's "physician recommending whatever medicine fits the patient" framing distills the competitive position. The 1999 letter restates it bluntly: "we can give the customer whatever he needs to buy — rather than his getting what the competitor's parent needs to sell."

This is the [[moat]] formulation specific to NetJets: not low cost, not brand, not switching costs (the customer literally owns part of the plane), but **product breadth × scale-driven service ubiquity**. Open architecture is the durable structural advantage.

## Rich Santulli

Founder/CEO, industry inventor, and the principal explanatory variable for NetJets' durability:

> An uncompromising insistence on delivering only the best to his customers is embedded in the DNA of Rich Santulli, CEO of the company and the inventor of fractional ownership. ([[berkshire-2001-letter]])

Like most Berkshire managers, Santulli had no economic need to work and "spends his time at EJA because it's his baby" ([[berkshire-1998-letter]]). Buffett, in turn, became the program's largest individual user (Buffett family logged 800+ fractional hours in 2001), having signed up four years before the acquisition through a Frank Rooney referral. Charlie Munger signed up in 1999.
