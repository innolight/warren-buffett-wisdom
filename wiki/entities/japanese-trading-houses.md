# Japanese Trading Houses (sōgō shōsha)

The five large Japanese general trading companies — **Itochu, Marubeni, Mitsubishi, Mitsui, and Sumitomo** — in which Berkshire Hathaway built passive minority positions beginning July 4, 2019. Each is a highly diversified enterprise operating "somewhat similar to the way Berkshire itself is run." By yearend 2023 Berkshire owned about 9% of each (pledged never to exceed 9.9%), at a total cost of ¥1.6 trillion against a yearend market value of ¥2.9 trillion. The position is the clearest illustration in the letters of Berkshire applying its passive-ownership philosophy outside the U.S., paired with a deliberate currency-hedging structure.

## Why it matters

The Japanese position is a worked example of [[passive-ownership-advantage]] at Berkshire scale and abroad — a large, long-term, non-controlling stake in well-run businesses bought at "friendly" prices, intended to be held "indefinitely." Three features make it a durable case study:

- **Shareholder-friendly capital allocation as the buy thesis.** Buffett singles out policies "much superior to those customarily practiced in the U.S.": each of the five has reduced its share count at attractive prices since Berkshire began buying; managements are "far less aggressive about their own compensation than is typical in the United States"; and each pays out only about ⅓ of earnings as dividends, retaining the rest to build businesses and repurchase shares. This is the same discipline Berkshire prizes in itself — reluctance to issue shares, price-dependent buybacks, modest payout. See [[share-repurchases]].
- **Currency hedging by matching liabilities, not forecasting.** "Neither Greg nor I believe we can forecast market prices of major currencies. We also don't believe we can hire anyone with this ability." Rather than bet on the yen, Berkshire financed most of the position with ¥1.3 trillion of yen-denominated bonds — likely "more yen-denominated debt outstanding than any other American company." The weak yen produced an $8B unrealized gain on the equities (a 61% dollar gain) and a separate $1.9B gain on the debt recognized over 2020–23 under GAAP. The structure neutralizes currency exposure through matched liabilities instead of through prediction — an application of the same humility that underlies [[circle-of-competence]].
- **Partnership optionality.** Buffett notes the holdings may "lead to opportunities for us to partner around the world" with five well-respected companies whose interests "are far more broad than ours," backed by Berkshire's standing offer of instantly available liquid resources.

## History

- **July 4, 2019** — Berkshire begins open-market purchases. Because of Berkshire's size, building the positions "takes a lot of patience and an extended period of 'friendly' prices. The process is like turning a battleship" — "an important disadvantage which we did not face in our early days."
- **2023** — Greg Abel and Buffett travel to Tokyo to meet the five managements; Berkshire increases its holdings in all five. Yearend ownership ~9% each; pledge not to exceed 9.9%. Cost ¥1.6T; market value ¥2.9T; unrealized dollar gain 61% ($8B). See [[berkshire-2023-letter]].
- **2024** — Berkshire continues buying. As it approached the self-imposed 10% ceiling, "the five companies agreed to moderately relax the ceiling," so "you will likely see Berkshire's ownership of all five increase somewhat." Aggregate cost $13.8B against $23.5B yearend market value. The yen-bond funding produced $2.3B of cumulative after-tax gains on the debt ($850M in 2024). Holdings are explicitly intended for "many decades" — "Greg and his eventual successors will be holding this Japanese position." See [[berkshire-2024-letter]].

## The relaxed ceiling and the yen-balanced math (2024)

The 2024 letter records two developments. First, the original 10% ownership cap was loosened by mutual agreement — as Berkshire neared the limit, the five companies "agreed to moderately relax the ceiling," signaling that they welcome Berkshire as a durable, supportive owner rather than treating the cap as a defensive line. Berkshire reciprocates: "committed to supporting their boards of directors" and expecting to "find other ways to work productively with the five companies in the future."

Second, the letter quantifies why the yen-balanced structure is attractive on its own terms, not merely as a hedge. The position is funded with fixed-rate yen-denominated borrowing ("no 'floaters'"), chosen because "Greg and I have no view on future foreign exchange rates and therefore seek a position approximating currency-neutrality" — the same can't-forecast-currencies humility as in 2023. The carry is favorable: for 2025, expected dividend income from the five is **~$812 million** against an expected yen interest cost of only **~$135 million**. GAAP requires recognizing yen-debt currency swings in earnings; cumulative after-tax gains on the borrowing reached $2.3B, of which $850M fell in 2024. ([[berkshire-2024-letter]])
