# Reading an Earnings Call: The Buffett–Munger Mental Models

## Question

What would Buffett and Munger listen for on a company's earnings call, and what questions would they want answered?

## The short answer

**They wouldn't be on the call.**

Buffett committed $500 million to PetroChina on the annual report alone: "I read it. And that's the only thing I ever did. I never contacted any management. I never got a brokerage report. I never asked for anybody's opinion." His reason applies to every call ever held ([berkshire-2008-meeting](../sources/berkshire-2008-meeting.md)):

> "What's the sense of talking to management? I mean, basically, if you talk to management of almost every company, they'll say they think their stock is a wonderful buy, and they'll give you all the good stuff and skip over things that — it just doesn't make any difference."

Munger: "we have lower due diligence expenses than anybody else in America — and we have had less trouble because we had less expense." Berkshire holds the same line as an issuer — no guidance, no investor calls, no budgets submitted to Omaha ([berkshire-2018-meeting](../sources/berkshire-2018-meeting.md), [berkshire-2005-meeting](../sources/berkshire-2005-meeting.md)).

So invert the question. **A call is worthless for facts and useful for judging character.** The facts are in the 10-K, the 10-Q and the footnotes — documents management can sanitize but must sign. The call shows you what no document can: how a CEO behaves unscripted, under questioning, in front of the people they most want to please. You listen to find out whether you can trust this person's future numbers.

Every model below follows from that.

## Eight mental models

### 1. Read the documents first; the call is never the input

Buffett's method is slow reading, not listening: "45 minutes or an hour on a report," and for six or eight companies in one industry, "six or eight hours, perhaps, and then their quarterlies" ([berkshire-1996-meeting](../sources/berkshire-1996-meeting.md)). Munger's caveat: "it takes a long time to read the annual report even if it's a comparatively simple business, because if you really are trying to understand it, it's not a bit easy." The alternative gave him nothing: "I don't think we've ever gotten an idea, you know, in 40 years from a Wall Street report."

**The rule:** value the business before the call, from its filings and its competitors' filings. Then the call can do only two things — change a number in your model, or change your confidence in the person quoting it. It is almost always the second. Know which one is happening.

### 2. Candor predicts behaviour; it is not a courtesy

From the owner's manual ([berkshire-1983-letter](../sources/berkshire-1983-letter.md)): "the CEO who misleads others in public may eventually mislead himself in private." The public story doesn't just describe management's thinking — it becomes management's thinking. A CEO who explains away a bad quarter to analysts starts believing the explanation, and the next capital-allocation decision runs on it.

The sharpest test is the post-mortem ([berkshire-2000-letter](../sources/berkshire-2000-letter.md)): "Charlie and I have almost never witnessed a candid post-mortem of a failed decision, particularly one involving an acquisition." The one named exception is The Washington Post Company, which "unfailingly and objectively reviews its acquisitions three years after they are made." Everywhere else, "triumphs are trumpeted, but dumb decisions either get no follow-up or are rationalized."

**Listen for:** whether bad news comes first-person, unprompted, and early — or has to be dragged out by the fourth analyst. Whether last year's failed initiative is ever mentioned again once it stopped working. See [buffett-management-criteria](buffett-management-criteria.md), [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

### 3. Vocabulary is the tell — "bad terminology is the enemy of good thinking"

Every custom earnings metric exists to remove a cost from the number you are valuing. The label is how it gets removed. Buffett ([berkshire-2001-letter](../sources/berkshire-2001-letter.md)): "When companies or investment professionals use terms such as 'EBITDA' and 'pro forma,' they want you to unthinkingly accept concepts that are dangerously flawed." His gloss: "In golf, my score is frequently below par on a pro forma basis: I have firm plans to 'restructure' my putting stroke and therefore only count the swings I take before reaching the green."

**Ignore the label. Ask one question: did this cost shareholders anything?** It did if cash left the business, or if the owner's share of the business shrank. Either way it belongs in earnings. If neither happened, the add-back is fair.

Two labels defeat that question, so treat both as noise:

- **"Non-recurring" answers the wrong question.** It tells you whether the cost repeats — useful for forecasting *next* year. It says nothing about whether shareholders paid for it *this* year. They did.
- **"Non-cash" does not mean "not a cost."** Either the cash already went out (an asset bought earlier, now wearing out), or shareholders paid in ownership instead of cash (stock comp).

The four items you'll hear excluded most:

| What management excludes | Did it cost shareholders? | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stock-based compensation | **Yes — the owner's share shrank.** No cash leaves, but you now own less of every future dollar. Buffett: "What else could it be – a gift from shareholders?" | [berkshire-2018-letter](../sources/berkshire-2018-letter.md) |
| Restructuring charges | **Yes — cash left**, whether or not it repeats. Berkshire "has gone down that road dozens of times, and our shareholders have always borne the costs." | [berkshire-2016-letter](../sources/berkshire-2016-letter.md), [berkshire-2018-letter](../sources/berkshire-2018-letter.md) |
| Depreciation (via EBITDA) | **Yes — the asset wears out** and future cash must replace it. In capital-intensive businesses the charge is too small, not too big: at BNSF it "falls far short of the capital outlays needed to merely keep the railroad running" | [berkshire-2015-letter](../sources/berkshire-2015-letter.md) |
| Acquisition amortization | **Usually no — the one fair add-back.** The cash was spent once at the acquisition, and the intangible often isn't decaying, so the charge deducts a cost nobody bears twice | [berkshire-2015-letter](../sources/berkshire-2015-letter.md) |

Compare the last two rows: depreciation and amortization are both non-cash, one is a real cost and the other usually isn't. That is why the label sorts nothing and the question does.

Lincoln, via Buffett: "If you call a dog's tail a leg, how many legs does it have? Four, because calling a tail a leg doesn't make it one. Abe would have felt lonely on Wall Street."

Note that the analysts on the call are usually *complicit*, not adversarial — "parroting the phony, compensation-ignoring 'earnings' figures fed them by managements... Maybe they fear losing 'access' to management" ([berkshire-2015-letter](../sources/berkshire-2015-letter.md)). A hostile question from the sell side is a rarer signal than it looks. Full treatment: [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md), [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md), [owner-earnings](../concepts/owner-earnings.md).

### 4. Smooth earnings are a warning, not an achievement

Real businesses are lumpy. Google's founders set the standard Buffett likes ([berkshire-2004-meeting](../sources/berkshire-2004-meeting.md)): "if numbers are lumpy or peculiar when they get to them, they're going to be lumpy or peculiar when they get to the public." Explaining the lumps is management's job. Sanding them off is "moving around numbers from one quarter to another or moving them for one year to another. And I have seen a lot of that. It's deceptive."

The pressure to sand is rarely fraud. It is deference: "Even if their compensation didn't depend on it, the managers would feel, we don't want to let Warren down on this. So we'll take an optimistic view of reserves... and then he won't look like a jerk in front of Wall Street." So Berkshire removes the expectation rather than policing the result — "our managers do not submit budgets into Omaha. I have no idea what we're going to earn next quarter" ([berkshire-2005-meeting](../sources/berkshire-2005-meeting.md)).

**Listen for:** a company that hits its number every quarter in a business the world does not deliver smoothly. Earnings that steady were estimated, not earned.

### 5. Follow the incentive, not the story

The compensation plan tells you what management will do. The narrative doesn't. Buffett boarding a NetJets plane ([berkshire-2005-meeting](../sources/berkshire-2005-meeting.md)): "the last thing in the world I want to tell the pilot is that I'm running late." Say it and a checklist gets skipped. "Companies do that time after time in their compensation plans."

This matters most where the number is a judgement call nobody can disprove. On insurance reserves: "we've got 45 billion of loss reserves. Well, who knows whether the right figure is 45 billion or 46 or 44? And if the desire is to report some given number in a given quarter, instead of saying 45 billion, you say 44-and-three-quarters billion." Buffett has watched reserves fall "very suspiciously" in the year or two before a stock offering ([berkshire-2013-meeting](../sources/berkshire-2013-meeting.md)).

**Before any call, read the proxy, not just the deck.** Ask what this quarter's result pays management, then check whether the metric they headline is the metric that pays them. See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

### 6. The call is how the institutional imperative reaches the CEO

The call is not a neutral disclosure venue. It is the quarterly channel that pushes a CEO to copy what everyone else is doing. Berkshire shrank National Indemnity's traditional book "by 80 percent or something of the sort when the business became less attractive." Buffett doubts a public company could have done it ([berkshire-2013-meeting](../sources/berkshire-2013-meeting.md)):

> "I'm not sure any manager of a public company that was answering to quarterly earnings calls and that sort of thing... could've really stood up to the kind of pressure that they would receive if they followed a similar policy."

And: "if you own a half of 1 percent of the company or less, and other people are doing things that Wall Street is applauding and you're not doing them, it could be very hard to resist."

The call costs time as well. Precision Castparts' Mark Donegan previously "had to spend some time explaining quarterly earnings to analysts... running a public company, you do waste your time on quite a bit of stuff that isn't productive" ([berkshire-2016-meeting](../sources/berkshire-2016-meeting.md)).

**Listen for:** whether management defends a shrinking business on economics, or apologizes for it. A CEO apologizing for rational contraction will eventually stop contracting. See [institutional-imperative](../concepts/institutional-imperative.md).

### 7. If you need the call, you are outside your circle

A good idea is obvious without extra precision. Buffett judged PetroChina worth about $100 billion, found it at about $35 billion, and stopped working ([berkshire-2008-meeting](../sources/berkshire-2008-meeting.md)): "we really like things that you don't have to carry out to three decimal places. If you have to carry it out to three decimal places, it's not a good idea." His test: "if somebody walked in the door here and they weighed somewhere between 300 and 350 pounds. I might not know how much they weigh, but I would know they were fat. That's all I'm looking for, is something that's financially fat."

The other side is Gen Re's 23,000 derivative contracts: "Charlie and I could've spent 24 hours a day, and had the help of 10 or 20 math Ph.Ds. and we still wouldn't have known what was going on. It cost us about $400 million to find out." Munger on those assets: "Good until reached for." The auditors had certified them ([berkshire-2013-meeting](../sources/berkshire-2013-meeting.md)).

**The diagnostic:** if your thesis falls apart when management's commentary turns out to be false, the margin of safety was coming from the commentary — which means there isn't one. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [margin-of-safety](../concepts/margin-of-safety.md), [model-risk](../concepts/model-risk.md).

### 8. Check the claim against the action

The most common claim on a call — "our stock is undervalued" — is the easiest to test. It is credible only if the company is buying, and self-refuting if it is selling ([berkshire-1996-meeting](../sources/berkshire-1996-meeting.md)):

> "Any management that is talking about selling their stock and they say it's very undervalued, either doesn't know what's good for their present shareholders or they may have their tongue in cheek."

A CEO who calls the stock cheap while issuing equity, paying for acquisitions in stock, or granting options at that price has told you the claim is decorative. See [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md), [stock-as-acquisition-currency](../concepts/stock-as-acquisition-currency.md), [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md).

## The question list

What Buffett would want answered. No analyst asks most of these, because they are uncomfortable.

**On the business's economics**
1. What capital spending do you need just to hold your competitive position — and how does that compare to the depreciation charge? ([owner-earnings](../concepts/owner-earnings.md))
2. Can you raise prices 10% next year without losing volume to a competitor? If not, what protects the margin?
3. Where has the moat widened this year, and where has it narrowed? ([moat](../concepts/moat.md))

**On capital allocation**
4. Where did last year's new capital go, and what did it return?
5. At today's price, is the stock a better use of a dollar than reinvestment? What price would change that answer?
6. What are you deliberately shrinking or exiting because the economics got worse?

**On accounting**
7. Which of your adjustments to GAAP earnings appeared in all five of the last five years? Why is a five-year recurring cost non-recurring?
8. For any long-tail estimate — reserves, allowances, percentage-of-completion, capitalized costs — what assumption changed this quarter, and what would the number be under last year's assumption?
9. Was any revenue or expense pulled forward or pushed out of this quarter?

**On character and incentives**
10. Name a decision from three years ago that didn't work, and what you changed because of it. *(The Washington Post test.)*
11. What does this quarter's result pay you, personally?
12. What would have to happen for a quarter to come in badly — and would you let it, or would you find the numbers?

## What they would deliberately ignore

- **Guidance.** A business is worth decades of cash flows. A forecast of the next 13 weeks is not an input to that, and Berkshire refuses to give one.
- **The result versus consensus.** A gap against analyst estimates is information about analysts.
- **Analyst "access" and sell-side commentary.** Forty years, zero ideas ([berkshire-1996-meeting](../sources/berkshire-1996-meeting.md)).
- **The prepared remarks.** Scripted, lawyered, and built from the filing you already read. The signal is in the unscripted Q&A — where models 2, 5, and 6 apply.
- **Macro forecasting on the call.** A CEO's view on rates or GDP is outside their circle as surely as it is outside yours.

## Where this framework has limits

**It only works on businesses simple enough that the filings settle the question.** That is a filter on what Buffett buys, not a reading technique. If your mandate forces you into complicated businesses, "just read the annual report" is advice you cannot take.

**It presumes permanent capital.** A controlling stake and no redemption risk are what let Buffett ignore the quarterly conversation: "We did not have outside influences that pushed us in directions that we didn't want to go" ([berkshire-2013-meeting](../sources/berkshire-2013-meeting.md)). An investor who must explain a bad quarter to their own investors faces the same pressure they are diagnosing in the CEO.

**Disclosure quality has fallen, which cuts against him.** Buffett ([berkshire-2013-meeting](../sources/berkshire-2013-meeting.md)): "I'm not sure I find present financial statements more useful or, in some cases, as useful as I found them 30 or 40 years ago." Munger on banks: "so many footnotes, and there's so much gobbledygook... they're not my grandfather's banks." If the documents carry less signal than they used to, the live unscripted exchange is worth *more* than this page concedes. Buffett never reconciles this. He resolves it by not buying those companies.

**There is no checklist, and he says so.** Asked how to spot number-manipulation, Buffett declined: "I can't give you a checklist of 40 items or something of the sort that you look for in the balance sheet or the income account or the footnotes." What he offers instead is pattern recognition: "you can spot certain people who you know are, one way or another, playing games with the numbers. They give themselves away." Munger's darker version — many of them "are not being deliberately fraudulent, because they're deluded. In other words, they believe what they're saying." That is where the 1983 warning lands: the CEO who misleads others in public eventually misleads himself in private, and by then he will pass any sincerity test you apply on a call.

## Open questions

- Does the *transcript* beat the live call? Buffett's method is reading, not listening, and a written record of unscripted answers is closer to his medium than the call is.
- Is there a class of business — regulated utilities, insurers — where the call is genuinely load-bearing because the filings can't settle reserve or rate-base adequacy?
- Berkshire's own meeting uses pre-submitted questions, via journalists and analysts. How does that square with the unscripted exchange this page rests on?
