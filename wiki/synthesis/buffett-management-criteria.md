# What Buffett Looks for in Management

## Question

What qualities does Buffett require of the managers who run businesses he owns or buys?

## The short answer

The four-point acquisition test ([acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), 1977) asks for businesses "operated by honest and competent people." The 2003 restatement sharpens this to "talented and honest managers." Both formulations are necessary and insufficient alone — ability without integrity produces a capable thief; integrity without ability produces a well-meaning failure.

## Six criteria, in priority order

**1. Integrity first — shareholders over self**
When the manager's interests diverge from owners', they side with the owners. Tom Murphy is the named exemplar: "In those rare instances in which Murph's personal interests diverged from those of shareholders, he unfailingly favored the owners." ([berkshire-1995-letter](../sources/berkshire-1995-letter.md)) This is non-negotiable and non-compensatable. Bad character in a manager is not diluted by good economics.

**2. Capital allocation skill**
After ten years of retaining 10% of net worth annually, a CEO has deployed more than 60% of all capital in the business — making this the most consequential skill. The test: does every retained dollar increase per-share intrinsic value? Not EPS, not size, not peer benchmarks. Most CEOs never develop this skill because they rose through marketing, engineering, or politics, not capital deployment. ([berkshire-1987-letter](../sources/berkshire-1987-letter.md)) See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md).

**3. Owner orientation**
Run the business as if you own 100%, it is your family's only asset, and you cannot sell it for a century. This is the operating-manager mandate from [berkshire-1998-letter](../sources/berkshire-1998-letter.md). Buffett does not install this orientation — he identifies it in managers who already have it. The six-point acquisition ad (1982) is explicit: "Management in place — Berkshire cannot supply it."

**4. Resistance to the institutional imperative**
The [institutional-imperative](../concepts/institutional-imperative.md) — corporate herd behavior — defeats most managers even when they are intelligent. Buffett pays a premium for the rare manager who resists:
- In insurance: holding pricing discipline while competitors write unprofitable business for volume. NICO maintained this across four consecutive CEOs. ([berkshire-2004-letter](../sources/berkshire-2004-letter.md))
- In banking: the Wells Fargo thesis (1990) was explicitly a bet on Reichardt/Hazen as managers who didn't follow lemming-like peer lending. ([berkshire-1990-letter](../sources/berkshire-1990-letter.md))
- In acquisitions: Murphy and Reichardt both attack costs "as vigorously when profits are at record levels as when they are under pressure."

**5. Cost discipline and scope restraint**
Pay able people well, but hate unnecessary headcount. "Let abilities, not egos, determine what they attempt." Empire-building — pursuing size as a yardstick — is the most common failure mode in the letters. The structural tell is a CEO who tracks their Fortune-500 sales rank but not their profitability rank. ([berkshire-1981-letter](../sources/berkshire-1981-letter.md))

**6. Candor — honesty that reaches self-honesty**
"The CEO who misleads others in public may eventually mislead himself in private." ([berkshire-1983-letter](../sources/berkshire-1983-letter.md)) The test: does the manager use the words "mistake" and "error" about their own decisions? Boardrooms where those words are forbidden produce self-deception that makes correction impossible. The 2024 letter adds the governance consequence: "If you start fooling your shareholders, you will soon believe your own baloney." ([berkshire-2024-letter](../sources/berkshire-2024-letter.md))

## Structural caveat: franchises tolerate what businesses cannot

[Franchise-vs-business](../concepts/franchise-vs-business.md) adds an asymmetry. A true franchise (Coke, Gillette, early newspapers) tolerates mediocre management for long stretches — the economics carry a bad manager. A commodity business requires a "have-to-be-smart-every-day" manager just to survive. The management bar therefore rises as the business moves from franchise toward commodity. Never apply franchise-management standards to a business, and never pay franchise multiples unless you've confirmed the franchise test is met.

## Aligned incentives as a diagnostic signal

Compensation structure is both a tool and a diagnostic. Buffett looks for managers who:
- Accept symmetric pay tied to results in their bailiwick — rewarded for value *added*, not inherited run-rate ([berkshire-1985-letter](../sources/berkshire-1985-letter.md), [berkshire-1994-letter](../sources/berkshire-1994-letter.md), [berkshire-2024-letter](../sources/berkshire-2024-letter.md))
- Buy stock in the open market rather than receiving it as grants — bearing downside alongside upside
- Face a capital charge on incremental investment, so "sending cash to Omaha" is structurally rewarded over deploying capital at sub-standard returns

"Managers eager to bet heavily on their abilities usually have plenty of ability to bet on." ([berkshire-1991-letter](../sources/berkshire-1991-letter.md)) See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).

## Open questions

- Does management quality compound (does a great manager get better over time within a Berkshire context)? The GEICO / Tony Nicely arc suggests yes, but the General Re cleanup suggests culture can deteriorate before acquisition.
- Where is the line between franchise tolerance and management dependency? The media reclassification shows franchises can cross the line — at what point does franchise erosion make the "tolerates mismanagement" test unreliable?
