# Size as Anchor on Returns

**The larger a capital base, the harder it becomes to earn high *percentage* returns — eventually the dollars required to move the needle outgrow the available opportunity set, and a high growth rate forges its own anchor.**

## Actionable takeaway

- **Discount any manager's historical track record in proportion to how much the asset base has grown since that record was earned.** Exceptional returns on millions are structurally unrepeatable on billions; the historical rate tells you about the old opportunity set, not the current one.
- **Treat fee-aligned managers who keep raising capital as structurally conflicted.** Fee income grows with AUM even after size erodes performance; the honest manager caps their own fund. See [investment-friction](investment-friction.md).
- **When evaluating a very large company or conglomerate at full scale, set realistic expectations:** "a bit better than average with materially less permanent-loss risk" may be the honest ceiling once no single opportunity can move the needle.
- **If you are a capital allocator yourself, monitor whether your minimum check size is outgrowing your opportunity set.** The quality threshold for new positions must rise as required deployment size rises — or you will fill positions with inferior opportunities simply to put capital to work.

## Definition

The larger the capital base, the harder it becomes to earn high *percentage* returns. In a finite world, an exceptional growth rate eventually outgrows the opportunity set that produced it — the absolute dollars required to "move the needle" grow until no available investment is both large enough and good enough to maintain the prior rate. Buffett's recurring metaphor: **"huge sums forge their own anchor."** The principle is mechanical and applies to any investor or manager; Berkshire is its most thoroughly documented case, but the same arithmetic constrains every fund that grows large.

## Why it matters

This is the single most important reason a great long-run track record is **not** a forecast. Three consequences follow:

1. **Past returns earned on small capital are not repeatable at scale.** The compounding that built a fortune on millions cannot be reproduced on billions, because the universe of adequately large, adequately cheap, adequately understandable opportunities shrinks as the dollars to be deployed grow.
2. **A growing manager's incentives diverge from the client's.** More assets mean more fees, so managers keep raising money even as size erodes the returns they can deliver. The investor must discount a record precisely when the asset base has ballooned.
3. **Honest expectation-setting requires anchoring future targets below historical performance.** Buffett's repeated practice of telling shareholders that future returns will be lower is the disciplined application of this principle. See [equity-return-expectations](equity-return-expectations.md).

## The mechanism: high growth rates self-destruct at scale

The 1989 letter gives the clearest statement of why the anchor is inescapable:

> In a finite world, high growth rates must self-destruct. If the base from which the growth is taking place is tiny, this law may not operate for a time. But when the base balloons, the party ends: A high growth rate eventually forges its own anchor. ([berkshire-1989-letter](../sources/berkshire-1989-letter.md))

Buffett illustrates with Carl Sagan's bacteria that double every fifteen minutes — whose descendants would, in two days, "weigh more than the sun" were it not that "some obstacle always impedes this kind of exponential growth." Compounding at a high rate is a transient state, not a permanent property; the base eventually grows large enough to exhaust its own fuel.

The same letter pairs this with a second drag: Berkshire's early outperformance enjoyed a "double-dip" (intrinsic-value gains *plus* the market re-rating those businesses upward), and that catch-up bonus had already been realized — leaving only a "single-dip" ahead. Size compounds an already-diminishing tailwind.

## Direction is certain; only the rate of decay is unknown

By 1992 the framing had hardened from "self-destruct" to "incontestable":

> Our second conclusion — that an increased capital base will act as an anchor on our relative performance — seems incontestable. The only open question is whether we can drag the anchor along at some tolerable, though slowed, pace. ([berkshire-1992-letter](../sources/berkshire-1992-letter.md))

The *direction* of the effect is no longer in question; only the *rate of decay* remains uncertain. The job is not to escape the anchor but to drag it as slowly as possible.

## The incentive trap: why managers don't self-correct

The 2016 letter generalizes the anchor to all large active managers and names the structural reason most keep raising capital:

> Finally, there are three connected realities that cause investing success to breed failure. First, a good record quickly attracts a torrent of money. Second, huge sums invariably act as an anchor on investment performance: What is easy with millions, struggles with billions (sob!). Third, most managers will nevertheless seek new money because of their personal equation — namely, the more funds they have under management, the more their fees. ([berkshire-2016-letter](../sources/berkshire-2016-letter.md))

Buffett dates his own recognition to January 1966, when — managing $44 million — he wrote his limited partners that "substantially greater size is more likely to harm future results than to help them" and closed the partnership to new entrants. The most honest managers cap their own size; the fee structure ensures most do not. This is why the size-anchor is also an [investment-friction](investment-friction.md) argument: the manager's growth, good for the manager, is bad for the client. See [passive-investing](passive-investing.md).

## At scale: what remains achievable

By 2023 the anchor's consequences were fully quantified. Berkshire's $561B GAAP net worth was by far the largest of any American business — roughly 6% of the aggregate net worth of the entire S&P 500. "Doubling our huge base is simply not possible"; only "a handful of companies in this country" can "truly move the needle," and abroad "there are essentially no candidates"; "we have no possibility of eye-popping performance." The realistic aspiration: **"a bit better than the average American corporation"** with "materially less risk of permanent loss of capital" — and "Anything beyond 'slightly better' … is wishful thinking." ([berkshire-2023-letter](../sources/berkshire-2023-letter.md))

This closes the arc from 1989: a high growth rate forged its anchor, and the anchor has now reduced achievable ambition to a modest premium over the average large company.

## Partial offsets — softening, not reversing

The 2010 letter names what *partially* offsets the anchor without pretending to cancel it:

1. **A cadre of owner-operators.** Independently wealthy CEOs who "are volunteers, not mercenaries" and cannot be lured away — a management quality that does not erode with size. See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).
2. **Capital-allocation flexibility across the whole economy.** Unlike most companies that can only reinvest "within the industry in which they have been operating" — "a 'universe' for capital allocation that is both tiny and quite inferior" — Berkshire can deploy its surplus wherever returns are best, public or private, any industry. See [capital-allocation](capital-allocation.md).

The 2009 letter noted a related consolation: Berkshire's "defense has been better than its offense" — a large, conservatively financed base outperforms more reliably in down markets than in strong ones. These structural advantages change the *pace* of decay, never its *direction* — exactly the 1992 framing of "drag the anchor along at some tolerable, though slowed, pace."
