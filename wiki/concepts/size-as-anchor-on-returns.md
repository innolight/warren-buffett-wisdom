# Size as Anchor on Returns

## Definition

The larger the capital base, the harder it becomes to earn high *percentage* returns. In a finite world, an exceptional growth rate eventually outgrows the opportunity set that produced it — the absolute dollars required to "move the needle" grow until no available investment is both large enough and good enough to maintain the prior rate. Buffett's recurring metaphor: **"huge sums forge their own anchor."** The principle is mechanical and applies to any investor or manager; Berkshire is its most thoroughly documented case, but the same arithmetic constrains every fund that grows large.

## Why it matters

This is the single most important reason a great long-run track record is **not** a forecast. Three consequences follow:

1. **Past returns earned on small capital are not repeatable at scale.** The compounding that built a fortune on millions cannot be reproduced on billions, because the universe of adequately large, adequately cheap, adequately understandable opportunities shrinks as the dollars to be deployed grow.
2. **A growing manager's incentives diverge from the client's.** More assets mean more fees, so managers keep raising money even as size erodes the returns they can deliver. The investor must discount a record precisely when the asset base has ballooned.
3. **Honest expectation-setting requires anchoring future targets below historical performance.** Buffett's repeated practice of telling shareholders that future returns will be lower is the disciplined application of this principle. See [[equity-return-expectations]].

## The mechanism: high growth rates self-destruct (1989)

The 1989 letter gives the clearest statement of why the anchor is inescapable:

> In a finite world, high growth rates must self-destruct. If the base from which the growth is taking place is tiny, this law may not operate for a time. But when the base balloons, the party ends: A high growth rate eventually forges its own anchor. ([[berkshire-1989-letter]])

Buffett illustrates with Carl Sagan's bacteria that double every fifteen minutes — whose descendants would, in two days, "weigh more than the sun" were it not that "some obstacle always impedes this kind of exponential growth." Compounding at a high rate is a transient state, not a permanent property; the base eventually grows large enough to exhaust its own fuel.

The 1989 letter pairs this with a related point: Berkshire's early outperformance enjoyed a "double-dip" (the businesses' intrinsic-value gains *plus* the market re-rating those businesses upward), and that catch-up bonus had already been realized — leaving only a "single-dip" ahead. The anchor of size compounds an already-diminishing tailwind.

## "Incontestable" drag on relative performance (1992)

By 1992 the framing is matter-of-fact:

> Our second conclusion — that an increased capital base will act as an anchor on our relative performance — seems incontestable. The only open question is whether we can drag the anchor along at some tolerable, though slowed, pace. ([[berkshire-1992-letter]])

The phrasing matters: the *direction* is certain (size lowers relative returns); only the *rate of decay* is uncertain. The job is not to escape the anchor but to drag it as slowly as possible.

## The general law for all managers (2016)

The 2016 letter generalizes the principle beyond Berkshire, embedding it in the argument for low-cost index funds:

> Finally, there are three connected realities that cause investing success to breed failure. First, a good record quickly attracts a torrent of money. Second, huge sums invariably act as an anchor on investment performance: What is easy with millions, struggles with billions (sob!). Third, most managers will nevertheless seek new money because of their personal equation — namely, the more funds they have under management, the more their fees. ([[berkshire-2016-letter]])

Buffett dates his own recognition to January 1966, when — managing $44 million — he wrote his limited partners that "substantially greater size is more likely to harm future results than to help them" and closed the partnership to new entrants. The lesson is that the most honest managers cap their own size; the structure of fees ensures most do not. This is why the size-anchor is also an [[investment-friction]] argument: the manager's growth, good for the manager, is bad for the client. See [[passive-investing]].

## Berkshire's own trajectory (2009 → 2023)

The letters track the anchor tightening in real time:

- **2009.** "Our performance advantage has shrunk dramatically as our size has grown, an unpleasant trend that is certain to continue… huge sums forge their own anchor and our future advantage, if any, will be a small fraction of our historical edge." The same letter notes a consolation: Berkshire's "defense has been better than our offense" — it has reliably *beaten* the index in down years even while lagging in strong ones, a profile that follows naturally from a large, conservatively financed base. ([[berkshire-2009-letter]])
- **2010.** "The job gets tougher as the numbers get larger. We will need both good performance from our current businesses and more major acquisitions." The "elephant gun has been reloaded" — but the need for ever-larger deals is itself the anchor in operation. ([[berkshire-2010-letter]])
- **2023.** The most concrete statement in the series. Berkshire's $561B GAAP net worth was "by far — the largest… recorded by any American business," roughly 6% of the aggregate net worth of the entire S&P 500. The consequences spelled out: "Doubling our huge base is simply not possible"; only "a handful of companies in this country" can "truly move the needle," and abroad "there are essentially no candidates"; "we have no possibility of eye-popping performance." The realistic aspiration is reduced to **"a bit better than the average American corporation"** with "materially less risk of permanent loss of capital" — and "Anything beyond 'slightly better' … is wishful thinking." ([[berkshire-2023-letter]])

The 2023 articulation closes the arc that began in 1989: a high growth rate forged its anchor, and the anchor has now reduced the achievable ambition to a modest premium over the average large company.

## Partial offsets — softening, not reversing

The 2010 letter is careful to name what *partially* offsets the anchor without pretending to cancel it:

1. **A cadre of owner-operators.** Independently wealthy CEOs who "are volunteers, not mercenaries" and cannot be lured away — a management quality that does not erode with size. See [[berkshire-conglomerate-model]].
2. **Capital-allocation flexibility across the whole economy.** Unlike most companies, which can only reinvest "within the industry in which they have been operating" — "a 'universe' for capital allocation that is both tiny and quite inferior" — Berkshire can deploy its surplus wherever returns are best, public or private, any industry. See [[capital-allocation]].

These advantages let Berkshire drag the anchor more slowly than a pure fund would. They do not lift it. Permanent capital, operator quality, and a wide opportunity set change the *pace* of decay, never its *direction* — exactly the framing of the 1992 "drag the anchor along" formulation.
