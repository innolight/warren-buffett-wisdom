# Valuation Framework

## Definition

All investment decisions reduce to a single question that Buffett, following Aesop, frames as: is a bird in the hand worth two in the bush? To answer it, you must answer only three questions:

1. **How certain are you that there are indeed birds in the bush?**
2. **When will they emerge, and how many will there be?**
3. **What is the risk-free interest rate?**

And, of course, don't literally think birds. Think dollars.

The three questions map directly onto the components of any discounted-cash-flow analysis: (1) certainty of future cash flows, (2) their magnitude and timing, and (3) the discount rate. John Burr Williams provided the formal apparatus in *The Theory of Investment Value* (1938): the value of any security is the present value of all its future cash distributions, discounted at the appropriate interest rate. Aesop, twenty-six centuries earlier, had the intuition.

## Why it matters

**Universal applicability.** The framework applies unchanged across every asset class: farms, oil royalties, bonds, stocks, lottery tickets, manufacturing plants. There is no special valuation logic for equities vs. real assets vs. financial instruments. The three questions always govern.

**Unchanged by technology.** Neither the steam engine, the harnessing of electricity, nor the creation of the automobile changed the formula one iota. The Internet does not change it either. A technological revolution may alter *the answers* to the three questions for specific businesses — higher growth, greater certainty, faster emergence — but it does not alter the questions themselves. Claiming that a new technology makes the formula obsolete is the recurring error that produces every speculative bubble.

**Growth and value are not opposing philosophies.** Growth is simply one variable in the equation — a higher expected number of future birds. It can be a large positive (high-return business with substantial reinvestment opportunities) or a negative (low-return business that must consume capital to grow). The "growth vs. value" framing commonly used by investment managers reflects ignorance of this relationship, not sophistication about it. Both camps are answering the same three questions; the disagreement, when real, is about the inputs, not the method.

**Speculation is a different game, not a competing method.** Speculation — where the focus is on what the next buyer will pay rather than what the asset will produce — is neither illegal nor immoral. But it is a different activity. The valuation framework governs investing; it has nothing to say about what the next fellow will bid.

## How it's discussed in sources

- [[berkshire-1992-letter]] — **the Williams formula foundation.** The essay that explicitly dissolves the growth/value dichotomy: "Growth is always a component in the calculation of value, constituting a variable whose importance can range from negligible to enormous and whose impact can be negative as well as positive." Restates the definition of investing as seeking value at least sufficient to justify price paid; labels the opposite (consciously paying more than calculated value, hoping to sell higher) as speculation. The Williams citation establishes the theoretical lineage.

- [[berkshire-2000-letter]] — **the canonical Aesop formulation.** The 2000 letter is where Buffett reduces the framework to its three-question irreducible form and names Aesop as the source. The dot-com bubble is the foil: promoters were selling "birdless bushes" — confident assertions about cash flow timing and magnitude that the underlying businesses could not support. The same letter introduces Buffett's tongue-in-cheek name for the practical opportunity the framework creates: the **Inefficient Bush Theory (IBT)** — finding situations where even conservative answers to all three questions reveal that the current price is well below value. The IBT is not a competing theory; it is the framework applied in a market where Mr. Market occasionally misprices severely.

## Notable quotes

> The oracle was Aesop and his enduring, though somewhat incomplete, investment insight was "a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush." To flesh out this principle, you must answer only three questions. How certain are you that there are indeed birds in the bush? When will they emerge and how many will there be? What is the risk-free interest rate (which we consider to be the yield on long-term U.S. bonds)? — [[berkshire-2000-letter]]

> And, of course, don't literally think birds. Think dollars. — [[berkshire-2000-letter]]

> Aesop's investment axiom, thus expanded and converted into dollars, is immutable. It applies to outlays for farms, oil royalties, bonds, stocks, lottery tickets, and manufacturing plants. And neither the advent of the steam engine, the harnessing of electricity nor the creation of the automobile changed the formula one iota — nor will the Internet. — [[berkshire-2000-letter]]

> Market commentators and investment managers who glibly refer to "growth" and "value" styles as contrasting approaches to investment are displaying their ignorance, not their sophistication. Growth is simply a component — usually a plus, sometimes a minus — in the value equation. — [[berkshire-2000-letter]]

> In our opinion, the two approaches are joined at the hip: Growth is always a component in the calculation of value, constituting a variable whose importance can range from negligible to enormous and whose impact can be negative as well as positive. — [[berkshire-1992-letter]]

> Growth benefits investors only when the business in point can invest at incremental returns that are enticing — in other words, only when each dollar used to finance the growth creates over a dollar of long-term market value. In the case of a low-return business requiring incremental funds, growth hurts the investor. — [[berkshire-1992-letter]]
