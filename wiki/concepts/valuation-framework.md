# Valuation Framework

**All investment decisions reduce to three questions: how certain are the future cash flows, when do they arrive and how large, and what is the risk-free rate to discount them by.**

## Actionable takeaway

- **Answer the three questions explicitly before buying anything.** Do not substitute enthusiasm, growth narrative, or peer behavior for answers to (1) certainty of future cash flows, (2) their magnitude and timing, (3) the appropriate discount rate. Inability to answer confidently is a signal to stay out — not to guess harder.
- **Reject the growth-vs-value frame.** Growth is one variable inside question 2. When a business earns above its cost of capital, growth helps; when it earns below, growth destroys value. The only investment question is: does the price leave room for the expected cash flows to justify buying?
- **Track the risk-free rate as the denominator of all asset prices.** When the rate falls, the present value of every future cash flow rises — across stocks, farms, apartments, and oil wells simultaneously. Structurally low rates make attractive prices scarce everywhere at once.
- **Price paid above what the three questions can support is speculation, not investment.** Hoping the next buyer will pay more is a different activity with different risks. Know which game you are playing.

## Definition

The framework in Aesop's formulation: is a bird in the hand worth two in the bush? To answer, you need only three questions:

1. **How certain are you that there are indeed birds in the bush?**
2. **When will they emerge, and how many will there be?**
3. **What is the risk-free interest rate?**

Think dollars, not birds. John Burr Williams formalized the same intuition in *The Theory of Investment Value* (1938): value is the present value of all future cash distributions, discounted at the appropriate rate. Aesop had the essence twenty-six centuries earlier.

## Why it matters

**Universal applicability.** The formula applies unchanged to farms, oil royalties, bonds, stocks, lottery tickets, and manufacturing plants. No asset class requires its own special valuation logic; the three questions always govern.

**Unchanged by technology.** The steam engine, electricity, and the automobile did not alter the formula. Neither does the Internet. A technological revolution may change *the answers* — higher growth, lower certainty, faster emergence — but not the questions themselves. Claiming otherwise is the recurring error behind every speculative bubble.

**Speculation is a different game.** Paying more than calculated value in the hope of selling higher is neither illegal nor immoral — but it is speculation, not investment. The framework is silent on what the next buyer will pay.

## Growth amplifies returns above the cost of capital — and destroys value below it

Growth enters the three-question framework as the quantity and timing of future cash flows (question 2), but it adds value only when a business can redeploy capital at returns above the cost of that capital. The arithmetic is mechanical:

- **High return + growth = compounding.** Retained earnings redeployed at high rates compound intrinsic value at those same rates; more growth accelerates the compounding.
- **Low return + growth = destruction.** "Growth can destroy value if it requires cash inputs in the early years that exceed the discounted value of the cash that those assets will generate in later years." ([berkshire-2000-letter](../sources/berkshire-2000-letter.md))

The decisive quantity is the **return on incremental invested capital relative to its cost**, not the growth rate in isolation. This is why "growth investing" and "value investing" are not opposing styles — both are answering the same three questions, and growth is one input to question 2. See [great-good-gruesome](great-good-gruesome.md) for the operational taxonomy this produces.

## The bubble failure mode: confident answers without evidence

Every speculative bubble is a collective refusal to answer the three questions rigorously. The dot-com bubble's signature was "merchandising birdless bushes" — confident assertions about the timing and magnitude of cash flows from businesses that had never produced them. ([berkshire-2000-letter](../sources/berkshire-2000-letter.md)) "Nothing sedates rationality like large doses of effortless money." The formula is always right; the question is only whether the inputs fed into it are honest.

## How it's discussed in sources

- [berkshire-1992-letter](../sources/berkshire-1992-letter.md) — formally dissolves the growth/value dichotomy using the Williams DCF equation; shows growth as a variable that is sometimes negative; labels paying above calculated value as speculation
- [berkshire-2000-letter](../sources/berkshire-2000-letter.md) — the canonical three-question Aesop formulation; the dot-com bubble as the failure-mode worked case; names the corollary the **Inefficient Bush Theory**: conservative answers to all three questions sometimes reveal a price well below value
- [berkshire-2021-letter](../sources/berkshire-2021-letter.md) — restates question 3 in universal form: low long-term rates raise the present value of every productive asset simultaneously, making attractive prices structurally scarce across all classes at once
