# Super-cat Insurance

## Definition

"Super-cat" — short for super-catastrophe — refers to reinsurance and retrocession coverage purchased by primary insurers (and by reinsurers themselves) to protect against losses from singular mega-catastrophes: large hurricanes, earthquakes, windstorms, similar events. A super-cat policy typically activates only when:

1. The buyer's own losses from a single event exceed a high retention threshold (e.g., $10 million); and
2. Industry-wide insured losses from that event exceed some minimum (often $3-5 billion).

In most cases the policy covers only a specific geographic area or peril, and many cover only a "second event" or later — meaning the buyer keeps the first super-cat for itself.

## Why it became a Berkshire business

Three structural advantages made super-cat reinsurance an unusually good fit for Berkshire:

1. **Premier capital strength.** A super-cat buyer is paying for certainty that the seller will be solvent precisely when its own house is on fire. "The certainty that Berkshire will be both solvent and liquid after a catastrophe of unthinkable proportions is a major competitive advantage for us." ([[berkshire-1994-letter]]) Most competitors must lay off large positions to other reinsurers; the buyer cannot trust that the laid-off chain will pay.

2. **Indifference to single-year results.** "We simply don't care what earnings we report quarterly, or even annually, just as long as the decisions leading to those earnings (or losses) were reached intelligently." ([[berkshire-1989-letter]]) Most insurers cannot tolerate the lumpiness of super-cat — a single quarter loss could embarrass management. Berkshire can.

3. **Willingness to retain rather than lay off.** "Berkshire's policy, conversely, is to retain the business we write rather than lay it off. When rates carry an expectation of profit, we want to assume as much risk as is prudent. And in our case, that's a lot." ([[berkshire-1989-letter]])

The result: Berkshire writes super-cat policies in sizes no one else will quote (up to $500M same-day, $400M policies written in 1994), and prices them with reference to its own balance sheet rather than a reinsurance laid-off chain.

## The lumpy 15% vs. smooth 12%

Buffett's recurring framing — "we prefer a lumpy 15% to a smooth 12%" ([[berkshire-1990-letter]], [[berkshire-1995-letter]]) — is the explicit articulation of the structural advantage. Most managers, exposed to shareholder pressure for earnings smoothness, suboptimize to avoid quarterly volatility. Berkshire's owner-aligned structure and Buffett's communication discipline permit accepting volatility in exchange for higher long-term return. Super-cat is the canonical embodiment.

## The volatility profile

In a plain-vanilla year: $100 million of super-cat premiums in, no qualifying event, ~$100 million underwriting profit. In a major-event year: same premiums in, perhaps $200-600 million paid out. The 1990 letter's blunt framing:

> Our yearly combined ratio on this business will almost never fall in the industry range of 100 - 120, but will instead be close to either zero or 300%.

Pricing assumes the long-run loss ratio is approximately 90% — Buffett targets a 10% underwriting margin on average ([[berkshire-1991-letter]]). The honest disclosure: "there is no way for us — or anyone else — to calculate the true odds on super-cat coverages. In fact, it will take decades for us to find out whether our underwriting judgment has been sound." ([[berkshire-1993-letter]])

## The 1989 origin

Berkshire's super-cat business began in earnest after Hurricane Hugo and the California earthquake of 1989 left the reinsurance market temporarily short of capacity. "Just as instantly, Berkshire Hathaway offered to write up to $250 million of catastrophe coverage, advertising that proposition in trade publications. Though we did not write all the business we sought, we did in a busy ten days book a substantial amount." ([[berkshire-1989-letter]])

The accelerator was [[ajit-jain]] (first named in the 1989 letter), who joined Berkshire's reinsurance operation in 1986 and became the architect of the super-cat franchise. By 1994: "Ajit Jain, our super-cat manager, whose underwriting skills are the finest." By 1995: "the guiding genius of our super-cat business."

## The 1991 typhoon — and what counted as the year's biggest cat

The 1991 letter offers a useful reframing for U.S.-centric thinking: the biggest insured catastrophe of the year was neither the Oakland fire nor Hurricane Bob but a September typhoon in Japan, with $4-5 billion of insured loss. Super-cat is a global business, and the historical-rate basis for U.S. perils is inadequate for thinking about the global book.

## Three lucky years (1993, 1994, 1995)

By the 1995 letter, super-cat had had three straight profitable years — and Buffett's emphasis was on tempering enthusiasm:

> We know that the results of years like the past three will be at least partially offset by some truly terrible year in the future. We just hope that "partially" turns out to be the proper adverb. ([[berkshire-1995-letter]])

The 1992 letter's accounting refinement: Berkshire defers super-cat premium recognition until the policy expires or a loss occurs (rather than pro-rating revenue across the term). Reason: catastrophes cluster in the back half of the year, and most second-event policies cannot generate losses early in their term. The result: significant super-cat *profits* always land in Q4; large losses can land in any quarter.

## What super-cat is not

The 1989 letter is careful to distinguish super-cat from broader "innocent" reinsurance writing — the soft-market reinsurance Berkshire itself wrote unprofitably in the 1970s, learning the long-tail lesson that produces decades of late-arriving claims. Super-cat is mechanically different: short tail, defined trigger, explicit reference to a balance sheet rather than to a laid-off chain.

The recurring caution: "A bad reinsurance contract is like hell: easy to enter and impossible to exit." ([[berkshire-1995-letter]])
