# Super-cat Insurance

Super-catastrophe reinsurance covers singular mega-catastrophes — major hurricanes, earthquakes, windstorms — and structurally rewards only the financially-fortified writer willing to retain risk and tolerate binary results.

## Actionable takeaway

- When evaluating a catastrophe reinsurer, ask whether it **retains** risk or lays it off through a retrocession chain. A chain fails precisely when the mega-event triggers; the buyer is paying for certainty of payment in the worst case, not the average case.
- Accept binary combined ratios as a feature: near-zero in calm years, up to 300% in major-event years. The long-run edge exists only for a writer who will not be forced to shrink at the trough.
- After a major catastrophe, look for evidence the writer **expanded capacity at hardened rates** — that is the franchise proof. An unblemished loss record in a quiet stretch proves nothing; it may reflect favorable draws, not skill.

## Definition

"Super-cat" covers reinsurance purchased by primary insurers (and by reinsurers themselves) to protect against losses from singular mega-catastrophes. A policy typically activates only when the buyer's own losses from a single event exceed a high retention threshold and industry-wide insured losses exceed a minimum (often $3–5 billion). Most policies are geographically specific; many cover only a "second event," leaving the first super-cat loss with the buyer.

## Why it matters

Super-cat is the clearest demonstration that reported-result volatility and long-run capital risk are different things. Most insurers cannot tolerate the earnings lumpiness super-cat produces — a single quarter can swing from large profit to large loss. That institutional intolerance creates a structural market inefficiency: after a major catastrophe, competitors exit or curtail, and the surviving writer commands premium rates precisely when capacity is shortest.

"We prefer a lumpy 15% return to a smooth 12%." ([berkshire-1990-letter](../sources/berkshire-1990-letter.md), [berkshire-1995-letter](../sources/berkshire-1995-letter.md)) Most managers suboptimize to avoid quarterly volatility; an owner-aligned structure that tolerates lumpiness turns the competitor's weakness into a durable moat. Super-cat is the canonical application of [volatility-as-opportunity](volatility-as-opportunity.md) to the insurance business. The honest corollary: true odds cannot be calculated — returns come from superior balance-sheet strength and structural patience, not actuarial models. ([berkshire-1993-letter](../sources/berkshire-1993-letter.md))

## Who can write this business

Three requirements that most writers cannot simultaneously satisfy:

**Capital strength.** A super-cat buyer pays for certainty that the seller will be solvent when its own house is on fire. "The certainty that Berkshire will be both solvent and liquid after a catastrophe of unthinkable proportions is a major competitive advantage for us." ([berkshire-1994-letter](../sources/berkshire-1994-letter.md)) With capital roughly 10–20× that of most competitors, Berkshire could write a $400 million earthquake policy same-day without a retrocession chain behind it.

**Willingness to retain.** "Berkshire's policy, conversely, is to retain the business we write rather than lay it off. When rates carry an expectation of profit, we want to assume as much risk as is prudent. And in our case, that's a lot." ([berkshire-1989-letter](../sources/berkshire-1989-letter.md)) Retaining means pricing with reference to your own balance sheet rather than a chain's, and writing in sizes no competitor will quote.

**Specialized judgment.** [Ajit Jain](../people/ajit-jain.md) joined Berkshire's reinsurance operation in 1986 and became the franchise's architect. Super-cat underwriting is not algorithmic; it requires case-by-case assessment of event probability, geographic scope, and correlation across seemingly unrelated exposures. By 1995 Buffett described him as "the guiding genius of our super-cat business." For the three underwriting principles Jain has never violated, see [underwriting-discipline](underwriting-discipline.md).

## The volatility profile: binary, not bell-curve

In a plain-vanilla year: roughly $100 million of premiums collected, no qualifying event, approximately $100 million underwriting profit. In a major-event year: same premiums, $200–600 million paid out. "Our yearly combined ratio on this business will almost never fall in the industry range of 100–120, but will instead be close to either zero or 300%." ([berkshire-1990-letter](../sources/berkshire-1990-letter.md))

Pricing targets a long-run underwriting margin of approximately 10%, requiring that infrequent catastrophe years do not overwhelm accumulated premiums from calm years. Hurricane Andrew (1992) cost roughly $125 million — approximately one full year of super-cat premium income — confirming the expected range rather than invalidating the economics. ([berkshire-1992-letter](../sources/berkshire-1992-letter.md))

## The hard-market advantage: catastrophes clear the field

After Hurricane Hugo and the California earthquake in 1989 depleted market capacity, Berkshire offered up to $250 million of coverage in trade publications and booked a substantial position in ten days. After 9/11, Jain's operation wrote a jumbo book of coverages that no shell-shocked competitor could credibly match. The franchise built through pricing discipline proves most valuable precisely when the rest of the market is frozen.

Three consecutive profitable years (1993–1995) prompted an honest caution: "We know that the results of years like the past three will be at least partially offset by some truly terrible year in the future." ([berkshire-1995-letter](../sources/berkshire-1995-letter.md)) Luck and skill look identical during a quiet run.

## The reinsurance paradox: permanent capital dissolves the market penalty

A reinsurer is paid to absorb the earnings volatility primary insurers want to shed. The paradox: a publicly-traded reinsurer is then penalized for exactly that volatility — in credit ratings and P/E multiples — forcing value-destroying retrocessions or rejection of sound but lumpy risks. ([berkshire-1998-letter](../sources/berkshire-1998-letter.md))

Berkshire dissolves the paradox by structure, not skill. Permanent capital and a shareholder base that explicitly accepts lumpy results remove the market's tax on variance. Most public insurers cannot credibly sustain the "lumpy 15%" preference under quarterly earnings pressure — which is precisely why the niche persists.

## What super-cat is not

The 1989 letter distinguishes super-cat from "innocent" reinsurance — the soft-market business Berkshire itself wrote unprofitably in the 1970s, which produced decades of late-arriving claims. Super-cat is mechanically different: short tail, defined trigger, explicit balance-sheet backing rather than a laid-off chain. "A bad reinsurance contract is like hell: easy to enter and impossible to exit." ([berkshire-1995-letter](../sources/berkshire-1995-letter.md)) That warning applies to inadequately priced long-tail reinsurance — the broader category super-cat is specifically designed to avoid.
