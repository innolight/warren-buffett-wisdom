# Super-cat Insurance

## Definition

"Super-cat" — short for super-catastrophe — refers to reinsurance and retrocession coverage purchased by primary insurers (and by reinsurers themselves) to protect against losses from singular mega-catastrophes: large hurricanes, earthquakes, windstorms, and similar events. A super-cat policy typically activates only when:

1. The buyer's own losses from a single event exceed a high retention threshold (e.g., $10 million); and
2. Industry-wide insured losses from that event exceed some minimum (often $3-5 billion).

In most cases the policy covers only a specific geographic area or peril, and many cover only a "second event" or later — meaning the buyer keeps the first super-cat loss for itself. A key definitional feature is the 72-hour rule: losses from a single "event" are aggregated over a 72-hour window, which prevents disputes about whether losses from a multi-day storm or earthquake sequence count as one event or many.

## Why it matters

Super-cat is the clearest demonstration that volatility of reported results and risk to long-term capital are different things. Most insurance competitors cannot tolerate the earnings lumpiness super-cat produces — a single quarter can swing from $100 million profit to $300 million loss. That institutional intolerance creates a structural market inefficiency: after a major catastrophe, competitors exit or curtail, and the surviving writer can charge premium rates precisely when the supply of coverage is shortest.

Buffett's recurring framing — "we prefer a lumpy 15% to a smooth 12%" ([berkshire-1990-letter](../sources/berkshire-1990-letter.md), [berkshire-1995-letter](../sources/berkshire-1995-letter.md)) — is the explicit articulation of this edge. Most managers, exposed to shareholder pressure for earnings smoothness, suboptimize to avoid quarterly volatility. An owner-aligned structure that tolerates lumpiness turns the competitor's weakness into a durable moat. Super-cat is the canonical embodiment of [volatility-as-opportunity](volatility-as-opportunity.md) applied to the insurance business.

The honest disclosure on this market: "there is no way for us — or anyone else — to calculate the true odds on super-cat coverages. In fact, it will take decades for us to find out whether our underwriting judgment has been sound." ([berkshire-1993-letter](../sources/berkshire-1993-letter.md)) The returns come not from superior actuarial models but from superior balance-sheet strength and structural patience.

## The structural advantage

Three characteristics made super-cat an unusually good fit for Berkshire:

**Capital strength.** A super-cat buyer is paying for certainty that the seller will be solvent precisely when its own house is on fire. "The certainty that Berkshire will be both solvent and liquid after a catastrophe of unthinkable proportions is a major competitive advantage for us." ([berkshire-1994-letter](../sources/berkshire-1994-letter.md)) Most competitors must lay off large positions to other reinsurers; the buyer cannot be certain the laid-off chain will pay when the mega-event arrives. Berkshire can write a $400 million earthquake policy same-day because its balance sheet makes the promise credible without a laid-off chain behind it.

**Willingness to retain.** "Berkshire's policy, conversely, is to retain the business we write rather than lay it off. When rates carry an expectation of profit, we want to assume as much risk as is prudent. And in our case, that's a lot." ([berkshire-1989-letter](../sources/berkshire-1989-letter.md)) The result: Berkshire prices policies with reference to its own balance sheet rather than a reinsurance chain, and writes in sizes no one else will quote.

**Ajit Jain's judgment.** [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md) joined Berkshire's reinsurance operation in 1986 and became the architect of the franchise. The underwriting in super-cat is not algorithmic; it requires case-by-case assessment of event probability, geographic scope, and correlation with other exposures. By 1995 Buffett described him as "the guiding genius of our super-cat business." The combination of Jain's judgment and Berkshire's capital is what enables same-day pricing on $500 million risks.

## The volatility profile

In a plain-vanilla year: roughly $100 million of super-cat premiums in, no qualifying event, approximately $100 million underwriting profit. In a major-event year: same premiums in, perhaps $200–600 million paid out. The 1990 letter's blunt framing:

> Our yearly combined ratio on this business will almost never fall in the industry range of 100–120, but will instead be close to either zero or 300%.

Pricing assumes the long-run loss ratio is approximately 90% — Buffett targets a 10% underwriting margin on average ([berkshire-1991-letter](../sources/berkshire-1991-letter.md)). The volatility profile, illustrated by actual events: after Hurricane Hugo and the California earthquake in 1989 left the reinsurance market short of capacity, Berkshire immediately offered up to $250 million of coverage in trade publications and booked a substantial position in ten days. After Hurricane Andrew in 1992, the loss was roughly $125 million — approximately matching a full year of premium, and an excellent illustration of the zero-or-300% range. Three subsequent profitable years (1993, 1994, 1995) prompted this honest tempering: "We know that the results of years like the past three will be at least partially offset by some truly terrible year in the future. We just hope that 'partially' turns out to be the proper adverb." ([berkshire-1995-letter](../sources/berkshire-1995-letter.md))

A useful reframing for U.S.-centric thinking: the biggest insured catastrophe of 1991 was neither the Oakland fire nor Hurricane Bob but a September typhoon in Japan, with $4–5 billion of insured loss. Super-cat is a global business, and the historical-rate basis for U.S. perils is inadequate for thinking about a global book.

An accounting refinement worth noting: Berkshire defers super-cat premium recognition until the policy expires or a loss occurs, rather than pro-rating revenue across the term. The rationale — catastrophes cluster in the back half of the year, and most second-event policies cannot generate losses early in their term — means significant super-cat profits always land in Q4, while large losses can land in any quarter. ([berkshire-1992-letter](../sources/berkshire-1992-letter.md))

## What super-cat is not

The 1989 letter distinguishes super-cat from broader "innocent" reinsurance — the soft-market reinsurance Berkshire itself wrote unprofitably in the 1970s, which produced decades of late-arriving claims. Super-cat is mechanically different: short tail, defined trigger, explicit reference to a balance sheet rather than a laid-off chain.

"A bad reinsurance contract is like hell: easy to enter and impossible to exit." ([berkshire-1995-letter](../sources/berkshire-1995-letter.md)) The warning applies to the broader reinsurance market, not to well-priced super-cat — but it explains why pricing discipline is the entire game.

## The reinsurance paradox and Berkshire's structural advantage (1998)

The 1998 letter articulates a structural insight about the super-cat and reinsurance business that explains why Berkshire's ownership matters: "A reinsurer gets paid for absorbing the volatility that the client insurer wants to shed. Yet a publicly-held reinsurer is itself penalized by markets for that same volatility, forcing it to lay off business via retrocessions or reject good business." ([berkshire-1998-letter](../sources/berkshire-1998-letter.md))

Berkshire's permanent capital and "Fort Knox" balance sheet dissolve this paradox. Berkshire can absorb volatility on behalf of clients without being penalized for it by shareholders—because Berkshire's shareholder base is explicitly long-term and accepts lumpy results. The preference for "a lumpy 15% return to a smooth 12%" is the cultural prerequisite that makes the franchise viable. Most public insurers cannot credibly make that preference stick.

## The 9/11 test (2001)

September 11, 2001 was the largest single loss event in insurance history. Berkshire took approximately $2.275B of total insurance losses—substantial, but the company was prepared for this class of event: it had sized its terrorism exposure, its capital was more than adequate, and its direct super-cat operation kept its pricing discipline intact.

The contrast with General Re was stark. [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md)'s operation had applied the three underwriting principles throughout: accepting only properly evaluable risks, controlling aggregation, and maintaining pricing integrity. "Ajit has never violated any of the three." ([berkshire-2001-letter](../sources/berkshire-2001-letter.md)) General Re, meanwhile, had accumulated nuclear/chemical/biological (NCB) aggregation that "would have been fatal had, say, terrorists detonated several large-scale nuclear bombs." The failure was exposure pricing—General Re (and most of the industry) priced terrorism risk by extrapolating past experience rather than reasoning about current exposure.

The post-9/11 period also illustrated the pricing-improvement cycle that makes disciplined super-cat writing rewarding over time. Coverage became scarce; premiums rose sharply. Berkshire's financial strength allowed Ajit to write an unusual book of post-9/11 jumbo risks that no competitor could match: a South American refinery, global airline terrorism risk, a North Sea oil platform, the Sears Tower, the FIFA World Cup, and the 2002 Winter Olympics. The franchise that had been built through decades of pricing discipline was most valuable precisely when the rest of the market was shell-shocked and capacity-constrained.

The deeper lesson: "just as is the case in investing, insurers produce outstanding long-term results primarily by avoiding dumb decisions, rather than by making brilliant ones." ([berkshire-2001-letter](../sources/berkshire-2001-letter.md)) The dumb decision—pricing terrorism risk out of historical experience rather than current exposure—was made industry-wide. Berkshire made it too, through General Re. Ajit's operation did not.

## The franchise at maturity (2002-2003)

By 2002, Ajit's reinsurance operation held $13.4B of float—"more than all but a handful of insurers have ever built up"—from a 1986 standing start with a workforce of 20. ([berkshire-2002-letter](../sources/berkshire-2002-letter.md)) By 2003, with a staff of 23, Ajit ran "the largest reinsurance operation in the world" and could still retain a $1B-prize policy entirely (the PepsiCo promotion, with $250M of present-value exposure). ([berkshire-2003-letter](../sources/berkshire-2003-letter.md))

The economics had proven the thesis. Over 18 of the 37 years Berkshire had been in the insurance business, the cost of float was negative—meaning Berkshire was paid to hold the money. Ajit's segment had achieved this in nearly every year, including years when General Re was producing catastrophic losses. The structural advantage—permanent capital allowing retention rather than retrocession—continued to be expressed most cleanly in the super-cat and large-risk reinsurance franchise.

The enduring lesson: the profitability of super-cat and large-risk reinsurance over time depends entirely on disciplined pricing and adequate reserves. The business looks easy when the loss record is good; it destroys value when the same confidence is carried into the next cycle without updating for changed exposures.
