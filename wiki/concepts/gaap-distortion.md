# GAAP Distortion

**Systematic gaps between a company's reported figures and its economic reality that arise from the accounting rules themselves — rule-mandated, not management-chosen.**

## Actionable takeaway

- For any company with a large equity portfolio, discard GAAP net income as the primary analytical signal; use **operating earnings** (which strips both realized and unrealized investment gains/losses) instead.
- When GAAP earnings swing wildly year to year, ask first: is this rule-mandated portfolio marking or genuine operating change? A "crazy 1,900% increase in GAAP earnings" with operating results "little changed" is the diagnostic, not the exception.
- When evaluating non-cash charges, distinguish direction: **acquisition amortization** inflates stated cost above economic reality (a legitimate add-back where the underlying intangible retains value); **depreciation** in capital-intensive businesses *understates* true maintenance cost and must never be added back without checking actual capex.
- Treat a large goodwill write-down as a **valuation-error signal**, not evidence of business deterioration. The question it answers is "did management overpay?" not "is the business declining?"
- "EBITDA" adds back amortization and depreciation indiscriminately — these point in opposite directions. A capital-intensive business pitching EBITDA is almost certainly flattering its economics.

## Definition

GAAP distortion refers to gaps between a company's reported figures and its economic reality that arise from the accounting rules themselves — not from management decisions about what to include or exclude. This is the key distinction from [earnings-quality](earnings-quality.md) failures: where earnings-quality problems originate from management labeling and adjustment choices, GAAP distortions are rule-mandated. The accounting framework requires including items that are economically misleading, or excluding items that matter, regardless of what management prefers.

## Why it matters

GAAP is a consistent reporting framework, not an economic measurement system. For most businesses, the two track reasonably well. But where a company holds a large equity portfolio or has grown substantially through acquisitions, the gaps compound and can reach a scale that makes standard analysis unreliable — converting the income statement from a signal into noise.

## Investment items embed portfolio movements in operating results

Three rules force portfolio events into the income statement, swamping operating performance:

**Unrealized investment gains/losses in net income.** A FASB rule effective for 2018 requires that the net change in unrealized equity gains and losses appear in net income each quarter and year. For a company with a large equity portfolio, a single quarter can swing $10B or more through the income statement. Buffett's verdict when the rule was announced: the GAAP bottom line would become "useless for analytical purposes" ([berkshire-2017-letter](../sources/berkshire-2017-letter.md)).

**Realized investment gains/losses.** GAAP has always required including realized gains and losses in net income. These fluctuate with the timing of sales — a portfolio manager sells "when that seems the intelligent thing to do," not to influence earnings — so reported gains can be high in a weak portfolio year, and vice versa. The 2018 unrealized-gains rule compounded this pre-existing problem.

**Goodwill impairments.** A large write-down passes through GAAP net income as a loss but diagnoses a *valuation error*, not operating deterioration. Berkshire's 2020 PCC write-down was explicitly a misjudgment of purchase price — "I was simply too optimistic about PCC's normalized profit potential" — not evidence that PCC's business had declined ([berkshire-2020-letter](../sources/berkshire-2020-letter.md)). A write-down can dominate the GAAP figure and be mistaken for a business failure when it is actually an acquisition-price correction.

## Non-cash charges cut in opposite directions

Two non-cash charge rules produce distortions of opposite sign, making their combined add-back (as in EBITDA) analytically destructive:

**Acquisition-related amortization overstates cost.** Purchase accounting requires amortizing acquired intangibles — customer relationships, brand, non-competes — over time. But where these assets retain their economic value, no real depletion is occurring. The charge inflates stated cost above reality. This is the one widely accepted legitimate add-back in adjusted earnings calculations. See [earnings-quality](earnings-quality.md) for the full framing.

**Depreciation understates cost in capital-intensive businesses.** Depreciation calculated on historical cost systematically understates the real replacement cost of assets. Maintenance capex routinely exceeds the accounting charge. Adding back depreciation (as EBITDA does) produces earnings figures above what the business actually earns for its owners. See [owner-earnings](owner-earnings.md) for the formula.

The practical upshot: these two distortions point in opposite directions, and EBITDA that conflates them is making two errors simultaneously — one directionally correct and one not.

## How it's discussed in sources

- [berkshire-2016-letter](../sources/berkshire-2016-letter.md) — background context: the pre-existing realized-gains distortion documented; BNSF GAAP depreciation gap shown to understate true maintenance cost — both rule-mandated problems established before the 2017 rule change
- [berkshire-2017-letter](../sources/berkshire-2017-letter.md) — the founding source: new FASB unrealized-gains rule announced; potential for $10B+ quarterly swings named; GAAP bottom-line called "useless for analytical purposes"; Friday-evening release explained as the practical remedy; "normalized per-share earning power" named as the right scorecard
- [berkshire-2018-letter](../sources/berkshire-2018-letter.md) — first year the rule appeared in results; actual swings confirmed the warning; sharpest amortization/depreciation distinction drawn (acquisition amortization as legitimate add-back; GAAP depreciation as understatement of economic cost); "Focus on operating earnings, paying little attention to gains or losses of any variety"
- [berkshire-2019-letter](../sources/berkshire-2019-letter.md) — the clearest illustration: "a crazy 1,900% increase in GAAP earnings" driven by accounting mechanics while operating earnings were "little changed"; the rule described as enshrining "capricious fluctuations" into mandatory reporting
- [berkshire-2020-letter](../sources/berkshire-2020-letter.md) — adds the goodwill-impairment case: large write-downs flow through GAAP net income but diagnose a valuation error, not business failure; the PCC write-down as the case study; the full GAAP component breakdown is on the source page
- [berkshire-2023-letter](../sources/berkshire-2023-letter.md) — most rhetorically forceful restatement: the mandated figure called "worse-than-useless"; EBITDA declared "a banned measurement at Berkshire"; operating earnings offered "only as a starting point, not a finishing line"
- [berkshire-2024-letter](../sources/berkshire-2024-letter.md) — late-period restatement: record operating earnings emphasized over GAAP; "EBITDA, a flawed favorite of Wall Street, is not for us"
