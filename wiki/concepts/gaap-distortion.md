# GAAP Distortion

## Definition

GAAP distortion refers to systematic gaps between a company's reported figures and its economic reality that arise from the accounting rules themselves — not from management decisions about what to include or exclude. This is the key distinction from [earnings-quality](earnings-quality.md) failures: where earnings-quality problems originate from management labeling and adjustment choices, GAAP distortions are rule-mandated. The accounting framework requires including items that are economically misleading, or excluding items that matter, regardless of what management prefers.

Four specific rules create the most significant distortions in practice:

1. **Unrealized investment gains/losses in net income** — a FASB rule effective for 2018 reporting periods requires that the net change in unrealized equity gains and losses appear in net income each quarter and year. For a company like Berkshire with $170B in marketable equities, a single quarter can swing $10B or more through the income statement, swamping the operating performance that actually reflects business results. GAAP net income for such a company is, in Buffett's words, "useless for analytical purposes."

2. **Realized investment gains/losses** — even before the 2017 unrealized-gains rule, GAAP had always required including realized investment gains and losses in net income. These fluctuate randomly: a portfolio manager sells a security "when that seems the intelligent thing to do," not to influence earnings — so reported gains can be high in a year when the overall portfolio performed poorly, and vice versa. The new rule compounds this pre-existing problem.

3. **Acquisition-related amortization with no economic content** — purchase-accounting rules require that acquired intangibles be valued and amortized over time. But this amortization often has no corresponding economic cost: customer relationships, brand, and proprietary processes don't shrink simply because accounting requires reducing their book balance. This is the one widely accepted legitimate add-back in adjusted earnings calculations. See [earnings-quality](earnings-quality.md) for the full analytical framing of why it is legitimate while depreciation is not.

4. **Depreciation that understates true maintenance cost** — in capital-intensive businesses, depreciation based on historical cost systematically understates the real replacement cost of assets. BNSF requires maintenance capex far in excess of its GAAP depreciation charge. See [earnings-quality](earnings-quality.md) for the analytical distinction between depreciation (an understatement of economic cost) and acquisition amortization (a cost with no economic content).

## Why it matters

GAAP is a consistent reporting framework, not an economic measurement system. For most businesses, the two track reasonably well. But where a company holds a large equity portfolio or has grown substantially through acquisitions, the gaps compound and can reach a scale that makes standard analysis unreliable.

The analytical response Buffett articulates is threefold. First, use **operating earnings** — Berkshire's preferred figure, which excludes both realized and unrealized investment gains and losses — as the primary signal. This is different from "adjusted earnings" as typically presented by other companies; Berkshire's operating earnings strip only rule-mandated non-economic items, not management-chosen cost exclusions. Second, anchor evaluation on **normalized per-share earning power**: the 2017 letter names this as the correct scorecard, shifting focus from accounting periods to underlying business economics. Third, recognize the **Friday-evening release practice** as a deliberate remedy: because television commentary and newspaper headlines almost always focus on the year-over-year change in GAAP net income, Berkshire releases quarterly and annual results late on Friday or early Saturday, allowing investors maximum time for analysis before markets open Monday.

## How it's discussed in sources

- [berkshire-2017-letter](../sources/berkshire-2017-letter.md) — the central source; new FASB rule introduced; $170B equity portfolio at yearend; potential for $10B+ quarterly swings; GAAP bottom-line described as "useless for analytical purposes"; compounds the existing realized-gains problem; "normalized per-share earning power" named as the right scorecard; Friday-evening release rationale explained; amortization charges ($1.3B) flagged as "in large part not a true economic cost"; BNSF depreciation shortfall noted
- [berkshire-2016-letter](../sources/berkshire-2016-letter.md) — documents the existing realized-gains problem (already excluded from Berkshire's operating earnings before 2017); BNSF GAAP depreciation ($2.1B) shown to understate true maintenance cost; background context for the distortions the 2017 rule compounded
- [berkshire-2018-letter](../sources/berkshire-2018-letter.md) — first year the new unrealized-gains rule appears in reported results; actual swings of $25.4B (Q4 loss) to $18.5B (Q3 profit) in GAAP net income; "Our advice? Focus on operating earnings, paying little attention to gains or losses of any variety"; amortization vs. depreciation distinction drawn precisely ($1.4B acquisition amortization as legitimate exclusion; $8.4B depreciation as understatement)
- [berkshire-2019-letter](../sources/berkshire-2019-letter.md) — the clearest two-year illustration of the rule's distorting power: 2018 GAAP earnings $4B (down market; unrealized losses of $20.6B ran through income); 2019 GAAP earnings $81.4B (up market; unrealized gains of $53.7B ran through income) — a "crazy 1,900% increase in GAAP earnings" from accounting mechanics while operating earnings were "little changed"; the rule described as a "monumental shift" in accounting thinking that requires enshrinement of "capricious fluctuations" in quarterly and annual reported earnings; Buffett and Munger reiterate: focus on operating earnings and ignore both realized and unrealized investment gains or losses
- [berkshire-2023-letter](../sources/berkshire-2023-letter.md) — the most rhetorically forceful restatement of the unrealized-gains distortion: GAAP net income of $90B (2021), –$23B (2022), $96B (2023) versus operating earnings of $27.6B / $30.9B / $37.4B; the mandated figure called "worse-than-useless," sanctified by FASB, SEC, and Deloitte yet useless "in evaluating a business"; unrealized gains "at times can exceed $5 billion a day"; the pre-2018 convention noted as the prior rule ("the 'improvement' was mandated" in 2018; "Galileo's experience … should have taught us not to mess with mandates from on high"); operating earnings offered "only as a starting point"; **EBITDA declared "a banned measurement at Berkshire."** A late-period summary, not a new framework
- [berkshire-2020-letter](../sources/berkshire-2020-letter.md) — introduces a fifth distortion type: the $11B after-tax PCC goodwill write-down passes through GAAP net income as a loss but signals a *valuation error*, not a business failure; "I was wrong, however, in judging the average amount of future earnings and, consequently, wrong in my calculation of the proper price to pay for the business" — the impairment diagnoses the acquisition price, not the business quality; a large write-down can dominate GAAP net income and be mistaken for operating deterioration; complete 2020 GAAP breakdown: $21.9B operating + $4.9B realized gains + $26.7B unrealized gains − $11B write-down = $42.5B
- [berkshire-2024-letter](../sources/berkshire-2024-letter.md) — a one-line late-period restatement: record operating earnings ($47.4B) "endlessly" emphasized over the GAAP figure because operating earnings exclude capital gains/losses (realized or unrealized) that "swing wildly and unpredictably" year to year; "EBITDA, a flawed favorite of Wall Street, is not for us"
