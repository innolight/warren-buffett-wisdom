# Arbitrage (Risk Arbitrage)

**Buying a security at a discount to the expected outcome of a publicly-announced corporate event and earning the spread when the event closes — the "risk" is that the event fails.**

## Actionable takeaway

Before entering any risk-arbitrage position, answer four questions in order:

1. **How probable is the event?** Announced deals fail — antitrust blocks, financing collapses, boards reverse. Assign a realistic probability, not a hopeful one.
2. **How long will capital be tied up?** Annualize the return. A 6% spread over three months is 24% annualized; the same spread over two years is 3%.
3. **What is the upside if something better happens?** A competing bid can improve terms above the announcement. Structured contingent claims can resolve to large positive surprises.
4. **What is the downside if the event fails?** In leveraged buyouts, the target often falls well below its pre-announcement price when a deal breaks. Probability times magnitude — not probability alone.

Two structural rules: participate only in **publicly-announced** transactions (no rumor plays, no market-intelligence trades) and **concentrate** in a few well-analyzed positions rather than diversifying across many weakly-analyzed ones.

## Definition

Risk arbitrage in Buffett's usage — distinct from statistical pairs trading — is the practice of buying into a publicly-announced corporate transaction (merger, tender offer, liquidation, spin-off) at a discount to the expected outcome, then earning the spread when the event closes. The methodology originates with [ben-graham](../people/ben-graham.md) and Graham-Newman Corp., where Buffett learned it from 1954–56; the 1988 letter grounds Berkshire's practice in that 63-year lineage.

The four questions above force a structured expected-value calculation: probability-weighted upside against probability-weighted downside, with time explicitly priced. Any position that fails this calculation at realistic probabilities should be passed.

## Why it matters

Three reasons arbitrage is load-bearing in investment thinking:

1. **Returns uncorrelated with the market.** Spreads compress or expand based on deal-specific risk — regulatory climate, financing conditions, competing bids — not on whether the S&P rises or falls. This uncorrelated return stream is valuable precisely during market dislocations (see [volatility-as-opportunity](volatility-as-opportunity.md)).

2. **Forces disciplined downside analysis.** Question four — what happens if the deal fails — requires quantifying the worst case before committing. This is [margin-of-safety](margin-of-safety.md) discipline applied to time-bound event bets: not "what is the upside" but "what is the magnitude of loss if I am wrong."

3. **An unusually clean empirical test of market efficiency.** Arbitrage operates on public information only; no non-public facts, no hindsight selection. A 63-year track record across Graham-Newman, the Buffett Partnership, and Berkshire — approximately 20% pre-tax annually vs. roughly 10% for the market — is among the most methodologically clean challenges to Efficient Market Theory available.

## The failure mode: downside asymmetry when deals break

The most dangerous position is an LBO where the target has been bid up sharply on announcement. If the deal fails, the stock may fall well below its pre-announcement price, because that price itself had been inflated by deal speculation. Question four is not optional.

The Arcata Corp. case ([berkshire-1988-letter](../sources/berkshire-1988-letter.md)) illustrates the inverse: a 1981 LBO that included a contingent share of a federal condemnation claim settled years later for $519M, yielding $29.48 per share in additional proceeds after Berkshire had fully exited the announced position. Structured optionality on uncertain outcomes can resolve far above what an initial four-question analysis priced.

## The analytical edge lives within the public-information rule

Berkshire participates only in publicly-announced transactions — no trading on rumors or market intelligence. This constraint maintains legal integrity and preserves the methodological cleanliness that makes the track record a valid empirical test.

Within that constraint, analytical edge is real and earns its return. Rockwood & Co. (1954) required understanding an obscure tax structure: LIFO-valued cocoa-bean inventory could be distributed to shareholders in exchange for stock without triggering the embedded gain, if the distribution was part of a plan to shrink the business. Identifying and exploiting that spread required knowing the tax code, not knowing anything non-public. The constraint forces the analyst toward structural insight and away from information shortcuts (see [circle-of-competence](circle-of-competence.md)).

## Concentrate in the few well-understood situations

Typical arbitrage operations diversify across many small positions, relying on the law of large numbers to produce stable aggregate results. Berkshire takes a few large positions per year — the same logic as in [concentration-vs-diversification](concentration-vs-diversification.md): an analyst who can assess deal probability has reason to concentrate in the best situations, not diversify into also-rans.

The RJR Nabisco position (1988–89) is the live illustration: a single large bet, entered once the KKR deal had substantial financing and regulatory momentum, rather than spreading across dozens of less-analyzed deals at the same time. The casino has no analytical edge on individual bets and therefore benefits from volume; a skilled analyst does have an edge and should press it on the best opportunities.

## The empirical challenge to market efficiency

EMT claims that public information is fully priced. The 63-year arbitrage record runs directly at that claim:

- **Public information only** — every transaction publicly announced; no non-public research required.
- **Scale and continuity** — hundreds of transactions, no hindsight selection.
- **Result** — approximately 20% pre-tax annually vs. roughly 10% for the market; $1,000 at those rates over 63 years becomes ~$97M vs. ~$405K.

> In any sort of a contest — financial, mental, or physical — it's an enormous advantage to have opponents who have been taught that it's useless to even try. ([berkshire-1988-letter](../sources/berkshire-1988-letter.md))

The practical corollary: EMT's continued teaching keeps spreads available for those who do the work.
