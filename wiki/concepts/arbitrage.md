# Arbitrage (Risk Arbitrage)

## Definition

Risk arbitrage — Buffett's usage, not the modern statistical sense — is the practice of buying securities involved in publicly-announced corporate transactions (mergers, tender offers, liquidations, spin-offs) at a discount to the expected transaction price, then earning the spread when the transaction closes. The "risk" is that the announced event fails to occur.

The methodology is captured in four questions, articulated in [berkshire-1988-letter](../sources/berkshire-1988-letter.md) as the canonical framework for evaluating any arbitrage situation:

1. **How likely is it that the promised event will indeed occur?** Not all announced transactions close; some fail to antitrust, financing collapses, or seller second-thoughts.
2. **How long will your money be tied up?** Return must be measured as annualized; a 6% spread over three months is 24% annualized; the same spread over two years is 3%.
3. **What chance is there that something still better will transpire?** A competing bid can improve the outcome beyond the announced terms — as in the Arcata case below.
4. **What will happen if the event does not take place?** The downside if the deal breaks. In leveraged transactions, the target stock may fall sharply below its pre-announcement price when a deal collapses; the arbitrageur must assess both the probability and the magnitude of the downside.

The four questions together force a structured expected-value analysis: probability-weighted upside against probability-weighted downside, with time explicitly priced.

## Why it matters

Three reasons arbitrage is load-bearing in investment thinking:

1. **Returns uncorrelated with the general market.** Arbitrage spreads compress or expand based on deal-specific risk — regulatory climate, financing conditions, competing bids — not on whether the S&P 500 rises or falls. The return stream is genuinely different in character from equity market exposure. This is the basis for the [volatility-as-opportunity](volatility-as-opportunity.md) point: when markets fall sharply, non-correlated return streams become especially valuable.

2. **The cleanest empirical test of market efficiency.** Arbitrage operates on publicly-announced information only; no obscure facts, no insider knowledge, no hindsight selection. A 63-year arbitrage track record (Graham-Newman, Buffett Partnership, Berkshire) that substantially outperforms the market on those terms is among the most methodologically clean challenges to Efficient Market Theory (EMT) available. See the EMT section below.

3. **Forces rigorous downside analysis.** The fourth question — what happens if the deal fails — requires quantifying the worst-case before committing. This is the [margin-of-safety](margin-of-safety.md) discipline applied to time-bound event bets: not "what is the upside" but "what is the downside if I'm wrong." Any investor who learns to ask question four habitually has adopted a useful general discipline.

## Berkshire's approach

Berkshire's arbitrage practice differs from typical risk-arbitrage operations in two respects, both described in [berkshire-1988-letter](../sources/berkshire-1988-letter.md):

**Concentration, not diversification.** Typical arbitrage operations spread across many small positions, relying on the law of large numbers to produce stable aggregate results. Berkshire participates in a few large deals per year. This is the same concentration logic as in [concentration-vs-diversification](concentration-vs-diversification.md): when you can evaluate individual situations well, concentrating in the best opportunities produces better risk-adjusted results than diversifying across many so-so ones. The casino analogy applies in reverse — the casino *can* rely on many bets because it has no analytical edge that would make individual bets preferable; a skilled analyst who can assess deal probability has a reason to concentrate.

**Announced transactions only; no rumor plays.** Berkshire participates only in deals that are publicly announced. No trading on whispers, M&A rumors, or "market intelligence." This discipline keeps the operation within the public-information domain, avoids legal exposure, and maintains the methodological cleanliness that makes the track record a valid empirical test.

## Case studies

Three cases anchor the 1988 arbitrage essay, spanning 35 years of practice:

### Rockwood & Co. (1954)

Buffett's first documented arbitrage, at age 24 while working at Graham-Newman. Rockwood was a Brooklyn chocolate company sitting on a large LIFO-valued cocoa-bean inventory that had appreciated substantially. A quirk in the tax code allowed companies to distribute assets to shareholders in exchange for stock without triggering the embedded gain — provided the distribution was part of a plan to shrink the business. Rockwood tendered: shareholders could exchange each share for 80 pounds of cocoa beans.

Buffett's edge: he understood the tax structure; he bought shares, tendered them for beans, and sold the beans, cycling through repeatedly. The spread between the implied value of 80 pounds of beans and the share price was the arbitrage profit. His "only expense was subway tokens" — a characteristically terse measure of transaction costs. Jay Pritzker was the architect of the tax structure. This case illustrates the analytical dimension of early arbitrage: it required understanding obscure tax code, not just reading a merger agreement.

### Arcata Corp. (1981–88)

A seven-year, multi-stage case that illustrates both the upside of question three (competing bids and contingent claims) and the value of patience. In 1981, KKR announced an LBO of Arcata at $37 per share plus two-thirds of any eventual recovery on a redwood-forest condemnation claim the U.S. government had not yet resolved. Berkshire built a 7% position.

The deal immediately went sideways. KKR cut its offer to $33.50, then $35; a competing buyer emerged at $37.50 plus one-half of the redwood claim. Berkshire took its profit on the initial holding — roughly 15% annualized on a six-month position — and considered the case closed.

Then in 1987, years after Berkshire had fully exited the initial position, the U.S. government settled the redwood condemnation claim for approximately $519 million — far above expectations. Berkshire's earlier shares entitled it to $29.48 per share in additional proceeds, or roughly $19.3 million additional profit. Total realized gain substantially exceeded the initial spread calculation. The lesson: the four-question framework prices the announced terms; it cannot fully price optionality on contingent claims, which sometimes resolves to large positive surprises.

### RJR Nabisco (1988–89)

The largest and most prominent leveraged buyout of its era. Berkshire held 3.342 million shares at yearend 1988 at $282 million cost against $304.5 million market value. In January 1989, Berkshire increased the position to approximately 4 million shares; in February, when KKR's tender offer closed, the position was fully eliminated. Pre-tax profit approximately $64 million on a holding period of a few months.

This case illustrates the concentration principle in action: a single large bet on a high-confidence situation (the KKR deal had substantial financing and regulatory momentum by the time Berkshire entered in size) rather than spreading across dozens of smaller, less-analyzed positions.

## The EMT refutation

The 1988 letter's most durable theoretical contribution is its use of the 63-year arbitrage record as an empirical challenge to Efficient Market Theory.

The conditions that make the challenge methodologically clean:

- **Public information only.** Every transaction Berkshire participated in was publicly announced. No non-public information. The efficient market hypothesis claims that public information is fully priced; these results were earned on public information alone.
- **Scale and independence.** Hundreds of transactions over 63 years (Graham-Newman 1926–56, Buffett Partnership 1956–69, Berkshire 1969–88). Not a few lucky breaks.
- **No hindsight selection.** The record is a continuous journal, not a retrospective selection of winners.

The result: roughly 20% pre-tax annual returns from arbitrage vs. roughly 10% for the broader market. On $1,000 starting capital over 63 years at these rates: approximately $97 million (arbitrage) vs. approximately $405,000 (market). The compounding gap is staggering.

EMT's response to such evidence is typically institutional rather than analytical:

> A reluctance to recant, and thereby to demystify the priesthood, is not limited to theologians.

The practical corollary is characteristically paradoxical:

> In any sort of a contest — financial, mental, or physical — it's an enormous advantage to have opponents who have been taught that it's useless to even try.

Buffett's recommendation: "Grahamites should probably endow chairs to ensure the perpetual teaching of EMT." The continued teaching of market efficiency guarantees a population of competitors who decline to do the work that arbitrage requires, keeping spreads available for those who do.

## Relationship to Graham

The arbitrage methodology originates with [ben-graham](../people/ben-graham.md) and Graham-Newman Corp., where Buffett learned it from 1954–56. Graham's arbitrage framework is the direct ancestor of Buffett's four-question test. The 1988 letter grounds the full 63-year track record in the Graham-Newman starting point, crediting Graham with the intellectual origin of the methodology. Arbitrage is thus one of the clearest through-lines from Graham's value-investing doctrine into Berkshire's practice.
