# Leverage Discipline

Never borrow so much that a stumble becomes a catastrophe; never accept earnings that exist only because debt is measured by a definition crafted to make them appear.

## Actionable takeaway

- **Cover ALL interest, not just cash interest.** Require coverage of accrued PIK and zero-coupon obligations — not only cash payments. Measure coverage against [owner-earnings](../concepts/owner-earnings.md), not EBITDA: depreciation is a real cost, not a free add-back.
- **Apply the adversity test before buying.** Ask whether the business can service its debt through a two-year recession with zero credit-market access. A capital structure viable only under benign assumptions has no margin of safety by definition.
- **Reject the fallen-angel historical analogy.** Newly-issued junk bonds are structurally different from fallen angels; their historical default rates do not transfer. Require structural analysis of each deal's coverage on its own terms.
- **Watch for leverage addiction.** A management that has recently expanded leverage is unlikely to retreat voluntarily; the next adverse turn — not the current favorable conditions — is the test that matters.

## Definition

Leverage discipline is the principle that debt should be sized so that adverse outcomes leave the business viable, and that earnings manufactured through debt-structure definitions are not real earnings. The core asymmetry: "A small chance of distress or disgrace cannot, in our view, be offset by a large chance of extra returns." ([berkshire-1989-letter](../sources/berkshire-1989-letter.md)) Ruin has no recovery — a zero in a compounding sequence destroys everything that preceded it.

## Why it matters

Debt changes the nature of business failure. Without it, a stumble loses ground; with excessive debt, the same stumble can be fatal — fixed interest obligations do not pause during downturns, covenant breaches give lenders remedies equity holders lack, and forced asset sales during distress occur at the worst possible prices. The second problem is that excessive leverage generates accounting pressure: when operating earnings cannot service debt, the incentive is to redefine what counts as earnings until the coverage ratios look adequate.

## EBITDA is a ratchet that converts unserviceable debt into apparent coverage

The [berkshire-1989-letter](../sources/berkshire-1989-letter.md) traces the deception as a two-step progression. The first migration moves from EBIT to EBITDA by adding back depreciation. But depreciation is not optional — it represents the real consumption of productive assets. A business that spends only its depreciation allowance and calls the rest income is silently liquidating itself. The correct measure is [owner-earnings](../concepts/owner-earnings.md): replace accounting depreciation with actual maintenance capex to find the cash a business could actually distribute.

The second migration excludes accrued interest from the coverage denominator, comparing EBITDA not against total interest (cash and accrued) but against cash interest only. PIK and zero-coupon instruments accrete real obligations onto the balance sheet without triggering cash payments; excluding them fabricates a coverage ratio by defining out the costs being measured. This is John Kenneth Galbraith's "bezzle" reframed: both issuer and holder book income, no cash moves, and the reckoning is deferred to maturity or passed to the next holder.

The two manipulations are complementary: PIK/zero-coupon instruments reduce the cash-interest denominator; EBITDA excluding accruals inflates the earnings numerator. Together they make an unserviceable capital structure appear sound. "The man claiming to be a financial alchemist may become rich. But gullible investors rather than business achievements will usually be the source of his wealth." ([berkshire-1989-letter](../sources/berkshire-1989-letter.md))

*When a zero-coupon structure is honest:* Berkshire issued zero-coupon convertible debentures in 1989 — not a contradiction of the above, but a test of the distinction. That structure created a genuine cash benefit (tax deduction on accreted interest without cash outlay) and included put dates protecting holders from a ruinous maturity-day reckoning. The test: does the structure deliver a real economic benefit, or does it merely defer obligations the business cannot service?

## Newly-issued junk bonds were a different universe from the fallen-angel data

The [berkshire-1990-letter](../sources/berkshire-1990-letter.md) identifies the statistical sleight-of-hand that powered 1980s junk-bond marketing: historical default rates from fallen angels — investment-grade bonds downgraded when issuers ran into trouble — were used to project default rates for newly-issued LBO debt. The populations were structurally different. A fallen-angel issuer was originally designed with margin of safety; management typically has incentive to regain investment-grade status. A newly-issued LBO was designed from inception with coverage ratios that assumed no adversity; management's incentive was a sale or IPO exit before maturity.

The illustration: a Tampa television station whose interest expense exceeded gross revenues — a structure unviable under any realistic operating assumption. "The roads of business are riddled with potholes; a plan that requires dodging them all is a plan for disaster." "Mountains of junk bonds were sold by those who didn't care to those who didn't think — and there was no shortage of either." A bond repayable only if everything goes right has no margin of safety. See [margin-of-safety](../concepts/margin-of-safety.md).

## Debt creates catastrophic asymmetry, not merely added risk

The 2010 "Life and Debt" section distills the core logic: "Any series of positive numbers, however impressive the numbers may be, evaporates when multiplied by a single zero. History tells us that leverage all too often produces zeroes, even when it is employed by very smart people." ([berkshire-2010-letter](../sources/berkshire-2010-letter.md)) The compounding argument for leverage assumes the return sequence never contains a zero; history does not support that assumption.

Refinancing risk compounds the danger: companies with large debts routinely assume they can refinance at maturity — usually correctly, until a company-specific problem or market-wide credit seizure makes that assumption wrong, and "only cash will do the job." The dagger mounted on the steering wheel: normal roads are survivable; the one pothole at speed is not. The same structural fragility extends to leveraged derivatives positions (see [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md)), which become unmanageable at precisely the moment markets are most illiquid. Leverage is also self-reinforcing: "once having profited from its wonders, very few people retreat to more conservative practices." The constructive policy response is the [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md): a minimum cash reserve sized to survive any foreseeable disruption without accessing credit markets.
