# Franchise vs. Business

**The binary that determines which valuation model applies: a franchise earns high returns from structural pricing power and tolerates mediocre management; a business earns exceptional returns only through cost leadership or temporarily tight supply and can be killed by poor management.**

## Actionable takeaway

- **Apply the three-condition test before assigning a premium multiple.** Does the product or service face no close substitute in customers' minds? Is pricing unregulated? Can the company price aggressively and still earn high returns on capital? All three must hold — partial scores produce weak franchises at best.
- **Treat category reclassification as a valuation cliff, not a slope.** When any condition erodes, the correct model shifts from perpetuity (~25× after-tax) to bob-around (~10× after-tax). The collapse is abrupt; watch for structural forces — not merely cyclical ones — that can make the crossing permanent.
- **Use the management-tolerance test as a signal.** A business whose returns require an excellent manager is structurally different from one that survives mediocrity. Pay franchise multiples only where structure — not the person — protects returns.
- **Flag discipline-based advantages in commodity industries as fragile.** Consistent process discipline can produce franchise-like returns while competitors abandon it, but those returns carry no demand-side anchor and hold only as long as management maintains the discipline.

## Definition

An **economic franchise** arises from a product or service that:

1. is needed or desired;
2. is thought by its customers to have no close substitute; and
3. is not subject to price regulation.

The empirical test: a franchise can regularly price its product or service aggressively and thereby earn high rates of return on capital. **Franchises can tolerate mismanagement** — inept managers may diminish a franchise's profitability, but they cannot inflict mortal damage.

A **business**, in contrast, earns exceptional profits only if it is the low-cost operator or if supply is tight. Tightness in supply usually does not last long. With superior management, a company may maintain low-cost-operator status for a long time, but it faces unceasing competitive attack. **A business, unlike a franchise, can be killed by poor management.**

Many companies fall in between and are best described as "weak franchises" or "strong businesses."

## Why it matters

The distinction reframes valuation math. A franchise can be modeled as a growing perpetuity — earnings rise at some real rate, capital requirements stay modest, and the present value of future cash flows is large. A business should be modeled as a "bob-around" — earnings fluctuate around a mean, growth requires capital, and the multiple deserved on current earnings is much lower.

The 1991 letter does the explicit math for media, which Buffett had reclassified from franchise to business:

> Posit that the $1 million represents "normal earning power" and that earnings will bob around this figure cyclically … $1 million of earnings, discounted by the same 10%, translates to a $10 million valuation. Thus a seemingly modest shift in assumptions reduces the property's valuation to 10 times after-tax earnings (or about 6 1/2 times pre-tax earnings).

A franchise modeled at 25× after-tax (perpetuity growing at 6% discounted at 10%) becomes a business at 10× after-tax once the perpetual-growth assumption is removed. The valuation does not gently degrade as the moat erodes; it collapses to a different category of math.

## Franchise erosion: reclassification and the valuation cliff

The most consequential application of the framework is the media reclassification. Newspapers, network television, and magazines had been textbook franchises through the early 1980s — high penetration, indispensable to advertisers, pricing power outpacing inflation. By 1991, Buffett concluded that secular forces (not merely a cyclical downturn) had moved them across the line:

- Advertising dollars growing slowly.
- Retailers (Wal-Mart and direct-mail) bypassing mass media.
- Print and electronic ad channels proliferating, fragmenting audiences.

The result: "advertising dollars are more widely dispersed and the pricing power of ad vendors has diminished." The intrinsic value of [buffalo-evening-news](../entities/buffalo-evening-news.md), [capital-cities](../entities/capital-cities.md), and [washington-post](../entities/washington-post.md) all declined — not from management failure but from category shift.

> Gone are the days of bullet-proof franchises and cornucopian economics. ([berkshire-1991-letter](../sources/berkshire-1991-letter.md))

## Management tolerance as a diagnostic

The asymmetry runs both directions:

- A franchise **tolerates** mediocre management. Buffett's TV-station example: "if you were smart enough to buy a network TV station very early in the game, you could put in a shiftless and backward nephew to run things, and the business would still do well for decades." See [berkshire-1995-letter](../sources/berkshire-1995-letter.md) on the "have-to-be-smart-once" vs. "have-to-be-smart-every-day" distinction.
- A business **requires** great management to survive. Retailers, airlines, commodity producers — these need talented operators day after day to stay alive, let alone profitable.

The framing is a useful purchase filter: pay a franchise multiple only when the franchise test is met; otherwise pay a business multiple regardless of management quality.

## Variant: commodity industries with discipline-based returns

Clayton Homes (acquired 2003) adds an edge case. Manufactured housing is structurally commodity — substitutable product, intense price competition, no demand-side switching costs. Clayton earned franchise-like returns not from product differentiation but from underwriting discipline maintained while competitors adopted an originate-and-distribute model during the 1990s securitization boom. When that model collapsed, competitors' loan books imploded; Clayton's disciplined portfolio held. The differentiation was invisible in the product itself but entirely real in the criterion applied to who was financed.

The key distinction from a conventional franchise: this "moat" is supply-side discipline, not demand-side preference. Competitors could theoretically replicate it but chose not to because it required foregoing near-term volume. The advantage persists only as long as management maintains the discipline — the same fragility that afflicts any culture-based competitive advantage.

See [berkshire-2003-letter](../sources/berkshire-2003-letter.md) for the full manufactured-housing industry diagnosis.
