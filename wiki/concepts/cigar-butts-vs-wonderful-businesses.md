# Cigar Butts vs. Wonderful Businesses

## Definition

A canonical Buffett distillation of his own evolution as an investor, articulated most fully in the 1989 "Mistakes of the First Twenty-five Years" essay:

> **It's far better to buy a wonderful company at a fair price than a fair company at a wonderful price.** ([[berkshire-1989-letter]])

The "cigar butt" approach — buying stocks at a sufficiently low price that some hiccup in the business gives a chance to unload at a decent profit — works mechanically only for a liquidator. For an ongoing investor, it fails on two counts:

1. **The original "bargain" usually isn't.** "In a difficult business, no sooner is one problem solved than another surfaces — never is there just one cockroach in the kitchen."
2. **Time works against you.** "Any initial advantage you secure will be quickly eroded by the low return that the business earns. … **Time is the friend of the wonderful business, the enemy of the mediocre.**"

The contrast: a wonderful business compounds intrinsic value internally, so an investor who pays a fair price collects the compounding over time. A mediocre business at a bargain price requires a single re-rating event to be profitable — and the longer the holding period extends without that event, the closer the realized return drifts toward the business's mediocre underlying ROE.

## The Buffett evolution

The "cigar butt" approach is named for [[ben-graham]]'s tangible-asset-discount strategy — buying stocks below liquidation value, the way one might pick up a discarded cigar butt for one free puff. Buffett practiced it through the 1950s and 1960s with great success on small sums. He explicitly attributes the shift to [[charlie-munger]]:

> Charlie understood this early; I was a slow learner. But now, when buying companies or common stocks, we look for first-class businesses accompanied by first-class managements. ([[berkshire-1989-letter]])

The first acquisition of Berkshire itself (1965) is acknowledged as the founding mistake of the cigar-butt school — a textile business bought because it was cheap, not because it was good. Hochschild Kohn (a Baltimore department store, 1966) is the second example: "bought at a substantial discount from book value, the people were first-class, and the deal included some extras — unrecorded real estate values and a significant LIFO inventory cushion. How could I miss? So-o-o — three years later I was lucky to sell the business for about what I had paid."

## The toad-kissing variant (1992)

The 1992 letter restates the same principle as a critique of acquisition-hungry managers:

> Many acquisition-hungry managers were apparently mesmerized by their childhood reading of the story about the frog-kissing princess. Remembering her success, they pay dearly for the right to kiss corporate toads, expecting wondrous transfigurations. … Standing knee-deep in unresponsive toads, [the CEO] then announces an enormous "restructuring" charge.

And the personal admission:

> In my early days as a manager I, too, dated a few toads. They were cheap dates — I've never been much of a sport — but my results matched those of acquirers who courted higher-priced toads. I kissed and they croaked.

The 1992 distillation, recasting the same principle as acquisition policy: **"buy good businesses at fair prices rather than fair businesses at good prices."**

## Why fair prices for wonderful businesses are usually available

The implicit corollary: in liquid public markets, *fairly priced* wonderful businesses appear often enough to support a disciplined buyer. They rarely appear *cheaply* priced — but they don't need to. The compounding rate of the underlying business is doing the work, not the multiple expansion.

This is the central premise behind Berkshire's Coca-Cola purchase in 1988-89 ([[coca-cola]]): not a bargain at the time of purchase, but a wonderful business at a sensible price, with the business itself doing the compounding. By yearend 1991 — three years in — Coke's earnings had grown 38% per share while the stock price had risen far more, but Buffett's framing was unchanged: the long-run return depends on the business's earnings trajectory, not on the entry multiple.

## What this is not

This is *not* a license to overpay. The 1992 articulation includes the disciplined modifier: "an attractive price." A wonderful business bought at a manifestly stupid price is also a mistake. The hierarchy is:

1. Wonderful business at fair price → best.
2. Wonderful business at wonderful price → rare; back up the truck.
3. Fair business at wonderful price → cigar butt; works mechanically but not for compounding.
4. Wonderful business at stupid price → loses on multiple compression even if the business performs.
5. Fair business at fair price → no edge; avoid.
6. Anything at stupid price → avoid.

The cigar-butt critique is aimed at (3), not at (1).
