# Cigar Butts vs. Wonderful Businesses

**Buying a wonderful business at a fair price beats a fair business at a wonderful price — because time amplifies the underlying return, for good or ill, over every year of ownership.**

## Actionable takeaway

- Before buying, ask: can this business compound intrinsic value internally for a decade with minimal added capital? If not, the entry price must be exceptional — and even then, the clock works against you.
- Once you own a wonderful business at a fair price, hold it. Selling to capture a near-term re-rating surrenders the compounding that made the business worth owning.
- Never use shares of a high-quality business to acquire a mediocre one. The issuing shares keep appreciating; the acquired business may collapse. The gap between what you gave and what you received widens every year.
- Apply the cockroach test before buying a troubled business cheaply: in a difficult business there is never just one problem. The reputation of bad economics outlasts the reputation of any management hired to fix it.

## Definition

The "cigar butt" approach — from [ben-graham](../people/ben-graham.md)'s image of a discarded butt with one free puff left — means buying a stock so cheaply that any hiccup in the business gives a chance to sell at a profit. It works for a liquidator. For an ongoing investor, it fails on two counts: the original "bargain" rarely stays cheap once the next problem surfaces, and the mediocre underlying ROE pulls the realized return relentlessly toward itself with every year the position is held.

A wonderful business — one with durable competitive advantage and pricing power — compounds intrinsic value internally. An investor who pays a fair price collects that compounding over time, with the business doing the work. Buffett's 1989 distillation: *"It's far better to buy a wonderful company at a fair price than a fair company at a wonderful price. … Time is the friend of the wonderful business, the enemy of the mediocre."* He credits [charlie-munger](../people/charlie-munger.md) with understanding it first.

## Brilliant management cannot escape bad economics

The first practical fence: *"When a management with a reputation for brilliance tackles a business with a reputation for bad economics, it is the reputation of the business that remains intact."* Berkshire itself — bought in 1965 because the price looked cheap, not because the textile business was good — is the founding case. Hochschild Kohn (a Baltimore department store, 1966) reinforces it: bought at a discount to book with good management and hidden real-estate values; three years later Buffett was lucky to sell it for roughly what he paid.

The corollary the 1989 retrospective draws: *"We've done better by avoiding dragons than by slaying them."* The search for great businesses in easy circumstances beats the hunt for operational turnarounds.

## Cigar butts do not scale

The approach worked brilliantly on small sums — the 1950s were Buffett's best decade for relative performance, buying dozens of single-puff situations. The structural weakness only became apparent with size: beyond a certain scale, the hunt for cheap-but-mediocre situations yields diminishing returns, and marginal businesses remain the wrong foundation for a large and enduring enterprise.

See's Candies (acquired 1972 for $25M) was the turning point — and the proof. Earning roughly $4M pre-tax at acquisition on minimal tangible capital, See's possessed broad, durable competitive advantage that did not appear on its balance sheet. Buffett almost refused to pay the asking price. By 2014, See's had earned **$1.9 billion pre-tax** on incremental investment of only **$40 million**, with the capital it threw off funding further Berkshire acquisitions. "Envision rabbits breeding." For the structural anatomy of what makes See's a great business, see [great-good-gruesome](great-good-gruesome.md).

## Using quality stock to acquire a mediocre business is doubly destructive

The Dexter Shoe acquisition (1993) is the sharpest quantification: Berkshire paid $433 million for a domestic shoe manufacturer whose competitive advantage "was soon to evaporate because of foreign competition." The GAAP cost was $433M. The true economic cost was the Berkshire shares issued for the purchase, which by 2014 were worth approximately **$5.7 billion**. The shares kept appreciating as Berkshire compounded; Dexter's value went to zero. "As a financial disaster, this one deserves a spot in the Guinness Book of World Records."

The generalization: trading shares of a wonderful business for ownership of a so-so business is a permanent transfer that widens every year. See [stock-as-acquisition-currency](stock-as-acquisition-currency.md) for the acquisition mechanics.

## A single winner outweighs many errors

The late-period restatement runs as an asymmetry of consequences. A wonderful business compounds internally for decades; a mistake's damage is bounded. "A single winning decision can make a breathtaking difference over time. … Mistakes fade away; winners can forever blossom." ([berkshire-2024-letter](../sources/berkshire-2024-letter.md))

This is why a "decent batting average" suffices. The 2023 letter drew the same lesson from Coke and AMEX, held untouched for over two decades: "one wonderful business can offset the many mediocre decisions that are inevitable." ([berkshire-2023-letter](../sources/berkshire-2023-letter.md))

## What this is not

Not a license to overpay. A wonderful business at a manifestly excessive price is also a mistake. The hierarchy:

1. Wonderful business at fair price → best
2. Wonderful business at wonderful price → rare; back up the truck
3. Fair business at wonderful price → cigar butt; one puff, no compounding
4. Wonderful business at stupid price → loses on multiple compression even as the business performs
5. Fair business at fair price → no edge; avoid
6. Anything at stupid price → avoid

The cigar-butt critique targets (3), not (1).
