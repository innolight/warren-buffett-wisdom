# Investment Categories

## Definition

Buffett's 2011 taxonomy divides all investment possibilities into three categories, distinguished by whether the underlying asset produces anything. The framework's organizing principle is **purchasing-power preservation**: which category reliably delivers more purchasing power than was initially given up?

## The three categories

### Category 1: Currency-based investments

Bonds, mortgages, money-market funds, bank deposits — instruments denominated in a specific currency. Superficially "safe" because of contractual cash flows; actually among the most dangerous asset classes because:

- **Governments determine the ultimate value of money**, and systemic forces periodically produce inflationary policies.
- The dollar fell **86% in value** between 1965 and 2011 — meaning $1 of 1965 purchasing power requires $7 today.
- A tax-exempt institution needed 4.3% annual interest over that period merely to *maintain* purchasing power. A taxable investor paying 25% income tax who earned 5.7% from rolling T-bills saw 1.4% stripped by visible income tax and the remaining 4.3% devoured by the invisible inflation "tax" — a net real return of zero.
- High interest rates can compensate for this risk; low rates (as in 2011) do not: "Bonds promoted as offering risk-free returns are now priced to deliver return-free risk." — Shelby Cullom Davis, quoted by Buffett.

Beta of these instruments may be near zero, but their **risk in terms of purchasing-power loss is huge**. See [[risk-vs-volatility]] for why beta is the wrong measure.

Berkshire holds significant currency-based instruments despite this critique — primarily short-term U.S. Treasury bills — because ample liquidity occupies center stage and cannot be slighted. But this is a necessity, not a preference. See [[liquidity-fortress]].

### Category 2: Sterile assets

Assets that will never produce anything, purchased in the hope that someone else will pay more later. The paradigm case: **gold**.

Gold's two structural shortcomings: neither of much use nor procreative. Industrial and decorative demand is limited and cannot absorb new production. One ounce owned for an eternity remains one ounce. What drives gold purchases is the belief that the ranks of the fearful will grow — a self-reinforcing thesis that has proved correct in periods of fear but is ultimately circular.

The 2011 comparison: at $1,750/oz, the world's 170,000 metric tons of gold form a 68-foot cube worth $9.6 trillion — "Pile A." An equal sum — "Pile B" — could instead purchase:
- All U.S. cropland (400M acres, ~$200B annual output)
- 16 Exxon Mobils (the world's most profitable company, earning $40B+ annually)
- With ~$1 trillion left over for walking-around money

A century from now, Pile A will remain an inert cube. Pile B's farmland will have produced staggering quantities of crops; its Exxon Mobils will have delivered trillions in dividends and still hold vast assets. The cube cannot respond to human flourishing.

The sterile-asset category requires an ever-expanding pool of buyers. As "bandwagon" investors join, they create their own confirming truth — for a while. Bubbles blown large enough inevitably pop: "What the wise man does in the beginning, the fool does in the end." The same dynamic ran through tulips (17th century), Internet stocks and houses (early 2000s), and gold in any major fear cycle.

Both Category 1 and Category 2 enjoy **maximum popularity at peaks of fear**:
- Terror of economic collapse drives people to currency-based assets.
- Fear of currency collapse drives people to sterile assets like gold.
- Both peaks produce the wrong allocation: "cash is king" in late 2008, just when cash should have been deployed; "cash is trash" in the early 1980s, just when fixed-dollar investments were at their most attractive.

### Category 3: Productive assets

Businesses, farms, real estate — assets that produce output regardless of what currency is in use. The preferred category.

The key criterion: the asset should deliver output that **retains its purchasing-power value** while requiring a **minimum of new capital investment**. Farms, real estate, and many businesses (Coca-Cola, IBM, See's Candy) meet this double test. Regulated utilities fail it because inflation places heavy capital requirements on them — to earn more, their owners must invest more. Even so, they remain superior to sterile or currency-based alternatives.

Why productive assets are ultimately safer:
- Whether the currency a century from now is based on gold, seashells, or paper, people will exchange a few minutes of daily labor for a Coca-Cola or some peanut brittle. Human productive exchange persists independent of the monetary medium.
- A business is a "commercial cow" that lives for centuries and gives ever-greater quantities of "milk." Its value is determined by its capacity to deliver milk, not by the medium of exchange.
- The 20th century Dow rose from 66 to 11,497 while paying substantial dividends — despite two world wars, a depression, a dozen recessions, and countless monetary changes.

## Why it matters

The framework's contribution is to reframe what "safe" means. Category 1 instruments *feel* safe because their contractual cash flows arrive on time. But they are denominated in a currency whose purchasing power erodes — meaning the contractual certainty guarantees a real loss over long periods. Category 2 assets feel safe during specific fear episodes but produce nothing and depend on an expanding pool of fearful buyers.

The insight connects directly to Berkshire's preference for owning productive businesses and its reluctance to hold large amounts of long-dated fixed income. Float is invested overwhelmingly in equities and controlled businesses rather than bonds precisely because of this framework. See [[float]] and [[bonds-and-inflation]].

It also links to the formal definition of risk offered in the same letter: risk is not beta, it is the probability of permanent loss of purchasing power over the contemplated holding period. Category 1 and Category 2 instruments carry this risk systematically, even when their price volatility is low. See [[risk-vs-volatility]].
