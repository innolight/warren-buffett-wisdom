# Corporate Governance

## Definition

Corporate governance is the set of structures, incentives, and norms that determine whether a company is run in the interests of its owners or its managers. Buffett's consistent position: most public-company governance fails not because directors are bad people, but because structures produce misaligned incentives and the social dynamics of the boardroom punish candor.

## Why it matters

A company's governance quality is a risk multiplier. Where governance is strong, capital is allocated to create value for owners; where it is weak, management extracts value from owners while presenting an appearance of stewardship. Buffett's 2002–2003 essays — written in the aftermath of Enron, WorldCom, and the mutual-fund scandals — trace governance failures to causes that are structural, not individual: alignment (or its absence) through ownership, not through legal definitions.

## The structural cause of governance failure

The root mechanism is captured in a single admission from [[berkshire-2002-letter]]: "Too often I was silent when management made proposals that I judged to be counter to the interests of shareholders. In those cases, collegiality trumped independence."

Directors are typically chosen by or with the blessing of the CEO. They are embedded in a well-mannered boardroom with a powerful executive who drives the agenda. Challenging acquisitions, replacing the CEO, or resisting compensation-committee recommendations is socially costly — it registers as a personal affront in a setting where being considered "a team player" matters. "Boardroom atmosphere almost invariably sedates their fiduciary genes" ([[berkshire-2003-letter]]). This single observation explains far more governance failure than deliberate bad faith does.

### Formal independence as a thin reed

Legal definitions of director independence — no family ties to management, no material financial relationships — do not predict whether a director will actually challenge management when it matters. Buffett's 40-year observation across 19 boards and roughly 250 directors: most were technically independent but lacked at least one of business savvy, genuine interest, or courage to act. The 62-year mutual-fund case study is the strongest evidence: federal law has mandated independent fund directors since 1940; the result has been "absolutely pathetic" stewardship — routine rehiring of incumbent managers regardless of performance, mindless approval of above-market fees, and prompt endorsement of whoever paid the most when the management company was sold ([[berkshire-2002-letter]]).

The paradox of the independence rule: a director earning 100% of her income from director fees passes the "independent" legal test. A trusted business partner who earns 3% of income from legal fees for the company does not. The rule measures fee dependence, not intellectual or social independence.

### The compensation ratchet

CEO pay has a structural upward bias. Compensation consultants benchmark against peer groups; any above-median grant becomes the new peer minimum; peers grant to match, creating a new median. "In the 1890s, Samuel Gompers described the goal of organized labor as 'More!' In the 1990s, America's CEOs adopted his battle cry" ([[berkshire-2002-letter]]). Consultants have a structural interest in higher grants, since both existing and prospective clients are offended by downward recommendations. "There are two classes of clients you don't want to offend — actual and potential" ([[berkshire-2003-letter]]).

The result is that compensation overreaching "greatly accelerated in the 1990s as compensation packages gained by the most avaricious … were promptly replicated elsewhere" ([[berkshire-2003-letter]]). The ratchet runs in only one direction because the benchmarking mechanism is disconnected from performance.

### Options non-expensing as governance failure in miniature

The history of stock-option accounting is a case study in how management can capture accounting standards to serve its own interests. Arthur Andersen's Professional Standards Group ruled in 1992 that options were an expense. The firm's "rainmaking" partners overruled the PSG to protect client reported earnings. FASB voted 7-0 to require expensing. The Senate voted 88-9 against under CEO-lobbying pressure. The SEC backed off. "Let FASB have the honor; they had the system" ([[berkshire-2002-letter]]). Of the S&P 500, 498 companies chose non-expensing. Options became a mechanism for management to receive compensation without that pay appearing as a cost — precisely what auditors were meant to prevent.

The underlying logical contradiction is stark: "If options aren't a form of compensation, what are they? If compensation isn't an expense, what is it? And, if expenses shouldn't go into the calculation of earnings, where in the world should they go?" ([[berkshire-1998-letter]]). The General Re cash-comp swap makes the argument concrete: replacing General Re's option plan with an economically identical cash plan would have increased reported 1997 compensation expense by $63 million. Same economics, vastly different appearance — and management preferred the appearance.

### Auditor limitations

Even outside the options question, auditor effectiveness is constrained. Insurance accounting illustrates a general principle: "Insurance accounting is a self-graded exam, in that the insurer gives some figures to its auditing firm and generally doesn't get an argument" ([[berkshire-2001-letter]]). Auditors cannot audit what management chooses to obscure, and auditors who push back risk losing the engagement.

Audit committee oversight is similarly limited: "audit committees can't audit." The committee's leverage is in asking pointed questions and making the auditor "afraid of misleading it." The four questions Buffett proposes for audit committees — covering whether the auditor would have done it differently, whether the financials are in plain English, whether internal audit matches the auditor's standards, and whether any period-shifting actions were taken — put the auditor on record before, not after, a problem emerges ([[berkshire-2002-letter]]).

## The structural fix: ownership, not rules

Governance failures are a consequence of directors not bearing sufficient financial risk. Rules about independence, committee composition, and separation of chair and CEO roles address surface features without touching the root cause.

Berkshire's approach as a structural model:
- Directors own significant Berkshire stakes — mostly families with hundreds of millions of dollars, purchased in the open market over decades, not granted.
- Director fees are deliberately nominal.
- No D&O liability insurance — directors bear real financial downside alongside shareholders. "You win, they win big; you lose, they lose big. Our approach might be called owner-capitalism" ([[berkshire-2003-letter]]).
- Business savvy is a minimum requirement. A director who cannot critically evaluate management proposals is "a potted plant" — it does not matter how distinguished the name on the letterhead.

The rental-car principle: "who ever washes a rental car?" ([[berkshire-2003-letter]]) — a board that holds nominal or granted stakes is managing someone else's property and will behave accordingly.

Beyond the board, the most powerful enforcement mechanism is institutional owner action. "Twenty, or even fewer, of the largest institutions, acting together, could effectively reform corporate governance at a given company, simply by withholding their votes for directors who were tolerating odious behavior" ([[berkshire-2002-letter]]). Rules written by regulators are less effective than large shareholders exercising existing voting rights with genuine purpose.
