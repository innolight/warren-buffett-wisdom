# Corporate Governance

**The set of structures, incentives, and norms that determine whether a company is run for its owners or its managers — and why formal rules almost always get this wrong.**

## Actionable takeaway

- **Test independence by treasure, not title.** Ask where each director's financial welfare actually lies. A director earning 20%+ of annual income from board fees has structural incentive to defer to management regardless of formal "independence" status. A director with a large personally purchased stake has treasure aligned with shareholders.
- **Focus on three questions.** Does the company have the right CEO? Is the CEO overreaching on compensation? Will acquisitions create or destroy per-share value? These are the only governance questions with real economic consequence. Governance that gets these right is good governance; governance that dodges them is theater.
- **Weight purchased ownership over committee composition.** Directors who bought large stakes in the open market and bear real downside predict board quality better than any audit-committee independence percentage or structural rule.
- **Treat M&A advisors as advocates, not analysts.** A bank whose fee depends on a deal closing cannot give disinterested advice. Insist on a financially motivated critic alongside any deal advocate.

## Definition

Corporate governance is the set of structures, incentives, and norms that determine whether a company is run in the interests of its owners or its managers. Buffett's observation across six decades and more than 20 boards: most governance failures are not the product of bad people but of structures that reliably produce misaligned incentives and punish candor.

## Why it matters

Governance quality is a risk multiplier. Under owner-aligned governance, capital is allocated to create value; under weak governance, management extracts value while presenting an appearance of stewardship. The pattern repeats across every era and regulatory regime — which is itself the central lesson: the mechanism operates through social pressure and financial incentives, not through deliberate bad faith, which is why rules written by regulators keep failing to fix it.

## Independence is social, not legal — treasure alignment is the only real test

Buffett's own admission crystallizes the mechanism: "Too often I was silent when management made proposals that I judged to be counter to the interests of shareholders. In those cases, collegiality trumped independence." ([berkshire-2002-letter](../sources/berkshire-2002-letter.md)) A well-mannered boardroom with a powerful executive driving the agenda makes challenging acquisitions, replacing a CEO, or resisting compensation recommendations socially costly. "Boardroom atmosphere almost invariably sedates their fiduciary genes." ([berkshire-2003-letter](../sources/berkshire-2003-letter.md))

The 62-year mutual-fund case study is the strongest evidence: federal law has mandated independent fund directors since 1940; the result has been "absolutely pathetic" stewardship — routine rehiring of incumbent managers regardless of performance and mindless approval of above-market fees. Statutory independence does not predict willingness to act.

The correct test is Matthew 6:21 — "For where your treasure is, there will your heart be also." The independence paradox is sharpest in the 2019 observation: "the director for whom fees are important — indeed, craved — is almost universally classified as 'independent'" under SEC rules, while a director with a substantial purchased stake is often classified as lacking independence. ([berkshire-2019-letter](../sources/berkshire-2019-letter.md))

## The three questions that actually matter

Most institutional shareholder activism focuses on checklist compliance and misses the only questions with real economic consequence ([berkshire-2004-letter](../sources/berkshire-2004-letter.md)):

1. **Does the company have the right CEO?**
2. **Is the CEO overreaching in terms of compensation?**
3. **Are proposed acquisitions more likely to create or destroy per-share value?**

On all three, the CEO's interests may diverge from shareholders', and directors sometimes lack the knowledge or gumption to act. Checklist governance that satisfies procedures without answering these questions produces the wrong outcome.

## The compensation ratchet

CEO pay has a structural upward bias. Consultants benchmark against peer groups; above-median grants become the new peer minimum; peers grant to match, raising the median. "There are two classes of clients you don't want to offend — actual and potential." ([berkshire-2003-letter](../sources/berkshire-2003-letter.md)) The ratchet runs only upward because benchmarking is disconnected from performance. "Ratchet, Ratchet and Bingo" is Buffett's label for the firms whose fee recommendations have one direction. For the full treatment — options non-expensing, the FASB/Senate capture, and the stock-option accounting history — see [incentive-compensation](incentive-compensation.md).

## The fee-dependent director: a structural deference trap

By 2019, director fees had escalated to $250,000–$300,000 for roughly six board meetings a year — three to four times the U.S. median household income. A non-wealthy director (NWD) hoping to add a second directorship can vault to $500,000–$600,000 annually. The Matthew 6:21 logic applies directly: "if the NWD has seriously challenged his/her present CEO's compensation or acquisition dreams, his or her candidacy will silently die." ([berkshire-2019-letter](../sources/berkshire-2019-letter.md))

The mechanism self-reinforces through director selection: CEOs informally vet candidates through their networks. "Good" in this context is a code word for a director who doesn't challenge or embarrass management. Pit bulls don't get rehired; cocker spaniels do. The result is a pleasant, agreeable, intelligent board — but not one optimized for critical business judgment. No villain required; it is a structural outcome.

## M&A advisory capture: "don't ask the barber"

No CEO presenting a desired acquisition has ever, in Buffett's experience, brought in an informed critic to argue against it. Advisors are hired by the company pursuing the deal; their fees depend on closing. "Don't ask the barber whether you need a haircut." ([berkshire-2019-letter](../sources/berkshire-2019-letter.md)) The current advisory system — whatever its shortcomings for shareholders — works magnificently for CEOs and the professionals who feast on deals.

## Two reforms that actually work

CEO-free director meetings remove the social pressure that makes challenge costly. Across multiple boards, Buffett observed this process would have led to more thorough scrutiny of dubious proposals and more prompt CEO changes in several cases — no downside; many possible benefits. A whistleblower line lets employees report directly to the audit committee; issues raised "are usually not of a type discoverable by audit, but relate instead to personnel and business practices." ([berkshire-2004-letter](../sources/berkshire-2004-letter.md))

## The fix: ownership first, rules second

Governance failures stem from directors not bearing sufficient financial risk. Rules about independence, committee composition, and CEO/chair separation address surface features without touching the root. The structural counter: directors with large stakes purchased in the open market, nominal fees, and no D&O liability insurance — so they bear real financial downside alongside shareholders. "Who ever washes a rental car?" ([berkshire-2003-letter](../sources/berkshire-2003-letter.md)) The most powerful external mechanism is large institutional shareholders exercising existing voting rights with genuine purpose — not proxy-advisory checklists.

## Risk control cannot be delegated

For financial institutions: "a board of directors of a huge financial institution is derelict if it does not insist that its CEO bear full responsibility for risk control. If he's incapable of handling that job, he should look for other employment." ([berkshire-2009-letter](../sources/berkshire-2009-letter.md)) Rules-based risk oversight — a Risk Committee, a Chief Risk Officer — fails for the same reason rules-based board independence fails: rules cannot substitute for a CEO whose personal financial exposure demands that the job be done correctly.
