# Bonds and Inflation

## The underlying principle

**A contract that fixes a price in one direction while costs move in the other destroys value over time.** Long-dated fixed-rate bonds are the canonical case: the lender locks in the price of money for decades while the purchasing power of that money erodes. The same logic applies to long-term supply contracts without escalators, fixed-wage employment contracts in inflationary periods, and any other arrangement that nails down one side of a relationship the other side won't hold still on.

For a lender, the asymmetry is total: no upside from improved borrower performance, full downside from inflation. For an insurance company deploying [float](float.md) into long bonds, the asymmetry compounds — the float advantage is given back to the bondholder's counterparty in real terms.

Buffett's compressed restatement, via Polonius: **"Neither a short-term borrower nor a long-term lender be."**

## The insurance-industry contradiction (the case Buffett built the principle around)

Buffett's clearest articulation came in 1979. The insurance industry, responding to inflation, was switching from one-year to six-month auto policies — on the grounds that pricing even 12 months forward had become too uncertain. Yet those same insurers took the premiums collected and bought 30-to-40 year bonds with them.

> Having decided that one year is too long a period for which to set a fixed price for insurance in an inflationary world, [insurers] then have turned around, taken the proceeds from the sale of that six-month policy, and sold the money at a fixed price for thirty or forty years.

The same managers refused to price one product 12 months forward while pricing a different product 30–40 years forward, apparently without noticing the contradiction. The structural pattern — see [institutional-imperative](institutional-imperative.md) — is what made the error invisible from inside the industry.

## Berkshire's response

Where bonds were held at all, Buffett favored:

- **Shorter-duration issues** with sinking funds
- **Convertibles** — the conversion option effectively shortens expected duration, and the equity upside partially offsets the inflation drag

Even with this caution, Buffett admitted in 1979 to owning some 15-year bonds — calling it a mistake. "You do not adequately protect yourself by being half awake while others are sleeping."
