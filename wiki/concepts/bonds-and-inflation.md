# Bonds and Inflation

## Definition

Long-term fixed-rate bonds are contracts that fix the price of money for 20–40 years. In an inflationary world where every other commercial contract has moved to floating rates, annual review, or indexing, the long-term bond is an anachronism — and a dangerous one for the buyer (lender).

Buffett's 1979 argument: "The very long-term bond contract has been the last major fixed price contract of extended duration still regularly initiated in an inflation-ridden world." While buyers of auto insurance, medical services, newsprint, and office space would be greeted with laughter if they asked for a fixed price through 1985, buyers of money could still lock in a price through 2010 or 2020.

## The insurance industry's contradiction

The 1979 letter identifies a precise irony. The insurance industry, responding to inflation, began switching from one-year to six-month auto insurance policies — on the grounds that pricing even 12 months forward had become too uncertain. Yet those same insurers took the premiums collected and invested them in 30–40 year bonds.

"Having decided that one year is too long a period for which to set a fixed price for insurance in an inflationary world, they then have turned around, taken the proceeds from the sale of that six-month policy, and sold the money at a fixed price for thirty or forty years."

This is Buffett at his most pointed: the same managers who claimed they couldn't price one product 12 months forward were simultaneously pricing a different product 30–40 years forward, apparently without noticing the contradiction.

## Berkshire's bond policy

By 1979, Berkshire's insurance subsidiaries had not been net purchasers of straight long-term bonds for several years. Where bonds were held, Buffett favored:
- **Shorter-duration issues** with sinking funds
- **Convertible bonds** — the conversion option effectively creates a shorter expected duration (the bond can be terminated via conversion at an appropriate time), and the equity upside partially offsets the inflation drag

Even with this caution, Buffett admits owning some 15-year bonds as a mistake: "You do not adequately protect yourself by being half awake while others are sleeping."

His summary principle, restating Polonius: **"Neither a short-term borrower nor a long-term lender be."**

## Why it matters

The bond-inflation insight connects to a broader pattern in Buffett's thinking: **contracts that fix prices in one direction while costs move in the other destroy value over time**. This applies to:
- Long-term employment contracts with fixed wages in an inflationary environment
- Long-term supply contracts without escalation clauses
- And most acutely: long-term bonds

For an insurance company relying on [[float]], bond policy is not peripheral — it is the mechanism by which the float is deployed. A float-funded bond portfolio that loses purchasing power to inflation negates the competitive advantage of the float itself.
