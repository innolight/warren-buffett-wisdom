# Bonds and Inflation

**In an inflationary world, locking in the price of money for decades — while purchasing power erodes — is a one-way bet against yourself: no upside from the borrower's success, full downside from real-value loss.**

## Actionable takeaway

- **Avoid long-dated fixed-rate bonds when inflation is materially uncertain.** The lender's asymmetry is total: no participation in borrower upside, unlimited exposure to purchasing-power erosion.
- **Test every fixed-price contract for escalation protection.** Wherever costs can move but your price is nailed, the fix works against you — not just in bonds, but in supply contracts, fixed-wage agreements, or any arrangement that locks one side while the other floats freely.
- **Spot the duration mismatch.** An insurer that prices its liability at 6 months but funds it with 30-year bonds is selling money far cheaper than it prices its own product. The inconsistency surfaces the asymmetry.
- **Never deploy float into long bonds expecting the cost advantage to survive.** The insurance float benefit is surrendered in real terms to the bondholder's counterparty.

## The mechanism: fixed price, eroding currency

A contract that fixes a price in one direction while costs move in the other destroys value over time. Long-dated fixed-rate bonds are the canonical case: the lender locks in the price of money for decades while inflation erodes the purchasing power of every coupon and the principal repaid at maturity.

The asymmetry is total: no participation in the borrower's upside, full exposure to real-value erosion. The same logic extends to long-term supply contracts without escalators, fixed-wage employment agreements in inflationary periods — any arrangement that nails one side of a transaction the other side won't hold still on. For an insurance company deploying [float](float.md) into long bonds, the damage compounds: the float advantage is surrendered in real terms to the bondholder's counterparty.

Buffett's compressed formulation, via Polonius: **"Neither a short-term borrower nor a long-term lender be."**

## How the asymmetry blinds institutional managers

The clearest demonstration appeared in 1979. The insurance industry, spooked by inflation, was switching from annual to six-month auto policies — on the grounds that pricing even 12 months forward had become too uncertain. Yet those same managers took the premiums collected and bought 30-to-40-year bonds:

> Having decided that one year is too long a period for which to set a fixed price for insurance in an inflationary world, [insurers] then have turned around, taken the proceeds from the sale of that six-month policy, and sold the money at a fixed price for thirty or forty years.

They refused to price one product 12 months forward while pricing a different product 30–40 years forward — apparently without noticing the contradiction. The structural pull of convention (see [institutional-imperative](institutional-imperative.md)) made the error invisible from inside the industry.

## Partial remedies: duration compression and embedded equity optionality

When bond exposure was unavoidable, Buffett favored instruments that shorten effective duration or introduce equity participation:

- **Shorter-duration issues with sinking funds** — limiting the window over which inflation can compound against the lender.
- **Convertibles** — the conversion option caps duration in a positive scenario; the equity upside partially offsets inflation drag.

Neither remedy eliminates the asymmetry; both reduce the exposure window.
