# Incentive Compensation

**A compensation design is sound only if it charges managers fairly for the capital they use and pays them only for value they actually create — everything else transfers wealth from owners to managers as a byproduct of arithmetic.**

## Actionable takeaway

- **Treat option grants as a real compensation expense.** Subtract their economic cost from reported earnings; Buffett's rule of thumb is a 5–10% haircut at many companies. A company resisting expensing is signaling something about its governance.
- **Prefer companies whose managers hold purchased equity, not granted options.** Purchasers absorb carrying costs and downside identical to outside owners; option-holders bear no capital cost at all.
- **In any compensation plan, look for two features: a capital-charge (incremental capital must exceed a hurdle before bonuses accrue) and bailiwick scope (the manager is measured on the business she controls, not the enterprise).** Plans missing either feature reward the wrong things.
- **Treat option-repricing after a stock decline as a governance red flag** — a board willing to lower strike prices is transferring value from continuing shareholders to managers at the worst moment.

## Definition and why it matters

Incentive compensation is the design of managerial pay to align managers with owners. Done well, a plan rewards value actually created above the cost of capital used. Done badly, it extracts owner wealth through structural arithmetic — particularly through retained earnings — with no necessary link to skill. Compensation design is itself a form of capital allocation, and its failure modes affect both investment analysis (inflated reported earnings) and governance evaluation.

## The extraction mechanism: retained earnings as hidden owner cost

The core problem with standard fixed-price long-dated stock options is not generosity — it is that they let managers capture the value of *retained earnings they didn't create*.

The mechanism: a trustee retaining 75% of 8% annual interest grows an account 79% over ten years — every chart marches skyward — without adding any skill. A fixed-price option on that account rewards the trustee for arithmetic, not management. "Many stock options in the corporate world have gained in value simply because management retained earnings, not because it did well with the capital in its hands." The 2005 letters extend this into explicit arithmetic showing that a CEO can achieve triple-digit stock appreciation from a company whose underlying economics haven't changed at all, simply by withholding dividends and repurchasing shares.

The double standard: managers would never grant a ten-year fixed-price option on their business to an outside party — "ten months would be regarded as extreme." Yet they routinely grant precisely that to themselves. "No owner has ever escaped the burden of capital costs, whereas a holder of a fixed-price option bears no capital costs at all." The option holder's interest on dividend policy runs directly *against* the owner's: the option benefits from full retention, while a rational owner would prefer distributions.

## Three structural flaws — and their corrections

When options are used at all, three adjustments restore honest design:

1. **Scope.** Confine options to managers with company-wide responsibility. Sub-unit managers should be measured on their own bailiwick. "The .350 hitter expects, and also deserves, a big payoff even if he plays for a cellar-dwelling team. The .150 hitter should get no reward even if he plays for a pennant winner."
2. **Carrying-cost adjustment.** Build in a retained-earnings or capital-charge factor. Without it, the strike price fails to charge managers for capital withheld from owners — the option-holder is given for free what an owner pays for.
3. **True business value.** Strike at intrinsic business value, not a depressed market price. Managers who decry market prices as unreliable when fielding outside bids quietly use those same prices when granting themselves options. "Officers and directors sometimes consult the Tax Code to determine the lowest prices at which they can, in effect, sell part of the business to insiders."

## Positive design: capital-charge models that work

Three Berkshire arrangements illustrate the structural alternative.

**Capital charge with symmetric downside.** H.H. Brown's key managers earn a $7,800 base salary plus a percentage of profits *after a charge for capital employed* — they face the downside of capital waste as well as the upside of capital efficiency. The Schey–Scott Fetzer arrangement adds bailiwick scope: the bonus ties to Scott Fetzer results only; incremental capital earns a bonus above a hurdle and imposes a cost when it falls short. "In our book, alignment means being a partner in both directions, not just on the upside. Many 'alignment' plans flunk this basic test, being artful forms of 'heads I win, tails you lose.'"

**Long-duration scope.** GEICO's formula uses exactly two variables: policyholder growth and earnings on *seasoned* business (policies in force more than one year). First-year acquisition costs are excluded entirely, so associates are never penalized for writing profitable new customers. Berkshire absorbs year-one costs as an investment decision; the associate is measured on what she can actually control — long-duration customer value. "We want to have compensation policies that are both easy to understand and in sync with what we wish our associates to accomplish."

**Bonus on incremental earnings only.** The Forest River deal (Pete Liegl, 2024) takes $100,000 in salary plus 10% of earnings *above* the inherited run-rate, with an agreed adjustment for capital deployed in acquisitions. "If you lose money, so do we." The design ran unchanged for 19 years and encodes every feature the 1985 essay called for: bailiwick scope, incremental-only measurement, and symmetric capital-charge.

## The accounting fiction: options off the income statement

"If options aren't a form of compensation, what are they? If compensation isn't an expense, what is it? And, if expenses shouldn't go into the calculation of earnings, where in the world should they go?"

The practical proof: when General Re's option plan was replaced with an economically identical cash plan at Berkshire's acquisition, pro-forma compensation expense rose by $63 million with no change in the underlying economics of the pay. The cost was always there; accounting chose not to see it.

The political defeat is the cautionary institution story. Arthur Andersen's Professional Standards Group ruled that options were an expense; the firm's rainmaking partners overruled them. FASB voted 7-0 to require expensing; the U.S. Senate voted 88-9 against under CEO and company pressure; 498 of 500 S&P companies chose non-expensing. "Let FASB have the honor; they had the system." For investors, the practical implication: options non-expensing inflated reported earnings broadly through the 1990s. Berkshire's adjustment frequently cut per-share figures by 5%, with 10% not uncommon — enough to alter both portfolio decisions and acquisition valuations.

## Failure mode: comp-committee contagion

Compensation escalation is self-reinforcing. A board hires a consultant; the consultant surveys peer pay; the CEO earns a raise to median; the raised median becomes everyone's benchmark in the next round. "Ratchet, Ratchet and Bingo." No participant in a comp-committee negotiation has personal money at stake — neither option grants nor the resulting expense touches any negotiator's own finances. The structural corrective is purchased ownership, not formal independence. "If you don't know whose side someone is on, he's probably not on yours." Berkshire's counter-design: nominal director fees, no D&O insurance, and directors who hold large personally-purchased stakes — so family net worth is the dominant decision variable.

## The test: real ownership vs. option grants

Real ownership is the antidote to option repricing. At MiTek (2001), 55 managers invested at least $100,000 in cash for a collective 10% ownership stake. Purchase prices are permanent — no repricing possible; managers face downside exactly as shareholders do. "What they paid is what they live with." A CEO seeking repriced options after a stock decline has revealed his orientation: he views shareholders as patsies, not partners. The coinvestment model forces the manager to experience the business exactly as an outside owner does.
