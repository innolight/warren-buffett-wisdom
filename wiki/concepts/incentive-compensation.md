# Incentive Compensation

## Definition

A philosophy of managerial pay anchored on a simple test: **does the reward correspond to value the manager actually created, after charging fairly for the capital used?** Buffett's 1985 letter is the canonical articulation in this stretch — a sustained critique of the standard fixed-price ten-year stock option, followed by a description of Berkshire's alternative.

## Why it matters

Compensation design is itself a form of capital allocation. Poorly structured incentives — especially long-dated options struck at depressed market prices on businesses that retain most of their earnings — transfer wealth from owners to managers as a *byproduct of arithmetic*, with no necessary connection to managerial skill. Done well, incentive design aligns the manager with the disciplined owner; done badly, it inverts that alignment while pretending otherwise.

The framing matters beyond Berkshire because most public-company compensation programs of Buffett's era — and ours — exhibit at least one of the failure modes the 1985 essay identifies.

## The savings-account analogy

Buffett's central illustration. A trustee manages a $100,000 account at 8% interest, with the discretion to set the payout ratio. At a 25% payout over ten years:

- Account balance: $179,084 (up 79%).
- Annual earnings: $8,000 → $13,515 (up 69%).
- Annual "dividends": $2,000 → $3,378 (up 69%).
- Every chart marches skyward.

Now grant the trustee a ten-year fixed-price option on a portion of the account, struck at year-one fair value. The trustee captures substantial profit purely from retaining earnings — no skill, no carrying cost, no downside. "If he were both Machiavellian and a bit of a mathematician, your manager might also have cut the pay-out ratio once he was firmly entrenched."

The trick is that the *option's gain* requires no managerial value-add — only the workings of compound interest on retained earnings the owner already owned.

> Many stock options in the corporate world have worked in exactly that fashion: they have gained in value simply because management retained earnings, not because it did well with the capital in its hands.

## The double standard

> Leaving aside warrants… it is fair to say that nowhere in the business world are ten-year fixed-price options on all or a portion of a business granted to outsiders. Ten months, in fact, would be regarded as extreme.

Managers would never grant a ten-year fixed-price option on their business to an outside party. They grant precisely that to themselves and their colleagues. The option holder bears no capital cost; the owner always does. The option holder has no downside; the owner does.

> In fact, the business project in which you would wish to have an option frequently is a project in which you would reject ownership. (I'll be happy to accept a lottery ticket as a gift — but I'll never buy one.)

And the option holder's interests on dividend policy run *against* the owner's: the option benefits from full retention, while the owner of an account managed by an option-holding trustee would prefer 100% payout to deny the trustee participation in compounding.

## The three structural fixes

When options are used at all, three corrections:

1. **Scope.** Options are tied to overall corporate performance and should only go to managers with overall responsibility. Sub-unit managers should be paid against results in their own bailiwick.
   > The .350 hitter expects, and also deserves, a big payoff for his performance — even if he plays for a cellar-dwelling team. And the .150 hitter should get no reward — even if he plays for a pennant winner.
2. **Structure.** Build in a retained-earnings or carrying-cost factor — otherwise the strike fails to charge for capital the firm withholds from owners. The option-holder is given for free what an owner pays for.
3. **Pricing.** Strike at true business value, not depressed market price. Managers freely cite the unreliability of market prices when fielding outside bids — and then quietly use those same prices when granting options to themselves. "Officers and directors sometimes consult the Tax Code to determine the lowest prices at which they can, in effect, sell part of the business to insiders."

## The narrow defense of fixed-price options

Buffett's caveat: a small number of managers he admires have built genuine owner-oriented cultures using fixed-price options as the connective tissue. By their example and leadership, they have *taught* their managers to think like owners, and the option program is a tool in that teaching.

> "If it ain't broke, don't fix it" is preferable to "purity at any price."

In other words: the critique is not absolute; it is a critique of *indiscriminate* use, where the structural defects of the standard option dominate any cultural benefit.

## Berkshire's alternative

Six operating features:

1. **Targets local to the manager's bailiwick.** If See's does well, See's manager gets paid; the News manager does not. Vice versa.
2. **No coupling to Berkshire's stock price.** Operating performance is rewarded whether Berkshire stock rises, falls, or stays even.
3. **Performance is defined differently across units.** Tailwind businesses are held to one bar; headwind businesses to another. The owner-rower distinction from [[tailwinds-vs-headwinds]] is built into the comp design.
4. **No caps.** Top managers sometimes receive bonuses of 5× base salary or more; in 1985, one bonus could exceed $2M.
5. **Non-hierarchical.** A small-unit manager can earn more than a large-unit manager, depending on results.
6. **Seniority/age irrelevant.** "A 20-year-old who can hit .300 is as valuable to us as a 40-year-old performing as well."

The capstone: managers may use bonus money — or borrowed money — to buy Berkshire stock in the market. They become owners *by purchase*, not *by grant*. They bear the carrying cost. They have downside.

## The broader principle

Compensation should not transfer value *from* owners *to* managers as a default outcome of corporate plumbing. It should pay for value managers actually create, charged fairly against the capital used. The owner-orientation principles in [[owner-orientation]] are the underlying framework; this concept is what they look like when applied to pay design.

There is a kinship to [[share-repurchases]]: in both cases the right answer requires recognizing that retained earnings *are* capital, with a cost — and that managerial "achievements" funded purely by retention deserve no special applause.

## Notable quotes

> No owner has ever escaped the burden of capital costs, whereas a holder of a fixed-price option bears no capital costs at all.

> A managerial Rip Van Winkle, ready to doze for ten years, could not wish for a better "incentive" system.

> By accepting both the risks and the carrying costs that go with outright purchases, these managers truly walk in the shoes of owners.
