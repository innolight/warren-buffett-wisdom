# Tax Deferral

**Holding an appreciated position without realizing the gain is effectively borrowing the government's future tax bill at zero interest — and that loan compounds in the investor's favor for as long as you hold.**

## Actionable takeaway

- **Never sell simply to redeploy.** Every realization converts the government's interest-free loan into an immediate payment. The hurdle for any sale is not "can I find a better business?" but "is the after-tax improvement large enough to clear the realized drag?"
- **Calculate the after-tax cost of realization before selling.** A position that looks like a sideways switch on gross terms is a loss after tax. Run the math: what after-tax proceeds do you have to reinvest, and does the destination clear that bar?
- **Prefer ownership structures that defer indefinitely.** At ≥80% domestic subsidiary ownership, retained earnings compound within the consolidated entity without triggering capital gains — the same deferral logic built one level deeper into the ownership architecture.

## Definition

Deferred capital gains taxes function as an interest-free loan from the government that compounds in the investor's favor. Every year you hold an appreciated position without realizing the gain, you continue earning returns on capital that ultimately belongs to the tax authorities. Frequent realization destroys a large fraction of gross returns through tax drag; a single long-duration hold of the same underlying business captures most of those returns intact.

The structural principle holds at any positive tax rate and any positive return rate. The numbers scale; the logic does not change.

## Why it matters

Tax efficiency is one of the few reliable, structural edges available to any investor — individual or institutional. It requires no superior analytical skill, no proprietary information, and no special access. It requires only patience and a business worth holding.

Most return-enhancement strategies require outperforming other investors on the buying or selling decision. Tax deferral requires outperforming only the calendar: the longer the hold, the larger the government loan compounding on your behalf. Buffett's "favorite holding period is forever" is not sentiment. It is arithmetic.

## Frequent realization permanently shrinks the compounding base

The mechanism is exact: every year's tax payment permanently removes capital from the compounding stack. Berkshire's 1989 letter introduced the starkest formulation, comparing annual realization to a single exit tax on identical gross returns over the same holding period; the long-hold strategy delivers many times the after-tax wealth from timing alone. The 1993 Li'l Abner parable made the arithmetic vivid: twenty doublings of a dollar at 35% annual realization leaves roughly $22,000 after tax; a buy-and-hold strategy reaching 27½ doublings with one final 35% exit tax leaves roughly $130 million — more than 5,000× more ([berkshire-1993-letter](../sources/berkshire-1993-letter.md)). The extra doublings inflate the ratio beyond what tax timing alone would produce, but even a same-period comparison is dramatic. The structural point holds in any version: **the longer the deferral, the larger the compounding advantage, and every unnecessary realization is permanent.**

Buffett noted that he and Munger would follow a buy-and-hold policy "even if we ran a tax-exempt institution" — the tax savings are the surplus on top of a principle already justified by business logic.

## Unrealized gains are a non-callable government loan

When you hold a position with unrealized gains, you are effectively investing a portion of the government's eventual tax bill. The longer you hold, the larger the loan grows in absolute terms as gains compound, and the more compounding the government's share does on your behalf. The loan is interest-free and non-callable as long as you do not sell. Realization is the only trigger. Every unnecessary realization converts the loan into a payment — and that payment cannot be undone.

## Structural extension: ≥80% domestic ownership defers gains further

At 80% or greater ownership of a domestic subsidiary, US tax law (the dividends-received deduction) allows dividends to flow tax-free to the parent. Retained earnings within the subsidiary compound without generating capital gains until the subsidiary is eventually sold — extending the deferral benefit one level deeper into the ownership stack. This is part of why Berkshire seeks ≥80% ownership when acquiring whole companies: not merely control, but structural tax efficiency on subsidiary retained earnings. For the pricing and governance advantages of the same ownership threshold, see [passive-ownership-advantage](passive-ownership-advantage.md).
