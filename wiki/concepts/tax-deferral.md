# Tax Deferral

## Definition

Deferred capital gains taxes function as an interest-free loan from the government that compounds in the investor's favor. Every year you hold an appreciated position without realizing the gain, you continue earning returns on capital that ultimately belongs to the tax authorities — not to you. Frequent realization destroys a large fraction of gross returns through tax drag; a single long-duration hold of the same underlying business captures most of those returns intact.

The structural principle holds at any positive tax rate and any positive return rate. The numbers scale; the logic does not change.

## Why it matters

Tax efficiency is one of the few reliable, structural edges available to any investor — individual or institutional. It requires no superior analytical skill, no proprietary information, and no special access. It requires only patience and a business worth holding.

Most return-enhancement strategies require outperforming other investors on the buying or selling decision. Tax deferral requires outperforming only the calendar: the longer the hold, the larger the government loan compounding on your behalf. Buffett's "favorite holding period is forever" is not sentiment. It is arithmetic.

## The arithmetic: two canonical examples

### 1989 — the Rip-Van-Winkle comparison

Berkshire's 1989 letter presents the starkest formulation. Start with $1 and double it 20 times:

| Strategy | Gross result | Tax treatment | After-tax result |
| --- | ---: | --- | ---: |
| Annual realization (35% each year) | $1,048,576 | 35% tax on each year's gain | ~$25,250 |
| Single long-hold position (35% at end) | $1,048,576 | 35% once, on exit | ~$692,000 |

The long-hold strategy delivers roughly **27× the after-tax wealth** of the annually-realized strategy on identical gross returns. The difference is entirely tax timing, not analytical skill.

Source: [berkshire-1989-letter](../sources/berkshire-1989-letter.md).

### 1993 — the Li'l Abner variation

The 1993 letter reprises the lesson through the fictional Dogpatch sage Old Man Mose. In this telling the numbers differ: the annual-realization scenario still uses 20 doublings at 35% annual tax (≈$22,370 after-tax); the single-position scenario uses 27.5 doublings with one final 35% tax (≈$130M after-tax). The headline is roughly **5,800× the after-tax wealth** for the long-hold strategy.

The two scenarios are not apples-to-apples — 27.5 doublings versus 20 doublings compounds the gap beyond what tax alone would produce. Buffett's structural point is nonetheless valid and vivid: frequent realization at even modest tax rates is devastating to long-run compounding. The 1993 math is illustrative by design, not a precise comparison.

Source: [berkshire-1993-letter](../sources/berkshire-1993-letter.md).

## The deferred-tax-as-loan framing

When you hold a position with unrealized gains, you are effectively investing a portion of the government's eventual tax bill. If you own $1M of stock with a $700K cost basis and a 35% capital gains rate, roughly $105K of your current market value belongs, in future-obligation terms, to the government. But until you sell, you earn returns on that $105K too.

The longer you hold, the larger the loan grows in absolute terms (as gains compound) and the more compounding the government's portion does on your behalf. The loan is interest-free and non-callable as long as you don't sell. Realization is the only trigger. Every unnecessary realization converts the loan into a payment.

## The 80%+ ownership structural extension

At 80% or greater ownership of a domestic subsidiary, US tax law (the dividends-received deduction) effectively allows dividends to flow tax-free to the parent. Retained earnings within the subsidiary compound without generating capital gains until the subsidiary is eventually sold — extending the deferral benefit into the subsidiary structure itself.

This is part of why Berkshire seeks 80%+ ownership of operating businesses when buying whole companies: not merely control, but the structural tax efficiency of treating the subsidiary's retained earnings as compounding within the consolidated entity rather than as taxable inter-company income. The same logic that makes long-hold equity positions tax-efficient applies one level deeper in the ownership stack.

Partly covered in [passive-ownership-advantage](passive-ownership-advantage.md), which addresses the pricing and governance advantages of Berkshire's ownership structure; the tax dimension is the incremental point here.
