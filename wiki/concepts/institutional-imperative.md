# Institutional Imperative

## Definition

The tendency of corporate managements to follow what their peers do — even when it is irrational — and the corresponding difficulty of acting counter-institutionally. Institutions tend to drift toward the center of the herd: writing unprofitable business rather than ceding volume, buying the same assets as peers, reserving at industry-average levels to avoid standing out. Individual rationality is systematically overridden by social and competitive pressure.

The formal name comes later; the concept is operational in the 1977–1979 letters, always via insurance as the setting.

## Three forms in the 1970s letters

**1. Soft-market volume discipline (1977–1979).**
The hardest thing in insurance is letting the other fellow take business at foolish prices. "Unusual managerial discipline will be required, as it runs counter to normal institutional behavior to let the other fellow take away business — even at foolish prices." (1977) By 1979, Buffett observes that "we hear a great many insurance managers talk about being willing to reduce volume in order to underwrite profitably, but we find that very few actually do so." Phil Liesche at National Indemnity is named as a genuine exception.

**2. Reinsurance self-delusion (1978).**
Long-tail casualty reinsurance allows years of undetected underwriting losses. "It is very easy to fool yourself regarding underwriting results in reinsurance (particularly in casualty lines involving long delays in settlement)." When major market participants don't know their true costs, the competitive fallout hits everyone — including disciplined operators. The collective failure to reserve honestly suppresses industry-wide rate levels.

**3. Writing at a loss for investment income (1979).**
High interest rates create a new form of the imperative: underwriting at a loss becomes acceptable if investment income from float compensates. Buffett predicts this will establish "a new threshold of tolerance for underwriting losses" and that combined ratios will average higher in the future than in the past. The irrationality is institutional: each company follows the others into an unprofitable equilibrium.
