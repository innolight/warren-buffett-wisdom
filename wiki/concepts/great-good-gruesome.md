# Great, Good, and Gruesome Businesses

A three-tier taxonomy that ranks businesses by what they earn on each incremental dollar of capital required to sustain their growth — extraordinary returns on a near-fixed base (great), adequate returns on an expanding base (good), or poor returns while capital is continuously demanded (gruesome).

## Actionable takeaway

- Before buying a growing business, calculate its **incremental return on capital**: how much additional capital does the business need, and what does it earn on each new dollar? Growth that requires heavy capital at mediocre returns destroys value; the revenue line does not reveal this.
- Apply the **savings-account test**: a great business compounds on a near-fixed base; a good business grows by accepting new deposits at decent rates; a gruesome business forces deposits at inadequate rates. Identify which account you are opening before you buy.
- Prefer businesses where earnings grow *without* proportional capital commitment; accept those where reinvestment earns solid returns; avoid businesses where reinvestment is mandatory and returns remain poor.
- Apply the **endurance and manager-independence tests** before assigning "great": a moat that must be continuously rebuilt is not a moat, and a business whose results depend on a superstar manager is not institutionally great — that moat belongs to the individual, not the enterprise.

## Definition

Introduced in the [2007 Berkshire letter](../sources/berkshire-2007-letter.md), the framework is Buffett's own savings-account analogy:

> "Think of three types of 'savings accounts.' The great one pays an extraordinarily high interest rate that will rise as the years pass. The good one pays an attractive rate of interest that will be earned also on deposits that are added. Finally, the gruesome account both pays an inadequate interest rate and requires you to keep adding money at those disappointing returns."

The organizing question is: *what does this business earn on each incremental dollar of capital required to produce its growth?*

## Why it matters

The framework corrects a common confusion: **growth is not inherently good.** A business that grows rapidly by consuming capital at mediocre returns destroys value while appearing to succeed. The relevant question is not "does this business grow?" but "what does it earn on the capital that growth requires?"

The taxonomy also sharpens the relationship between [moat](moat.md) and capital structure. A great business needs both: durable competitive advantage *and* a structure where growth consumes minimal additional capital. A moated business that must constantly reinvest to defend its position is good at best.

## Great: earnings compound on a near-fixed capital base

[See's Candies](../entities/sees-candies.md) is the definitive proof. Bought for $25M in 1972, it generated $1.35B in cumulative pre-tax earnings over 35 years on only $32M of incremental capital. The ratio is the point: extraordinary earnings without needing to reinvest them to produce the next year's earnings. Every dollar earned is available for redeployment elsewhere — Berkshire's model. "Just as Adam and Eve kick-started an activity that led to six billion humans, See's has given birth to multiple new streams of cash for us."

The structural reasons: sold for cash (no receivables), short production cycle (minimal inventory), sustained pricing power that outpaces inflation, and no fixed-asset growth required. The business genuinely does not need more capital; that is what makes it great.

## Good: growth earns adequately but reinvestment is mandatory

[FlightSafety](../entities/flightsafety.md) is the good-business case. Real competitive advantages — the capital-intensive simulator fleet is itself a barrier to entry; pilot training demand is non-discretionary by regulation — produce solid returns. But a large fraction of earnings must go back into the business as aircraft models evolve and each new simulator costs millions. "There is nothing shabby about earning $82 million pre-tax on $400 million of net tangible assets." The economics are fundamentally different from See's: a dollar of reinvestment earns a respectable return, but reinvestment is not optional.

## Gruesome: poor returns compound alongside forced capital demands

The airline industry is the archetype. Enormous capital requirements — fleets, gates, maintenance, labor — combined with commodity economics (most passengers choose on price) prevent pricing power. "Investors have poured money into a bottomless pit, attracted by growth when they should have been repelled by it." A durable competitive advantage has proven elusive since the Wright Brothers. Buffett's self-deprecating admission: Berkshire bought U.S. Air preferred stock in 1989; the exit in 1998 was lucky — U.S. Air went bankrupt twice in the following decade.

## The moat must be institutional and enduring

A great business requires not just a current competitive advantage but an *enduring* one: "A moat that must be continuously rebuilt will eventually be no moat at all." It must also be *institutional* — a business whose great results depend on a superstar manager does not have a great business. The brain surgeon's practice earns outsized returns while the surgeon is there; the Mayo Clinic's moat survives any individual. Institutional moats compound across leadership transitions; manager-dependent advantages walk out the door. See [moat](moat.md) for the full typology of structural moat forms and failure modes.
