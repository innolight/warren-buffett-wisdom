# Great, Good, and Gruesome Businesses

## Definition

A three-tier taxonomy for business quality introduced in the 2007 Berkshire letter, organized as three types of savings accounts:

- **Great:** pays an extraordinarily high interest rate that *rises* as years pass. Minimal reinvestment required; earnings compound on a near-fixed capital base.
- **Good:** pays an attractive rate of interest that is earned also on new deposits added. Solid returns, but growth requires committing additional capital.
- **Gruesome:** pays an inadequate interest rate *and* requires you to keep adding money at those disappointing returns. Capital is consumed; returns remain poor.

## Why it matters

The framework corrects a common confusion: **growth is not inherently good.** A business that grows rapidly and requires significant capital to sustain that growth may destroy value if returns on that capital are inadequate. The relevant question is not "does this business grow?" but "what does it earn on each incremental dollar of capital required to produce that growth?"

The taxonomy also clarifies the relationship between [[moat]] and capital efficiency. A great business needs both: a durable competitive advantage that protects high returns, *and* a structure where growth consumes minimal additional capital. A business with a moat but requiring constant reinvestment to defend it is good at best. Without a moat, competitive dynamics guarantee returns normalize toward the cost of capital — gruesome territory.

## The three archetypes

### Great: See's Candies

[[sees-candies]] is the definitive great-business case study. Bought for $25M in 1972 when sales were $30M and pre-tax earnings were less than $5M; tangible capital then required was $8M. By 2007:

| Metric | 1972 | 2007 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Sales | $30M | $383M |
| Pre-tax earnings | <$5M | $82M |
| Tangible capital required | $8M | $40M |
| **Incremental capital invested (35 years)** | — | **$32M** |
| **Cumulative pre-tax earnings** | — | **$1.35B** |

The ratio is the point: $1.35 billion of earnings on $32 million of incremental capital over 35 years. See's generates extraordinary returns without needing those returns reinvested to produce the next year's earnings. Buffett: "Just as Adam and Eve kick-started an activity that led to six billion humans, See's has given birth to multiple new streams of cash for us."

This is structurally possible because: (1) the product is sold for cash — no receivables; (2) production cycle is short — minimal inventory; (3) pricing power consistently outpaces inflation while pound volume stays roughly flat; and (4) no fixed-asset growth is required. The capital sits near $40M because the business genuinely does not need more.

### Good: FlightSafety

[[flightsafety]] is the good-business case study. Acquired in 1996; pre-tax earnings then $111M on $570M net fixed assets. By 2007:

| Metric | Since 1996 |
| --- | ---: |
| Depreciation charged | $923M |
| Capex required | $1.635B |
| Net fixed assets (2007) | $1.079B |
| Pre-tax earnings growth | +$159M |
| Incremental capital invested | ~$509M |

The incremental return — $159M in added earnings on $509M of capital — is a solid ~31%. FlightSafety has real competitive advantages (the capital-intensive simulator fleet is itself a barrier to entry; demand is non-discretionary by regulation) and delivers good returns. But those returns require ongoing capital. Unlike See's, a large fraction of earnings must go back into the business as aircraft models evolve and each new simulator costs $12M or more.

The contrast with See's is not a condemnation. "There is, to follow through on our example, nothing shabby about earning $82 million pre-tax on $400 million of net tangible assets." But the economics are fundamentally different: a dollar of reinvestment earns a respectable but not extraordinary return.

### Gruesome: Airlines

The airline industry is the gruesome archetype. Since the Wright Brothers, airlines have demanded enormous capital — fleets, gates, maintenance, labor — while collectively earning poor returns on that capital. "A durable competitive advantage has proven elusive ever since the days of the Wright Brothers."

The structural problem: airlines are commodity businesses (most passengers choose on price) that require sustained capital investment just to maintain competitive position. "Investors have poured money into a bottomless pit, attracted by growth when they should have been repelled by it." Buffett's self-deprecating admission: "And I, to my shame, participated in this foolishness when I had Berkshire buy U.S. Air preferred stock in 1989." The exit was lucky — sold in 1998 for a gain during a brief burst of airline optimism. US Air went bankrupt twice in the following decade.

## The savings-account formulation

Buffett's own summary from [[berkshire-2007-letter]]:

> To sum up, think of three types of "savings accounts." The great one pays an extraordinarily high interest rate that will rise as the years pass. The good one pays an attractive rate of interest that will be earned also on deposits that are added. Finally, the gruesome account both pays an inadequate interest rate and requires you to keep adding money at those disappointing returns.

The formulation makes clear that the value of a great business is not just its current earnings yield, but the fact that it compounds without dilution. Every dollar earned is available for redeployment into other opportunities (Berkshire's model) rather than locked into maintaining the existing business. The great business generates a *widening* stream of distributable earnings; the gruesome business generates a *shrinking* claim on a growing asset base.

## The moat corollary

The great-business framework depends on an **enduring** moat — not just a current competitive advantage. "Our criterion of 'enduring' causes us to rule out companies in industries prone to rapid and continuous change. Though capitalism's 'creative destruction' is highly beneficial for society, it precludes investment certainty. A moat that must be continuously rebuilt will eventually be no moat at all."

The endurance criterion also rules out businesses whose results depend on a great manager. A superstar is a moat that goes when the manager goes. The brain surgeon's practice earns outsized returns while the surgeon is there; the Mayo Clinic's moat is institutional. The distinction matters for valuation: institutional moats command a premium; manager-dependent "moats" belong to the person, not the asset. See [[moat]].
