# Mistakes of Omission

**A failure to act on a high-conviction, within-competence situation — the costliest class of investment error, and the only one that leaves no trace in reported results.**

## Actionable takeaway

- Before passing on any situation *inside* your circle of competence, ask: will I be comfortable explaining this inaction in five years? Comfort with silence is the red flag.
- Size positions relative to conviction, not caution. Under-sizing a high-conviction idea is an omission too — the "I sort of bought it" error costs nearly as much as the full miss.
- When a stock rises after you've identified it as undervalued, that is not a signal to stop buying. It is the moment to re-examine conviction — not to trim.
- The boundary matters: passing on what is outside your competence is *not* an omission; it is sound judgment. The error is reserved for what you understand and fail to act on.

## Definition

Mistakes of omission are failures to act — to buy, to hold, or to fully size a position — on high-conviction situations within the investor's circle of competence. They are the opposite of mistakes of commission, which are actions that turn out to be wrong. The key qualifier, in Buffett's framing: the cost being measured is not missing what was outside one's competence — "It's no sin to miss a great opportunity outside one's area of competence" — but passing on what was inside it. ([berkshire-1989-letter](../sources/berkshire-1989-letter.md))

## Why omissions are systematically underweighted

Three forces compound the bias toward under-acting on high-conviction situations:

**No feedback signal.** A foregone gain has nowhere to land in financial statements. The investor who skipped a 100x compounder reports the same results as the investor who never saw it. No auditor flags the miss; no analyst asks about it in an earnings call.

**Asymmetric embarrassment.** A losing purchase becomes a visible story — a mark on the record. A missed buy is private. The institutional and reputational incentives point toward inaction: a manager rewarded only for visible results will never be called to account for the Coca-Cola they didn't buy enough of.

**Cognitive availability.** Visible losses dominate memory; foregone gains are abstract. The commission loss that stings $1M feels larger than the omission that cost $10M but left no trace.

## Four varieties of within-competence failure

**1. Not acting at all.** The 1989 letter formalized the category: "I have passed on a couple of really big purchases that were served up to me on a platter and that I was fully capable of understanding. For Berkshire's shareholders, myself included, the cost of this thumb-sucking has been huge." ([berkshire-1989-letter](../sources/berkshire-1989-letter.md))

**2. Under-sizing: stopping short of full conviction.** In early 1988, Buffett decided to buy 30 million shares of Fannie Mae — a $350–400M position he had owned before, understood well, and confirmed with management was welcome. After buying roughly 7 million shares, the price rose; he stopped and then sold what he held:

> I wish I could give you a halfway rational explanation for my amateurish behavior vis-a-vis Fannie Mae. But there isn't one. What I can give you is an estimate as of yearend 1991 of the approximate gain that Berkshire didn't make because of your Chairman's mistake: about $1.4 billion. ([berkshire-1991-letter](../sources/berkshire-1991-letter.md))

$1.4 billion, not a line on any financial statement. The Coca-Cola counter-case sits in the same letter: when Coke rose during Buffett's 1988–89 accumulation, he did not stop buying — and the position became Berkshire's most profitable holding. The contrast names the precise error: stopping because a stock has risen after you've identified it as a bargain.

**3. Premature exit: selling a winner too early.** Cap Cities produced two instances — an exit in 1978–80 and then, in 1993, the sale of 10 million shares at $63 per share, self-identified in the [berkshire-1994-letter](../sources/berkshire-1994-letter.md) as "being a repeat offender." By the time of the Disney merger in early 1996, those shares were worth approximately $1.27 billion — $635M in proceeds surrendered another $635M of gain. ([berkshire-1995-letter](../sources/berkshire-1995-letter.md))

**4. Failure to sell at the upside extreme.** The [berkshire-2003-letter](../sources/berkshire-2003-letter.md) named a mirror-image omission: not trimming major equity positions (Coca-Cola, American Express, Gillette, Washington Post) when they were "fully priced" at bubble valuations four years earlier. "If these stocks are fully priced now, you may wonder what I was thinking four years ago when their intrinsic value was lower and their prices far higher. So do I." The same contrarian discipline that commands patience when nothing is cheap should enforce selling when valuations are absurd.

## The circle-of-competence boundary

Not every missed gain is a mistake of omission. In the [berkshire-1999-letter](../sources/berkshire-1999-letter.md) — the year Berkshire's non-participation in the tech bubble looked, in hindsight, like a massive omission — Buffett drew the line: "Our problem — which we can't solve by studying up — is that we have no insights into which participants in the tech field possess a truly durable competitive advantage." The discipline is firm: "I don't understand" is not an omission. It is the correct application of circle-of-competence discipline. Passing on what is outside your competence is sound judgment; an omission requires that the opportunity was within your understanding and you chose not to act, or under-acted.

## Related but distinct: inaction on a recognized threat

A structurally related but distinct failure — failing to act on a *recognized problem* rather than a missed opportunity — has its own formulation in [noah-rule](noah-rule.md). The Gen Re Securities case (Buffett and Munger both knew the derivatives book was dangerous at the time of the 1998 merger; dithering cost ~$272M in an orderly wind-down) sits at the boundary of both concepts. ([berkshire-2003-letter](../sources/berkshire-2003-letter.md))

- [berkshire-1991-letter](../sources/berkshire-1991-letter.md) — the Fannie Mae case study; $1.4B cost quantified; Coca-Cola accumulation discipline as the explicit counter-case in the same letter
- [berkshire-1994-letter](../sources/berkshire-1994-letter.md) — Cap Cities sale of 10M shares at $63; self-identified as a repeat offense
