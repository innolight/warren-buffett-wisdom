# Passive-Return Crossbar

**The after-tax return on long-term passive investments (bonds or tax-exempts) is the crossbar equity capital must clear; when the crossbar rises above business ROE, owning a typical company produces less for the investor than a bond — even if the business is well-managed.**

## Actionable takeaway

- **Compute the crossbar before evaluating equities.** After-tax passive rate = long-bond yield × (1 − marginal tax rate), or current muni yield if tax-exempt. That number is the floor any equity must beat to produce real value-added.
- **In high-rate environments, most equities fail the test.** When long-term bonds yield 14–16%, only businesses earning well above average ROE remain better than passive. Index-level heuristics (historic P/E, book-value multiples) obscure this because the yardstick is wrong.
- **Weight toward economic-goodwill businesses.** Companies with minimal tangible-capital requirements can clear a rising crossbar; capital-intensive, low-ROE businesses cannot. See [economic-goodwill](economic-goodwill.md).
- **Treat disguised distributions as a failure signal.** Dividend-reinvestment plans with discount features, and stock-funded dividends ("selling shares to Peter to pay Paul"), signal that a business cannot clear the crossbar and is masking the fact.

## Definition

The crossbar is the after-tax passive rate of return an investor could earn by doing nothing but holding long-term bonds or tax-exempts. For equity capital to add value, a business must beat that rate after applying both the corporate and the individual investor's tax layers.

When the crossbar rises faster than business ROE, equity capital produces no aggregate value-added — even if business operations are improving. "It should be stressed that this depressing situation does not occur because corporations are jumping, economically, less high than previously. In fact, they are jumping somewhat higher: return on equity has improved a few points in the past decade. But the crossbar of passive return has been elevated much faster." ([berkshire-1981-letter](../sources/berkshire-1981-letter.md))

## Why it matters

The crossbar reframes "is this equity overvalued?" into a relative question with no equity-market quirks required. Two variables determine whether equities add real value:

1. **Business ROE** — improves marginally over decades but is bounded by competition and capital intensity.
2. **After-tax passive rate** — driven by inflation expectations and tax law; can move far faster than business ROE.

When (2) exceeds (1), most American businesses produce less for their owners than a bond. The market is slow to recognize this because investors and managers anchor to historic P/E ratios and book-value yardsticks: "It is much easier for investors to utilize historic p/e ratios or for managers to utilize historic business valuation yardsticks than it is for either group to rethink their premises daily."

## When the crossbar dominates: the high-rate case

The 1981 contrast is sharp. Historically: long-term taxable bonds 5%, tax-exempts 3%, business ROE as little as 10%, stocks at ~1.5× book — equities clearly above the crossbar. By 1981: taxable bonds exceeded 16%, tax-exempts 14%; American business earned ~14% on equity. For a 50%-bracket investor taking full dividends, that 14% pre-tax becomes roughly a 7% tax-exempt equivalent — equities **below** the crossbar for most U.S. corporations.

Buffett's conclusion: most American businesses in 1981 were "bad" businesses economically — not because they had become worse operators, but because passive money had become a better option. Berkshire itself, with a historic 21% return, continued to clear the crossbar — "but barely."

## Inflation as the corporate tapeworm

High inflation forces low-return businesses to retain earnings even when retention is destructive. "Inflation acts as a gigantic corporate tapeworm. That tapeworm preemptively consumes its requisite daily diet of investment dollars regardless of the health of the host organism. Whatever the level of reported profits (even if nil), more dollars for receivables, inventory and fixed assets are continuously required by the business in order to merely match the unit volume of the previous year."

A rational bondholder holding a worthless 5% instrument does not reinvest coupons at 5%. The equity investor has no symmetric option — the business conscripts earnings to maintain unit volume regardless of opportunity cost.

The result inverts the parable of the talents: high-return businesses should retain; low-return businesses should distribute. Inflation forces the opposite, compounding owners' losses in real terms.

## The disguises

When low-return businesses cannot honestly pay dividends, they often disguise the constraint:

- **Dividend reinvestment plans** — sometimes with discount features that all but force shareholders to reinvest.
- **New share issuances to fund dividend payments** — "selling newly issued shares to Peter in order to pay dividends to Paul."

Buffett's warning: "Beware of 'dividends' that can be paid out only if someone promises to replace the capital distributed."
