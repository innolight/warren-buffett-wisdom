# Passive-Return Crossbar

## The principle

The economic case for owning equities rests on the premise that combining capital with managerial and entrepreneurial skill produces returns above what passive investments (long-term bonds) yield. The hurdle the equity must clear — the **crossbar** — is the after-tax passive rate of return available to the same investor.

When the crossbar rises faster than business ROE, equity capital can produce *no value-added* on average — even though business ROE may have stayed flat or improved. "It should be stressed that this depressing situation does not occur because corporations are jumping, economically, less high than previously. In fact, they are jumping somewhat higher: return on equity has improved a few points in the past decade. But the crossbar of passive return has been elevated much faster." ([berkshire-1981-letter](../sources/berkshire-1981-letter.md))

## Why it matters

The crossbar reframes the question of "is the equity market overvalued?" into a relative question that doesn't depend on equity-market quirks at all. Two things determine whether equities produce real value-added for individual investors:

1. **Where business ROE lands.** Has improved slightly over decades but is bounded.
2. **Where the after-tax passive rate lands.** Driven by inflation expectations and tax law.

When (2) > (1) in after-tax real terms, owning a typical American business produces less for the investor than parking in long-term tax-exempts. The investor's *individual* capital allocation responds to that; the *aggregate* market often does not respond quickly because of nostalgic anchoring to historic P/E and book-value yardsticks. "It is much easier for investors to utilize historic p/e ratios or for managers to utilize historic business valuation yardsticks than it is for either group to rethink their premises daily."

## The 1981 numbers

The numerical illustration in 1981 was sharp:

- **Historic comparison:** long-term taxable bonds 5%, tax-exempts 3%, American business ROE ~11%, stocks at ~1.5x book on average → equities clearly above the crossbar → "good" businesses dominant.
- **1981 conditions:** long-term taxable bonds >16%, tax-exempts >14%, American business ROE ~14% pre-tax-on-the-investor → after-tax to a 50% bracket holder ~7% (for full dividend payout) → equities **below** the crossbar for most U.S. corporations.

Buffett's conclusion: "American equity capital, in aggregate, produces no value-added for individual investors" at 1981 conditions. Most companies in 1981 were therefore "bad" businesses *not because they had become bad operators* but because passive money had become a better option.

## Inflation as the corporate tapeworm

The second-order pathology: high inflation forces low-return businesses to retain earnings even when retention is destructive to shareholders. "Inflation acts as a gigantic corporate tapeworm. That tapeworm preemptively consumes its requisite daily diet of investment dollars regardless of the health of the host organism. Whatever the level of reported profits (even if nil), more dollars for receivables, inventory and fixed assets are continuously required by the business in order to merely match the unit volume of the previous year."

A logical 5% bondholder, sitting with a worthless bond, does not reinvest coupons in more 5% bonds. But the shareholder of a 5%-ROE business in inflation has no symmetric option — the business itself conscripts the earnings to maintain unit volume, regardless of opportunity cost.

The parable-of-the-talents inversion: high-return businesses *should* retain (and do well by it); low-return businesses *should* distribute. Inflation forces the opposite — low-return businesses must retain to survive, compounding their owners' losses in real terms.

## The disguises

When low-return businesses cannot honestly pay dividends, they often disguise the constraint:

- **Dividend reinvestment plans** (sometimes with discount features) that all but force shareholders to reinvest.
- **New share issuances to fund dividend payments** — "selling newly issued shares to Peter in order to pay dividends to Paul."

Buffett's warning: "Beware of 'dividends' that can be paid out only if someone promises to replace the capital distributed."

## Implications

- A high after-tax passive rate is a *headwind* even for great companies and a *killer* for mediocre ones.
- The escape — the businesses that clear the crossbar even in adverse conditions — are those with enduring economic Goodwill and minimal tangible-capital reinvestment requirements. See [economic-goodwill](economic-goodwill.md).
- Berkshire itself, Buffett notes in 1981, "continues to clear the crossbar of after-tax passive return — but barely." The 21% historic ROE provides modest margin over current after-tax passive rates. The margin is not assured.
