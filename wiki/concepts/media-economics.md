# Media Economics

The economics of dominant local media — specifically newspapers — as the clearest case study of a distribution-based franchise forming, sustaining extraordinary returns, and becoming structurally obsolete when the distribution channel is technologically bypassed.

## Actionable takeaway

- Identify whether a franchise's moat is **demand-based** (customers attached to the product, brand, or switching cost) or **distribution-based** (a controlling position in the only viable delivery channel). Demand-based moats require competitors to change something in the customer's head or behavior; distribution moats require only that the channel be made technologically redundant.
- Apply the **technological bypass test** to any distribution-based franchise: if the underlying channel became free and universally available, would the competitive advantage survive? If not, the moat depends on technology remaining stable — a structural vulnerability, not a durable franchise.
- For any incumbent media or local-monopoly platform: a pay model behind comprehensive local coverage is the only viable digital defense. Giving the product away online while charging for print destroys the paid format without building a digital replacement.

## Definition

The economics of dominant local newspapers, used as a case study because the newspaper franchise offers the most fully documented arc from formation to collapse in the Buffett letters. The lesson generalizes to any business whose competitive advantage rests primarily on controlling a distribution channel rather than on product, brand, or switching costs.

## Why it matters

The newspaper is the purest historical example of a [franchise](franchise-vs-business.md) whose moat was distribution-based rather than demand-based. Buffett's analysis across three letters — 1991, 2006, 2012 — traces the full arc: franchise at peak power, early warning of erosion, structural collapse, and the viable remnant. No other case in the letters shows a dominant franchise destroyed not by competitive failure but by technological bypass of the moat's foundation.

## How the monopoly forms: two-sided market lock-in

For most of the 20th century, a dominant local newspaper possessed extraordinary franchise economics rooted in two interlocking features:

**Circularity.** Readers preferred the paper with the most content; advertisers preferred the paper with the most readers. This two-sided market resolved to a single dominant paper in each local market. Once one paper achieved critical mass, the lagging competitor's economics deteriorated until it closed or was absorbed — "survival of the fattest" ([berkshire-2012-letter](../sources/berkshire-2012-letter.md)). The survivor's pricing power was then effectively unrestricted: "For owners this was economic heaven." ([berkshire-2006-letter](../sources/berkshire-2006-letter.md))

**Bulletin-board essentiality.** The penetration ratio — share of households reading the paper daily — was the empirical signature of dominance and mattered more than editorial quality. Even a mediocre dominant paper retained essential value; local advertisers had no alternative of comparable reach. (See [buffalo-evening-news](../entities/buffalo-evening-news.md) for the canonical case; by the early 1980s it had achieved the highest weekday penetration among large dailies nationally.)

## How the moat erodes: pricing power gives way

By 1991, Buffett had concluded that secular forces — not cyclical weakness — were moving media across the line from franchise to business. Three pressures:

1. Advertising dollars growing slowly as alternatives proliferated.
2. Large retailers (Wal-Mart, direct mail) bypassing mass media entirely.
3. Print and electronic ad channels multiplying, fragmenting audiences and diluting pricing leverage.

The diagnosis: "Gone are the days of bullet-proof franchises and cornucopian economics." ([berkshire-1991-letter](../sources/berkshire-1991-letter.md))

This was partial erosion, not existential collapse. The delivery infrastructure and local advertiser monopoly were still intact; pricing power had narrowed. For the valuation consequences of reclassifying a franchise to a business, see [franchise-vs-business](franchise-vs-business.md).

## How the moat collapses: distribution becomes free

The 2006 letter is the obituary. The mechanism differs from 1991 and the difference matters: cable, satellite, and the internet did not merely offer more advertising alternatives — they made information distribution free. Classified advertising, the most profitable revenue stream for most papers, migrated to Craigslist at zero marginal cost. Readers got sports scores, stock prices, and breaking news instantly, for free, from sources with global reach.

The thought experiment that clarifies the logic: "Simply put, if cable and satellite broadcasting, as well as the internet, had come along first, newspapers as we know them probably would never have existed." ([berkshire-2006-letter](../sources/berkshire-2006-letter.md))

The newspaper was not a great product delivered on paper; it was a **delivery-channel monopoly** that happened to carry content. When distribution became free, the moat was not eroded gradually — it was bypassed entirely. The Buffalo News in 2006 was the highest-penetration large daily in the country, run by an excellent publisher — but operational excellence was not in question: "fundamentals are definitely eroding in the newspaper business… The skid will almost certainly continue." An online version cannot reconstitute the original moat; the internet dissolves geographic lock-in, concentrating online readership at national scale rather than local.

## The viable remnant: local community monopoly

The 2012 letter qualifies the collapse: national primacy fell; local primacy did not.

Newspapers failed as first-to-know sources for sports scores, financial quotations, national politics, and job listings — categories where internet sources are faster, cheaper, and global. But for community-specific information, no national platform has replaced the local paper: "If you want to know what's going on in your town — whether the news is about the mayor or taxes or high school football — there is no substitute for a local newspaper that is doing its job." ([berkshire-2012-letter](../sources/berkshire-2012-letter.md))

Local news requires local reporters, local relationships, local knowledge — resources no national platform will build at community scale. "Wherever there is a pervasive sense of community, a paper that serves the special informational needs of that community will remain indispensable to a significant portion of its residents."

**The pay model is the only viable digital strategy.** Offering the paper free online while charging for print was structurally self-defeating. The Arkansas Democrat-Gazette adopted a pay format early: "over the past decade his paper has retained its circulation far better than any other large paper in the country." ([berkshire-2012-letter](../sources/berkshire-2012-letter.md)) Skimpy coverage produces skimpy readership; the formula is comprehensive local coverage behind a paywall.

## The transferable test: what kind of moat?

The newspaper case proves that distribution-channel moats are structurally fragile to technological bypass. The diagnostic question for any apparent franchise: would this business's competitive advantage survive if its delivery channel became free and universally available? See [moat](moat.md) for the full moat taxonomy and [franchise-vs-business](franchise-vs-business.md) for the valuation consequences of reclassification from franchise to business.
