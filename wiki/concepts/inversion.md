# Inversion

## Definition

Approaching a problem by asking what to *avoid* rather than what to pursue. The intellectual credit goes to Carl Jacobi, the Prussian mathematician who counseled "Invert, always invert" as a problem-solving method. Charlie Munger adopted it as a core mental model; Buffett credits Munger with its systematic application at Berkshire.

Munger's formulation, from the 2009 letter:

> All I want to know is where I'm going to die, so I'll never go there.

## Why it matters

Most decision-making frameworks focus on optimizing: selecting the best option from a known set. Inversion is prior to optimization — it first defines what would guarantee failure, then builds a structure that makes those outcomes impossible. This approach is especially powerful where:

- The positive case is hard to specify but failure modes are clear
- Decisions are irreversible, making catastrophic bad outcomes more costly than forgone upside
- Institutional forces systematically push toward error (see [institutional-imperative](institutional-imperative.md))

The positive strategy — "invest in predictable businesses, maintain financial strength, trust your managers, attract long-term partners" — emerges automatically once the failure modes are excluded. The avoidances do the work; the aspiration follows.

## The "What We Don't Do" framework (2009)

The 2009 letter is the canonical application of inversion to Berkshire's operating principles. Four avoidances, each the inverse of a specific failure mode:

**1. Avoid businesses whose futures can't be evaluated.**

> Just because Charlie and I can clearly see dramatic growth ahead for an industry does not mean we can judge what its profit margins and returns on capital will be as a host of competitors battle for supremacy.

The failure mode: committing capital to an exciting industry without being able to forecast the unit economics under competition. Identifying dramatic growth (autos in 1910, aircraft in 1930, televisions in 1950) is not enough — the relevant judgment is whether returns will remain after competition arrives. Where that judgment is unavailable, pass. See [circle-of-competence](circle-of-competence.md).

**2. Never become dependent on the kindness of strangers.**

The failure mode: running out of options at the worst possible moment. The solution is a permanent liquidity reserve large enough that any conceivable cash need is dwarfed by what Berkshire holds. The cost is foregone yield on idle cash; the benefit is the ability to act (and survive) through crises that destroy less liquid competitors. See [liquidity-fortress](liquidity-fortress.md).

**3. Don't supervise subsidiaries to death.**

> We would rather suffer the visible costs of a few bad decisions than incur the many invisible costs that come from decisions made too slowly — or not at all — because of a stifling bureaucracy.

The failure mode: excessive oversight killing the cultural engine that makes subsidiaries excellent. The invisible costs of slow decisions and demoralized managers exceed the visible costs of occasional errors by autonomous ones. The solution is not monitoring everything — it's hiring the right people and getting out of their way. See [owner-orientation](owner-orientation.md).

**4. Don't woo Wall Street.**

The failure mode: attracting short-term investors who push management toward short-term behavior. Building a compatible shareholder base requires *not* trying to appeal to every category of investor. Selective disengagement from conventional investor-relations activity is deliberate policy, not an oversight.

## Inversion as a meta-strategy

The four avoidances are not independent rules — they reflect a single logic: **define the conditions that reliably produce bad outcomes, then build a structure that makes those conditions impossible.**

Bad outcomes come from:
- Committing capital to futures you can't evaluate (Rule 1)
- Running out of options at the wrong moment (Rule 2)
- Killing the cultural engine that runs your subsidiaries (Rule 3)
- Attracting shareholders who push you toward short-termism (Rule 4)

Each rule inverts a failure mode. This is why Berkshire's operating principles are easier to state as negatives than as positives: the negatives are the actual load-bearing structure.

## The country-song corollary

Buffett's illustration of inversion at its most literal: "Sing a country song in reverse, and you will quickly recover your car, house and wife." The joke distills the method — invert the disaster and you recover the original good state.
