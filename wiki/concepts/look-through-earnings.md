# Look-Through Earnings

**The pro-rata share of a partial equity stake's total earnings — dividends plus retained earnings — that accrues to the partial holder. Buffett's term for economic earnings as distinct from accounting earnings.**

## Actionable takeaway

- When evaluating a partial stake, compute look-through earnings: reported operating earnings + proportionate share of investees' retained earnings − incremental tax on those retained earnings. The dividend alone is not your economic yield.
- Test the quality of retained earnings before relying on them: if the investee's management earns high returns on capital, retained earnings compound in your favor; if management deploys capital poorly, retention destroys value. Capital allocation quality is the multiplier.
- Track ownership-percentage changes when an investee repurchases shares. Buybacks silently enlarge your proportionate claim on future earnings at no additional outlay — GAAP ignores this; a long-term owner should not.
- Resist equating a low dividend yield with low economic return. A partial stake in a high-ROC business that retains 90% of earnings may be compounding intrinsic value far faster than a high-dividend stock that shows up noisily in reported income.

## Definition

Look-through earnings are the full economic earnings attributable to a partial equity position. GAAP records only dividends received below a 20% ownership threshold; look-through earnings include the complete proportionate share — dividends plus retained earnings. The retained portion is not lost; it compounds inside the business and accrues to the partial holder's intrinsic value even without appearing in reported income.

Buffett's formal label: **economic earnings** vs. **accounting earnings** ([berkshire-1982-letter](../sources/berkshire-1982-letter.md)). For a partial owner in an excellent business that retains earnings and reinvests them at high returns, the GAAP-to-economic gap is not an accounting error but a structural feature — and usually a benefit. Berkshire set its primary intrinsic-value benchmark as 15% annual look-through-earnings growth ([berkshire-1989-letter](../sources/berkshire-1989-letter.md)).

## Retained earnings compound for the part-owner whether or not received

A partial owner's claim on retained earnings is real even when those earnings never appear as income. "If a tree grows in a forest partially owned by us, but we don't record the growth in our financial statements, we still own part of the tree" ([berkshire-1980-letter](../sources/berkshire-1980-letter.md)).

The intellectual foundation was stated by John Maynard Keynes in his 1924 review of Edgar Lawrence Smith's *Common Stocks as Long Term Investments*:

> "Well-managed industrial companies do not, as a rule, distribute to the shareholders the whole of their earned profits. In good years, if not in all years, they retain a part of their profits and put them back into the business. Thus there is an element of compound interest (Keynes' italics) operating in favour of a sound industrial investment. Over a period of years, the real value of the property of a sound industrial is increasing at compound interest, quite apart from the dividends paid out to the shareholders."

Buffett surfaced this genealogy in [berkshire-2019-letter](../sources/berkshire-2019-letter.md) to underscore that look-through earnings are not a financial abstraction — they describe the oldest documented mechanism of equity wealth creation.

## The quality of retained earnings depends entirely on management's reinvestment

A well-run company's retained earnings may be worth more than 100 cents on the dollar to the minority holder because management redeploys them at high returns on capital. A poorly-run company's retained earnings may be worth far less. "The value of those retained earnings is determined by the use to which they are put… It's the act that counts, not the actors" ([berkshire-1980-letter](../sources/berkshire-1980-letter.md)).

This is the look-through investor's central test: before trusting retained earnings as an asset, verify that the capital allocator is excellent. The answer determines whether the look-through figure is compounding for you or being frittered away. See [acquisition-criteria](acquisition-criteria.md) — criterion 3 (honest and competent management) is what the test resolves.

## GAAP creates a systematic blind spot for economic ownership

GAAP records only dividends from sub-20% stakes — even when the retained portion is far larger. In 1982, GEICO paid Berkshire $3.5M in after-tax dividends while Berkshire's attributable share of GEICO's retained earnings was roughly $23M. "If GEICO had earned less money in 1982 but had paid an additional $1 million in dividends, our reported earnings would have been larger despite the poorer business results… Clearly 'accounting' earnings can seriously misrepresent economic reality" ([berkshire-1982-letter](../sources/berkshire-1982-letter.md)).

By 1980, the retained portion of Berkshire's investee earnings exceeded total reported Berkshire operating earnings — most of the iceberg was below the accounting waterline. The structural asymmetry recurs at scale: in 2011, the Big Four investees (AmEx, [Coca-Cola](../entities/coca-cola.md), IBM, Wells Fargo) paid Berkshire $862M in dividends while Berkshire's attributable share of their total earnings was $3.3B ([berkshire-2011-letter](../sources/berkshire-2011-letter.md)).

## Market realization of look-through value is irregular but reliable over time

Retained earnings translate to market price eventually, but on no fixed schedule. Shareholders collect look-through value "in an auction market where prices are set by participants with behavior patterns that sometimes resemble those of an army of manic-depressive lemmings" ([berkshire-1982-letter](../sources/berkshire-1982-letter.md)). In any given year, look-through value may not be recognized at all; over long holding periods across well-selected businesses, the translation has been reliable — which is why look-through earnings outperform year-to-year GAAP income as an intrinsic-value gauge.

## Repurchases by investees silently enlarge the look-through claim

When an investee uses retained earnings to repurchase its own shares, the partial holder's proportionate ownership grows at no additional outlay. By 2021, Berkshire held 5.55% of Apple — up from 5.39% — without buying a single additional share, because Apple's ongoing buybacks reduced the total share count. GAAP recorded only $785M in Apple dividends; Berkshire's attributable 5.55% share of Apple's 2021 earnings was $5.6B — more than seven times the reported figure. "Each 0.1% of Apple's 2021 earnings amounted to $100 million" ([berkshire-2021-letter](../sources/berkshire-2021-letter.md)). See [share-repurchases](share-repurchases.md).

## How it's discussed in sources

- [berkshire-1977-letter](../sources/berkshire-1977-letter.md) — seed observation: Capital Cities produced ~$1.3M in attributable earnings; only $40,000 appeared in reported income as dividends
- [berkshire-1978-letter](../sources/berkshire-1978-letter.md) — elevated from observation to framework via SAFECO; "we believe the balance, although not reportable, to be just as real in terms of eventual benefit to us"
- [berkshire-1980-letter](../sources/berkshire-1980-letter.md) — canonical articulation: iceberg framing; tree-in-forest image; retained-earnings-corollary stated as a principle; retained investee earnings exceeded total reported Berkshire operating earnings for the first time
- [berkshire-1982-letter](../sources/berkshire-1982-letter.md) — accounting vs. economic earnings formally named; GEICO worked example; "manic-depressive lemmings" as the image for translation-lag irregularity
- [berkshire-1983-letter](../sources/berkshire-1983-letter.md) — codified as owner's-manual principle #6: "we much prefer to purchase $2 of earnings that is not reportable … than $1 of earnings that is reportable"
- [berkshire-1989-letter](../sources/berkshire-1989-letter.md) — definitional source: "look-through earnings" named for the first time; 15% annual growth benchmark set as the primary intrinsic-value target
- [berkshire-1990-letter](../sources/berkshire-1990-letter.md) — three-part calculation formula first published: operating earnings + proportionate investee retained earnings − incremental tax; Capital Cities example (~$82M attributable, ~$530K recorded); annual look-through calculations reported 1991–1994, reaching $1,030M by 1994
- [berkshire-2011-letter](../sources/berkshire-2011-letter.md) — Big Four quantification: $862M dividends vs. $3.3B attributable earnings; the systematic GAAP-to-intrinsic gap made explicit at Berkshire's mature scale
- [berkshire-2019-letter](../sources/berkshire-2019-letter.md) — Smith/Keynes intellectual genealogy; 10 top holdings: $3.8B dividends vs. $8.3B attributable retained earnings (retained exceeded dividends by more than 2:1)
- [berkshire-2021-letter](../sources/berkshire-2021-letter.md) — sharpest quantification: Apple GAAP $785M vs. attributable $5.6B (7:1 gap); unit-rate illustration ("each 0.1% of Apple's 2021 earnings = $100M"); silent compounding via buybacks explicitly quantified
