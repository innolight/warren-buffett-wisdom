# Look-Through Earnings

## Definition

The earnings *attributable* to a partial-equity stake — the holder's pro-rata share of the underlying business's earnings — as distinct from the dividend income that actually shows up in the holder's reported P&L. The gap between the two is not an accounting problem; it is a feature. When the underlying company retains earnings and deploys them well, the holder benefits even without receiving a check.

Buffett's later formal label: **"economic earnings"** vs. **"accounting earnings"** ([[berkshire-1982-letter]]). Economic earnings include all undistributed earnings attributable to the ownership stake, regardless of accounting threshold; accounting earnings include only dividends below 20% ownership.

## The retained-earnings corollary

A well-run company's retained earnings may be worth *more than 100 cents on the dollar* to the minority holder — because management deploys them at high returns on capital. Conversely, a poorly-run company's retained earnings may be worth much less than 100 cents — because management deploys them at low returns. The value of retained earnings is determined entirely by the use to which they are put, not by accounting recognition.

Buffett's 1980 articulation: "The value to Berkshire Hathaway of retained earnings is not determined by whether we own 100%, 50%, 20% or 1% of the businesses in which they reside. Rather, the value of those retained earnings is determined by the use to which they are put and the subsequent level of earnings produced by that usage. … It's the act that counts, not the actors."

## The 1980 iceberg

The 1980 letter contains the most fully developed articulation. With Berkshire's insurance concentration in <20%-owned positions, the part of investee earnings retained for Berkshire's account exceeded total reported Berkshire operating earnings — "conventional accounting only allows less than half of our earnings 'iceberg' to appear above the surface, in plain view. Within the corporate world such a result is quite rare; in our case it is likely to be recurring."

The "tree in the forest" image:

> If a tree grows in a forest partially owned by us, but we don't record the growth in our financial statements, we still own part of the tree.

## The accounting-vs-economic formalization (1982)

The 1982 letter explicitly distinguishes "accounting earnings" from "economic earnings." Example: GEICO 1982 dividends $3.5M after tax vs. ~$23M attributable undistributed earnings.

> If GEICO had earned less money in 1982 but had paid an additional $1 million in dividends, our reported earnings would have been larger despite the poorer business results. Conversely, if GEICO had earned an additional $100 million — and retained it all — our reported earnings would have been unchanged. Clearly "accounting" earnings can seriously misrepresent economic reality.

Buffett's conclusion in 1982: "Accounting numbers are the beginning, not the end, of business valuation."

## The translation lag

Realization of look-through earnings through capital gains is extraordinarily irregular as to timing. The 1982 image: shareholders translate retained-earnings value via "an auction market where prices are set by participants with behavior patterns that sometimes resemble those of an army of manic-depressive lemmings." Over long stretches the translation happens reliably; over short stretches it can swing wildly in either direction.

The 1982 letter introduces "St. Offset" — Arthur Okun's patron saint for economists — to describe the empirical reality: across a portfolio of partial-ownership positions, retained earnings in some investees may not translate at all into market value (or may translate negatively), while in others they may translate at >$1 of market value per $1 of retained earnings. The aggregate, if the businesses are selected well, has compounded reliably.

## Look-through earnings as Berkshire's primary economic measure

By 1983, look-through earnings are explicitly part of the owner's-manual framework. Principle #6: "Accounting consequences do not influence our operating or capital-allocation decisions. When acquisition costs are similar, we much prefer to purchase $2 of earnings that is not reportable by us under standard accounting principles than to purchase $1 of earnings that is reportable."

By 1984, Buffett estimates 1984 reported dividends from non-controlled holdings at ~$39M; total attributable retained earnings approximately $65M — together a substantial multiple of Berkshire's own controlled-business operating earnings.

## The 1990 explicit calculation method

The 1990 letter is the first time the look-through calculation is laid out in tabular form. The method:

> Look-through earnings consist of: (1) the operating earnings reported in the previous section, plus; (2) the retained operating earnings of major investees that, under GAAP accounting, are not reflected in our profits, less; (3) an allowance for the tax that would be paid by Berkshire if these retained earnings of investees had instead been distributed to us. ([[berkshire-1990-letter]])

The 1994 calculation (illustrating the format):

| Cap Cities/ABC | 13.0% | 85 |

The 14% hypothetical tax rate is the property-casualty insurer dividends-received deduction rate. The target growth rate Buffett commits to: ~15% annual look-through-earnings growth implies intrinsic-value compounding at that rate.

## Where look-through earnings appear strongest

Look-through earnings dominate reported earnings when investees retain earnings at high returns on capital. The Coca-Cola case is the canonical illustration of how a small dividend hides a large attributable-earnings contribution. The 1990 letter's framing:

> When Coca-Cola uses retained earnings to repurchase its shares, the company increases our percentage ownership in what I regard to be the most valuable franchise in the world. (Coke also, of course, uses retained earnings in many other value-enhancing ways.) Instead of repurchasing stock, Coca-Cola could pay those funds to us in dividends, which we could then use to purchase more Coke shares. That would be a less efficient scenario … If this less efficient procedure were followed, however, Berkshire would report far greater "earnings."

See [[coca-cola]].
