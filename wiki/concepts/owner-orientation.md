# Owner Orientation

## The principle

A public company should be run on the premise that its shareholders are partners in a business — not transient holders of a traded security. The mental and operating model is *managing-partner-for-owner-partners*, with corporate-form considerations subordinate to that relationship. The owner's manual published in the 1983 Berkshire letter is the canonical articulation.

## The 13 principles (1983 owner's manual)

Drawn from [[berkshire-1983-letter]]. Compressed:

1. **Corporate form, partnership attitude.** "We do not view the company itself as the ultimate owner of our business assets but, instead, view the company as a conduit through which our shareholders own the assets."
2. **Directors eat their own cooking.** ≥50% of family net worth in Berkshire for at least four of five directors.
3. **Long-term goal: maximize per-share intrinsic business value, not size.** "We measure by per-share progress."
4. **First preference: directly own diversified businesses that generate cash and earn above-average returns; second preference: own parts of such businesses via marketable equities.** Capital-allocation choice driven by price/availability and insurance-capital needs, not preference.
5. **GAAP-consolidated earnings reveal little.** Report by individual business and let owners judge.
6. **Accounting consequences do not drive operating or capital-allocation decisions.** Prefer $2 of unreportable earnings to $1 of reportable.
7. **Use little debt; structure it long-term and fixed-rate when used.** "We will reject interesting opportunities rather than over-leverage our balance sheet."
8. **No diversification at control prices that destroy shareholder value.** Owners can diversify themselves via direct market purchases.
9. **Test retention by results.** $1 of market value per $1 retained, on a five-year rolling basis. See [[dividend-policy]].
10. **Issue stock only when receiving as much in business value as given.** All forms — mergers, public offerings, stock-for-debt, options, convertibles. See [[stock-as-acquisition-currency]].
11. **Do not sell good businesses regardless of price; reluctant to sell sub-par ones if they generate some cash and the people are sound.** Gin-rummy management style is rejected.
12. **Candor in reporting.** Tell the business facts you'd want to know if the positions were reversed. "The CEO who misleads others in public may eventually mislead himself in private."
13. **Reserve on investment activities.** Won't discuss marketable-securities ideas; even denials become tells.

## Why it matters

Three operational consequences distinguish owner-oriented management from the more common reported-numbers-and-management-domain orientation:

- **Communication selects the shareholder base.** Buffett's 1983 "shareholder eugenics" section frames consistent, candid communication as the mechanism by which owner-oriented companies attract long-term owner-partner shareholders rather than short-term traders. The shareholder base, in turn, lets the company act on long-horizon principles without continual pressure to optimize for the next quarter.
- **Capital-allocation decisions become testable.** Retention, repurchases, dividends, share issuances, and acquisitions all become judgable against the per-share-intrinsic-value standard. "Our test of the wisdom of retaining earnings" is concrete; failure is visible.
- **Resistance to the institutional imperative is structurally easier.** Owner-aligned compensation (Buffett's 47% Berkshire stake, mentioned in 1984) removes much of the personal incentive to "fail conventionally." Decisions can be made on long-term economic merit rather than career-survival reflex. See [[institutional-imperative]].
