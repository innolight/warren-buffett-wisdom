# Investor's Misery Index

## The underlying principle

A nominal return is not a real return. Two layers stand between the headline number and actual purchasing-power growth:

1. **Inflation** — what the same dollar buys next year vs. this year.
2. **Tax on the nominal return** — ordinary-income tax on dividends, capital-gains tax on appreciation (eventually realized, even if retained for years).

Both layers attack the nominal figure. Buffett's compressed formulation:

> **Misery Index = Inflation Rate + Tax Cost of Converting Nominal Returns to Cash**

When the misery index exceeds the business's return on equity, the investor's real purchasing power shrinks — even if they consume nothing at all. The investor is, in effect, taxed on the inflation-component of nominal gains: a pure confiscation of purchasing power.

## Why it matters

Most investors evaluate performance in nominal terms; the misery index forces the honest question. Three implications:

1. **High inflation reframes what counts as a "good" return.** A business earning 15% ROE in a 12% inflation environment may destroy real wealth more reliably than create it.
2. **The tax layer makes nominal performance even more deceptive.** Even retained earnings create a deferred tax liability that compounds against you.
3. **No corporate solution exists.** "We have no corporate solution to this problem; high inflation rates will not help us earn higher rates of return on equity." The misery index is an external condition management cannot control.

## The 1979 numbers (the case Buffett built the framework around)

The 1979 letter provides the arithmetic. At ~20% compounded ROE and ~14% inflation, after-tax purchasing power gain approaches zero — roughly 6 percentage points of the nominal 20% go to taxes, leaving 14% to fight 14% inflation.

Buffett's blunt summary: "A business earning 20% on capital can produce a negative real return for its owners under inflationary conditions not much more severe than presently prevail."

The gold comparison crystallized it. Berkshire's book value per share in late 1964 bought roughly half an ounce of gold. By yearend 1979, after fifteen years of 20.5% annual compounding — "blood, sweat and tears" — the book value produced could still buy roughly half an ounce of gold. All the nominal growth had been consumed by inflation.
