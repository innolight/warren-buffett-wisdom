# Investor's Misery Index

**A composite erosion rate — inflation plus the tax cost of converting nominal gains to cash — that, when it exceeds a business's ROE, destroys the owner's real purchasing power even if nothing is consumed.**

## Actionable takeaway

- **Compute the real hurdle before buying.** Add the expected inflation rate to the effective tax rate on distributions (dividends + eventual capital-gains realization). That sum is the misery index — the floor a business's ROE must clear to produce any real wealth at all.
- **Avoid thin-margin businesses in inflationary environments.** The gap between ROE and the misery index is the only wealth actually created. A 12% ROE against a 10% misery index leaves 2 percentage points of real gain — any business friction erases it entirely.
- **Strip inflation from nominal ROE before crediting management.** Reported ROE that merely matches the misery index produces zero real value; management deserves credit only for the excess above it.
- **Recognize there is no corporate fix.** Business ROE does not automatically rise to offset a rising misery index. If the index exceeds what the business structurally earns, the investor absorbs the full loss — no operational decision by management changes that.

## Definition

A nominal return is not a real return. Two layers stand between the headline number and actual purchasing-power growth:

1. **Inflation** — what the same dollar buys next year vs. this year.
2. **Tax on the nominal return** — ordinary-income tax on dividends, capital-gains tax on appreciated value (eventually realized, even if earnings are retained for years).

Both layers attack the nominal figure. Buffett's compressed formulation:

> **Misery Index = Inflation Rate + Tax Cost of Converting Nominal Returns to Cash**

When the misery index exceeds the business's ROE, the investor's real purchasing power shrinks even with zero consumption. The investor is, in effect, taxed on the inflation-component of nominal gains — a pure confiscation of purchasing power.

## Why it matters

Most investors evaluate performance in nominal terms; the misery index forces the honest question. Three implications:

1. **High inflation reframes what counts as a "good" return.** A business earning 15% ROE in a 12% inflation environment may destroy real wealth more reliably than create it.
2. **The tax layer makes nominal performance even more deceptive.** Even retained earnings carry a deferred tax liability that compounds against the investor.
3. **The condition is external.** Business management cannot manufacture a higher ROE to offset a rising misery index — the inflationary environment is the constraint, not the operator's capability.

## The arithmetic of real-return erasure

The sharpest illustration: at roughly 20% compounded ROE and 14% inflation, after-tax purchasing-power gain approaches zero — approximately 6 percentage points of the nominal 20% go to taxes, leaving 14% to fight 14% inflation. All the nominal growth is consumed.

The gold comparison crystallizes it. Berkshire's book value per share in 1964 bought roughly half an ounce of gold. Fifteen years later, after 20.5% annual compounding — "blood, sweat and tears" — the same book value produced could still buy roughly half an ounce of gold.

> Just as the original 3% savings bond, a 5% passbook savings account or an 8% U.S. Treasury Note have, in turn, been transformed by inflation into financial instruments that chew up, rather than enhance, purchasing power over their investment lives, a business earning 20% on capital can produce a negative real return for its owners under inflationary conditions not much more severe than presently prevail. — [berkshire-1979-letter](../sources/berkshire-1979-letter.md)
