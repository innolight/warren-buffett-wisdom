# Mr. Market

## Definition

A personification — borrowed by Buffett from [[ben-graham]] (Chapter 8 of *The Intelligent Investor*) — of the stock market's daily price-quotation behavior. Imagine a business partner who, every day without fail, names a price at which he will either buy your interest or sell you his. The same partner has incurable emotional problems: sometimes euphoric, sometimes terrified, and his quote on any given day may bear no relation to underlying business value.

Buffett's 1987 canonization: "Mr. Market is there to serve you, not to guide you. It is his pocketbook, not his wisdom, that you will find useful."

## Why it matters

Mr. Market reframes the relationship between an investor and the market. The market is not a judge of business value but an *option counterparty* — you may transact when his quote suits you, or ignore him entirely. The more manic-depressive his behavior, the more valuable the optionality.

The framing inverts the conventional anxiety about volatility: a wildly fluctuating market produces irrationally low prices on solid businesses more often than a calm market does. For an investor who has done the work of estimating intrinsic value, Mr. Market's mood swings are a source of opportunity, not risk. See [[volatility-as-opportunity]].

## The Cinderella warning

"But, like Cinderella at the ball, you must heed one warning or everything will turn into pumpkins and mice: Mr. Market is there to serve you, not to guide you." If you fall under his influence — if his quote becomes the basis of your view of value — you have become the patsy. "As they say in poker, 'If you've been in the game 30 minutes and you don't know who the patsy is, you're the patsy.'" ([[berkshire-1987-letter]])

The discipline Mr. Market enforces: you must understand and value the business independently, more reliably than the market does. If you cannot, you do not belong in the game.

## Voting machine vs. weighing machine

Buffett pairs Mr. Market with another Graham line: **"In the short run, the market is a voting machine but in the long run it is a weighing machine."** ([[berkshire-1987-letter]], reprised in [[berkshire-1993-letter]]) The voting machine reflects sentiment; the weighing machine reflects earnings. Over years, business success is eventually recognized; delayed recognition only extends the window for accumulation at favorable prices.

The 1993 letter quotes Coca-Cola's history to make the point empirically: Coke went public in 1919 at $40 per share. By the end of 1920, the market had battered the stock down by more than 50%, to $19.50 — coldly reevaluating Coke's future prospects. At yearend 1993, that single share, with dividends reinvested, was worth more than $2.1 million.

## What Mr. Market replaces

The Mr. Market frame replaces — and is intentionally a substitute for — the efficient-market view of pricing. The 1987 letter is pointed:

> Ben's Mr. Market allegory may seem out-of-date in today's investment world, in which most professionals and academicians talk of efficient markets, dynamic hedging and betas. Their interest in such matters is understandable, since techniques shrouded in mystery clearly have value to the purveyor of investment advice. After all, what witch doctor has ever achieved fame and fortune by simply advising "Take two aspirins"?

See [[risk-vs-volatility]] for the parallel critique of beta as a measure of risk.
