# Mr. Market

A personification borrowed from Graham's *The Intelligent Investor* (Chapter 8): imagine a business partner who names a price every day to buy you out or sell you his share, and whose quotes swing between euphoria and terror with no reliable connection to underlying business value.

## Actionable takeaway

- Treat every market price as an *offer*, not a verdict. You have no obligation to transact; ignore Mr. Market whenever his quote doesn't suit you.
- Estimate intrinsic value independently *before* consulting his price — never *from* it. If you cannot value the business on your own, you have no frame of reference against which to judge his offer.
- When Mr. Market is euphoric, his manic offer deserves as little credence as his panicked one. The allegory runs both ways.
- For the operational question — when to act, what conditions must hold, and why forced selling inverts the principle — see [volatility-as-opportunity](volatility-as-opportunity.md).

## Definition

Mr. Market personifies the market's daily price-quoting behavior. He will show up every day without fail, name a price, and be perfectly happy to be ignored. His quotes are driven by mood, not by business economics. On some days he is euphoric and will pay almost anything; on others he is terrified and will sell his share for almost nothing.

Buffett's canonical formulation: "Mr. Market is there to serve you, not to guide you. It is his pocketbook, not his wisdom, that you will find useful." ([berkshire-1987-letter](../sources/berkshire-1987-letter.md))

## Why it matters

The Mr. Market frame redefines what the market *is*: not a judge of value but an option counterparty. You may transact when his quote suits you, or you may not. The more manic-depressive his behavior, the more valuable the optionality. A volatile, mood-driven market creates irrationally low prices on solid businesses more often than a calm one does. For the investment application of this principle, see [volatility-as-opportunity](volatility-as-opportunity.md).

## The discipline prerequisite: serve or be served

The allegory comes with an inversion test. If you let Mr. Market's quote become your estimate of value — if you read his mood as information about what the business is worth — you have switched from user to guided. "As they say in poker, 'If you've been in the game 30 minutes and you don't know who the patsy is, you're the patsy.'" ([berkshire-1987-letter](../sources/berkshire-1987-letter.md))

The prerequisite is independent valuation: "If you aren't certain that you understand and can value your business far better than Mr. Market, you don't belong in the game." His price is only useful to someone who already has an independent answer to compare it against.

## The voting machine and the weighing machine

Graham's complementary maxim: "In the short run, the market is a voting machine but in the long run it is a weighing machine." ([berkshire-1987-letter](../sources/berkshire-1987-letter.md), reprised in [berkshire-1993-letter](../sources/berkshire-1993-letter.md)) The voting machine tallies sentiment; the weighing machine tallies earnings. Business success is eventually recognized — which means delayed recognition only extends the window to accumulate at favorable prices.

The Coca-Cola history makes this empirical: a single $40 share bought in 1919 survived a more-than-50% price collapse in its first year of trading and was worth $2.1 million — with dividends reinvested — by 1993. ([berkshire-1993-letter](../sources/berkshire-1993-letter.md)) The weighing machine took decades to catch up; it caught up.

## What Mr. Market replaces

The allegory is a direct counter to the efficient-market hypothesis. The 1987 letter: "Ben's Mr. Market allegory may seem out-of-date in today's investment world, in which most professionals and academicians talk of efficient markets, dynamic hedging and betas. Their interest in such matters is understandable, since techniques shrouded in mystery clearly have value to the purveyor of investment advice." By 2022, Buffett's restatement is categorical: "'Efficient' markets exist only in textbooks"; stocks "often trade at truly foolish prices, both high and low." ([berkshire-2022-letter](../sources/berkshire-2022-letter.md)) The Mr. Market allegory is precisely why Berkshire "episodically" buys "pieces of wonderful businesses at wonderful prices." See [risk-vs-volatility](../concepts/risk-vs-volatility.md) for the parallel critique of beta as a measure of risk.

## The allegory runs both ways: mania is as dangerous as panic

Mr. Market's most dangerous form is not the panicked seller but the euphoric bidder. The dot-com cycle (1999–2002) was the starkest demonstration since the 1970s: speculators were "dancing in a room in which the clocks have no hands" ([berkshire-2000-letter](../sources/berkshire-2000-letter.md)), betting on what the next fellow would pay rather than on what the business would produce. The patient investor who declined Mr. Market's manic offers emerged with capital intact. "The insanity of valuations reached during The Great Bubble. Unfortunately, the hangover may prove to be proportional to the binge." ([berkshire-2002-letter](../sources/berkshire-2002-letter.md))

The enduring lesson: the allegory's warning runs symmetrically. The manic offer deserves no more credence than the depressed one. For the conditions under which to act on either extreme, see [volatility-as-opportunity](volatility-as-opportunity.md).
