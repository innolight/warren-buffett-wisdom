# Stock as Acquisition Currency

**Paying for an acquisition with your own stock means selling a piece of your business at the same price you pay for the target — when your stock trades below intrinsic value, you give away more than you receive.**

## Actionable takeaway

- **Compute the intrinsic value of shares you are surrendering, not their market price.** If that value exceeds the intrinsic value received, no growth story or synergy projection changes the arithmetic.
- **The test is not "is the deal good?" but "does the intrinsic value received exceed the intrinsic value given away?"** A genuinely excellent acquisition still destroys value if the shares used to pay for it are worth more than the price.
- **"Anti-dilutive to EPS" is not reassurance.** A deal can be EPS-accretive while destroying intrinsic value per share. The only test that matters is intrinsic-value dilution per share.
- **Count follow-on equity raises as part of acquisition cost.** Stock issued afterward to "restore balance sheet strength" carries the same shareholder cost as stock issued at closing.
- **Don't ask the barber whether you need a haircut.** An investment banker whose fee depends on deal-closing cannot assess the true value of the acquirer's shares being surrendered. Hire a second advisor whose fee is contingent on the deal *not* closing.

## Definition

When an acquirer pays by issuing its own stock, it is selling a portion of itself at market price to obtain a target priced at full intrinsic value. Sellers in negotiated transactions accept nothing less than their full intrinsic worth. Acquirers paying with stock receive only what the market currently assigns to those shares. When the market undervalues the acquirer — the normal condition for any well-run business, periodically — the acquirer hands over $2 of intrinsic worth to receive $1. ([berkshire-1982-letter](../sources/berkshire-1982-letter.md))

The principle extends beyond formal M&A. The 1983 owner's manual states: "We will issue common stock only when we receive as much in business value as we give. This rule applies to all forms of issuance — not only mergers or public stock offerings, but stock-for-debt swaps, stock options, and convertible securities as well." ([berkshire-1983-letter](../sources/berkshire-1983-letter.md))

## Why it matters

This is one of the most common and most invisible destroyers of shareholder value in corporate America. The mechanism is structural, not malicious — the arithmetic runs identically whether management intends harm or not. Investment bankers assess the target carefully while valuing the acquirer's shares at market rather than at intrinsic value. The CEO gains a larger organization to run; continuing shareholders lose per-share value. No natural corrective mechanism exists in the M&A process.

## The mechanism: undervalued currency at a full-value price

Securities law recognizes the problem for investment companies: the SEC prohibits a registered investment company from issuing shares below net asset value to acquire assets — plainly a gift to the acquired party. Operating companies perform the equivalent transaction routinely, with no equivalent prohibition.

Buffett's 1982 illustration: Company X, selling at 50% of asset value, issues shares at market-value parity with Company Y's full asset value. X's shareholders hand over $2 of intrinsic value for every $1 received. "For gold valued as gold cannot be purchased intelligently through the utilization of gold — or even silver — valued as lead."

## The EPS misframe: the wrong dilution test

Standard "anti-dilution" analysis counts shares and projects EPS. That is the wrong test. Dilution that matters is *dilution in intrinsic business value per share*, which requires judgment about future earnings, returns on capital, and competitive position — not a pro-forma EPS calculation. There have been mergers anti-dilutive to current EPS that were instantly value-destroying for the acquirer. The misframe persists because EPS is easy to measure and intrinsic value requires judgment — and because investment bankers value the acquirer's currency at market, never at intrinsic value.

## Management rationalizations to dismantle

Three common responses to the $2-for-$1 problem, each invalid:

1. **"The company we're buying will be worth a lot more."** Growth is implicit for both sides of the trade. An imbalance in the ratio of intrinsic values given and received does not improve because both sides grow.
2. **"We have to grow."** Who is the "we" that benefits? Existing shareholders' claim on current assets shrinks with every share issued, regardless of the target's prospects.
3. **"Sellers want stock for tax reasons."** A rationale for the seller's preference, not a defense of the buyer's interest.

## The linguistic reframe

The honest description is not "Company A acquires Company B" but "Part of A sold to acquire B" — or "Owners of B receive part of A in exchange for their properties." The mating-pregnancy line: "If corporate pregnancy is going to be the consequence of corporate mating, the time to face that fact is before the moment of ecstasy."

## The empirical verdict: the track record confirms the arithmetic

By 1997, Buffett had enough history to audit his own stock-funded deals. Aggregating all Berkshire stock-only mergers (excluding affiliated Blue Chip Stamps and Diversified Retailing): "our shareholders are slightly worse off than they would have been had I not done the transactions… when I've issued stock, I've cost you money." The cause was not that acquired businesses underperformed — it was the exchange ratio. Berkshire's portfolio is full of ".380 hitters"; trading any portion of it for a ".350 hitter," however good that business, is almost never a trade-up. Forward policy: pay cash whenever possible; when sellers insist on stock, offer an attractive cash option to draw as many as possible away from the dilutive component. ([berkshire-1997-letter](../sources/berkshire-1997-letter.md))

## The compounding failure mode: bad business × stock currency

Dexter Shoe (acquired 1993 in Berkshire stock) is the canonical case. Two independent mistakes compounded: the acquisition mistake (no structural protection against foreign competition) and the currency mistake (payment in Berkshire shares priced below their intrinsic value at the time). "I clearly made a mistake in paying what I did for Dexter in 1993. Furthermore, I compounded that mistake in a huge way by using Berkshire shares in payment." ([berkshire-2000-letter](../sources/berkshire-2000-letter.md))

The principle generalizes: cash mistakes cost only the cash. Stock mistakes cost the cash equivalent *plus* the forgone compounding on every surrendered share. A bad acquisition paid in stock is doubly destructive.

## The tax-structure dimension

A stock-for-stock deal carries a third penalty beyond intrinsic-value dilution: no step-up in asset-tax basis occurs at closing, leaving the full capital-gains exposure on any eventual sale. A cash purchase of assets permits goodwill amortization for tax purposes, creating materially better after-tax economics for the same pre-tax deal. "From the economic standpoint of the acquiring company, the worst deal of all is a stock-for-stock acquisition." ([berkshire-1999-letter](../sources/berkshire-1999-letter.md))

## The structural conflict in M&A advice

Investment bankers' fees depend on deals closing. In fifty years of board memberships, Buffett had never heard a banker ask what the acquirer's shares are actually *worth* — only what they are priced. "Don't ask the barber whether you need a haircut."

The 2009 structural fix: when stock is the proposed currency, hire a second advisor whose fee is contingent on the deal *not* closing. Only that alignment forces someone in the room to assess the true cost of the equity being surrendered — the question that boards systematically fail to ask. ([berkshire-2009-letter](../sources/berkshire-2009-letter.md))
