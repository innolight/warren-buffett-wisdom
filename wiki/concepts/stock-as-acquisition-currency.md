# Stock as Acquisition Currency

## The principle

When an acquirer pays for a business by issuing its own stock, the acquirer is *selling a portion of itself* to obtain the target. If the acquirer's stock trades below intrinsic value, the acquirer pays in undervalued currency for an asset valued at full intrinsic worth — surrendering more value than it receives.

Buffett's 1982 compressed statement: "the buyer is faced with the unhappy prospect of using a substantially undervalued currency to make its purchase… the acquirer must give up $2 of value to receive $1 of value."

## Why it matters

This is one of the most common — and most invisible — destroyers of shareholder value in corporate America. The mechanism is structural, not malicious:

- Acquirers in negotiated transactions get full intrinsic value: sellers won't accept less.
- Acquirers paying with stock can extract only the value the market currently assigns to that stock.
- If the market's current valuation lags intrinsic value (which is the normal condition for well-run businesses periodically), every share issued in acquisition is a partial sale at a discount to its real worth.

The arithmetic compounds. Buffett's investment-company illustration in 1982: Company X selling at 50% of asset value issues shares equal in market value to 100% of Company Y's asset value — handing over $2 of X's intrinsic value for $1 of Y's. The SEC would block this for registered investment companies; for operating companies the equivalent transaction is routinely cheered by the market.

## Three escape routes

The 1982 letter identifies three ways to avoid value destruction:

1. **A true business-value-for-business-value merger.** Both parties exchange shares at parity in intrinsic value, not market value. Rare in practice. The 1983 Berkshire/Blue Chip merger was attempted on this basis.
2. **Issue stock only when it trades at or above intrinsic value.** A condition that obtained for many acquirers in 1965–69, rarely since.
3. **Damage-repair repurchase.** After issuing shares for a target, the acquirer repurchases an equivalent number in the open market — effectively converting the deal into cash-for-stock. Less elegant; legitimate damage control.

## The dilution misframe

Standard EPS-based dilution math misses the substantive issue. A deal can be "anti-dilutive" to current EPS while destroying intrinsic value. Dilution that matters is *dilution in intrinsic business value per share*, which requires judgment about future earnings, returns on capital, and competitive position — not just a pro-forma EPS calculation.

## Three management rationalizations to dismantle

1. **"The company we're buying is going to be worth a lot more in the future."** Implicit in both sides of the trade. The imbalance survives the doubling.
2. **"We have to grow."** Who's the "we"? Existing shareholders' interest in current assets is *reduced* by issuance, regardless of growth.
3. **"Sellers want stock for tax reasons."** A rationale for the seller's preference, not for the buyer's interest.

## The linguistic reframe

Buffett's recommended sharper formulation: not "Company A acquires Company B" but **"Part of A sold to acquire B"** — or "Owners of B receive part of A in exchange for their properties." The mating-pregnancy line: "If corporate pregnancy is going to be the consequence of corporate mating, the time to face that fact is before the moment of ecstasy."

## Subsequent share issuance counts too

"Subsequent sales of common stock or convertible issues, either to complete the financing for a deal or to restore balance sheet strength, must be fully counted in evaluating the fundamental mathematics of the original acquisition." A deal that looks cash-funded but requires a follow-on equity raise has the same shareholder-dilution math as a direct stock-funded deal.
