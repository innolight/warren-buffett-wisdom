# Growth and Value

## Definition

"Growth" and "value" are not two contrasting investment styles. They are joined at the hip: growth is a variable in the valuation equation, not a separate category. The 1992 Berkshire letter makes this explicit:

> In our opinion, the two approaches are joined at the hip: Growth is always a component in the calculation of value, constituting a variable whose importance can range from negligible to enormous and whose impact can be negative as well as positive. ([berkshire-1992-letter](../sources/berkshire-1992-letter.md))

The foundation is the John Burr Williams discounted-cash-flow formula: the value of any asset is the present value of the cash it will generate over its lifetime, discounted at an appropriate interest rate. Growth enters that equation as one input among several — it is neither inherently good nor inherently separate from valuation. "What is 'investing' if it is not the act of seeking value at least sufficient to justify the amount paid?"

## Why it matters

Treating "growth" and "value" as opposed categories is a category error with real consequences. It causes investors to:

- Pay for growth indiscriminately, without asking whether incremental capital will earn returns above the cost of that capital.
- Dismiss "value" stocks without noticing that a low-reinvestment, high-return business may be compounding intrinsic value rapidly.
- Mistake speculation (buying an asset hoping someone will pay more) for investing (buying an asset whose calculated value justifies the price).

The 2000 letter, written against the backdrop of the dot-com bubble, is blunter: "Market commentators and investment managers who glibly refer to 'growth' and 'value' styles as contrasting approaches to investment are displaying their ignorance, not their sophistication. Growth is simply a component — usually a plus, sometimes a minus — in the value equation." ([berkshire-2000-letter](../sources/berkshire-2000-letter.md))

## When growth helps — and when it hurts

Growth creates value only when the business can deploy incremental capital at returns that exceed the cost of that capital. The 1992 letter:

> Growth benefits investors only when the business in point can invest at incremental returns that are enticing — in other words, only when each dollar used to finance the growth creates over a dollar of long-term market value. In the case of a low-return business requiring incremental funds, growth hurts the investor. ([berkshire-1992-letter](../sources/berkshire-1992-letter.md))

The logic is arithmetic, not rhetorical:

- **High-return business + growth = compounding.** A business earning 20% on equity that retains earnings and redeploys them at 20% is worth more each year than its book value grows, because the market will capitalize those returns at a multiple. Growth accelerates compounding.
- **Low-return business + growth = destruction.** A business earning 6% on equity that retains earnings forces capital into an activity generating 6%. Every dollar retained is a dollar the investor could have redeployed more productively elsewhere. More growth means more capital trapped at poor returns. The 2000 letter: "Growth can destroy value if it requires cash inputs in the early years that exceed the discounted value of the cash that those assets will generate in later years."

The relevant quantity is not the growth rate itself but the *return on incremental invested capital* relative to the cost of capital. A slowly growing business with high incremental returns (See's Candies, GEICO) creates more value than a fast-growing business with returns near or below the cost of capital. See [cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses](cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses.md) for the related distinction between wonderful businesses and fair businesses at cheap prices.

## The speculation distinction

Consciously paying more than calculated value — hoping that someone will pay a still-higher price later — is not investing. It is speculation. Buffett's framing from the 1992 letter:

> Consciously paying more for a stock than its calculated value — in the hope that it can soon be sold for a still-higher price — should be labeled speculation (which is neither illegal, immoral nor — in our view — financially fattening).

The 2000 letter repeats the point with more texture: "Now, speculation — in which the focus is not on what an asset will produce but rather on what the next fellow will pay for it — is neither illegal, immoral nor un-American. But it is not a game in which Charlie and I wish to play. We bring nothing to the party, so why should we expect to take anything home?" ([berkshire-2000-letter](../sources/berkshire-2000-letter.md))

The distinction is important because "growth investing" during a bubble often drifts into speculation without the practitioner noticing. The focus shifts from what the business will produce to what the next buyer will pay. This is a different activity — not wrong in itself, but mislabeled when called investing. See [margin-of-safety](margin-of-safety.md) for the discipline that keeps investment distinct from speculation.

## The Aesop framing (2000)

The 2000 letter restates the same idea through Aesop's "a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush." Three questions determine value: How certain are there birds in the bush? When will they emerge, and how many? What is the risk-free rate? That is the entire valuation framework — for farms, bonds, stocks, manufacturing plants, or dot-com companies. Growth is implicit in "how many birds, and when" — it is one input, not a separate philosophy.

The 2000 letter uses this to explain why the Internet did not change anything: "Neither the advent of the steam engine, the harnessing of electricity nor the creation of the automobile changed the formula one iota — nor will the Internet." A business's value is the discounted present value of future cash flows. Higher growth assumptions raise the numerator; higher uncertainty raises the discount rate. Neither observation requires a new framework.

## How it's discussed in sources

- [berkshire-1992-letter](../sources/berkshire-1992-letter.md) — the canonical essay. Explicit rejection of growth/value as contrasting categories. The John Burr Williams formula. "Investing" is by definition value-seeking, so "value investing" is redundant. Growth helps when incremental capital earns above cost; hurts when it does not. Margin of safety reaffirmed as the cornerstone.
- [berkshire-2000-letter](../sources/berkshire-2000-letter.md) — reinforcement through the dot-com bubble. The Aesop "bird in the hand" essay as the irreducible three-question valuation framework. Direct attack on "growth vs. value" language as ignorance. "Growth can destroy value if it requires cash inputs … that exceed the discounted value of the cash that those assets will generate."
