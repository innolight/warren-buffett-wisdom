# Float

Float is the pool of premiums an insurer holds between collection and claims payment — investable capital that costs nothing when underwriting discipline is maintained.

## Actionable takeaway

- Judge an insurer by the **cost** of its float, not its size. Float is only an asset if the combined underwriting result is at or below break-even over a full cycle. Float from undisciplined underwriting is debt at an unknown, potentially ruinous interest rate.
- Prefer float that is **long-tail and revolving**: claims continuously replaced by new business mean the GAAP liability overstates the true burden, and long duration gives the investment compounding more time. Discount float that can be withdrawn (life/annuity with surrender options) or repriced too frequently to accumulate.
- When an insurer does share repurchases, float stays constant while the share count shrinks — **float per share rises automatically**, compounding the advantage without any new capital.
- Ask whether the insurer has investment options commensurate with its float. A well-run float machine can still be economically strangled if its capital base forces it into narrow, yield-compressed assets.

## Definition

In P/C insurance, the policyholder pays the premium today; the insurer pays claims months or years later. The premiums held in the interval — the float — can be invested. If underwriting is at break-even or better, the insurer effectively borrows money at zero or negative cost to build an investment portfolio. For a permanent-capital insurer like Berkshire, float is the engine that converts underwriting discipline into long-duration compounding: float grew from roughly $19M at the 1967 [National Indemnity](../entities/national-indemnity.md) acquisition to $171B by 2024, generating $32B of after-tax underwriting profit over the final two decades alone.

## Why it matters

Float inverts the economics of ordinary business. A manufacturer borrows to build before it sells; a P/C insurer is paid before it incurs costs — sometimes decades before, in long-tail liability or retroactive reinsurance lines. The same lag that produces investable capital also conceals catastrophic mispricing for years. Float is therefore a blessing or a curse depending entirely on the discipline that generates it, which is why [underwriting-discipline](underwriting-discipline.md) is float's inseparable companion concept.

## The mechanism: premiums before claims

Ordinary businesses incur costs before or alongside their sales. P/C insurance inverts this: "we receive payment upfront and much later learn what our product has cost us — sometimes a moment of truth that is delayed as much as 30 or more years." ([berkshire-2024-letter](../sources/berkshire-2024-letter.md)) The amount and duration of the float depend on the type of insurance written: short-tail lines (auto, property) produce float that turns over in months; long-tail lines (liability, workers' compensation, asbestos) produce float that compounds for decades. Retroactive reinsurance — assuming liability for losses already incurred — can produce float that remains investable for fifty years or more. The structural source is not clever accounting; it is the time gap between when money arrives and when it leaves.

## The fundamental test: cost of float vs. alternative funding rate

Buffett canonized the right insurance scorecard in the [berkshire-1990-letter](../sources/berkshire-1990-letter.md): divide the annual underwriting result by average float to derive the effective cost of the capital the business generates, then compare that cost to what the insurer would otherwise pay for funds (approximately the long-term government bond yield). A cost of float below that benchmark means the insurance operation is creating value; above it, destroying value — identical logic to a bank paying more on deposits than it earns on loans.

In 19 of Berkshire's first 24 years in insurance (1967–1990), float cost less than the U.S. government paid for long-term money. Over the full 32 years from 1967 to 1998, average cost was well under zero — policyholders were in effect paying Berkshire to invest their capital.

Float at zero cost is functionally equivalent to equity. The 1995 counterfactual makes the logic explicit: had Berkshire replaced its $3.4B of 1994 float with actual equity, it would have owned the same assets, earned lower profits (the float was generating income, not consuming it), and had to issue dilutive new shares. "Since our float has cost us virtually nothing over the years, it has in effect served as equity. Of course, it differs from true equity in that it doesn't belong to us." ([berkshire-1995-letter](../sources/berkshire-1995-letter.md)) The strategic implication: the three determinants of insurance value are the amount of float, its cost, and — most important — the long-term outlook for both.

## Float is a revolving fund, not a draining liability

GAAP records float as a full liability, as if all reserves will be paid out and no new business will replace them. This overstates the burden for any insurer writing steadily expanding business. The correct framing: "Owing $1 that in effect will never leave the premises — because new business is almost certain to deliver a substitute — is worlds different from owing $1 that will go out the door tomorrow and not be replaced." ([berkshire-2016-letter](../sources/berkshire-2016-letter.md))

A second GAAP distortion compounds the first: Berkshire carries $15.5B of insurance goodwill as a partial offset to the float liability, but this figure reflects acquisition cost, not economic value, and barely moved between 2000 and 2016 while float grew $64B. The double distortion — float over-stated as liability, goodwill under-stated as asset — is the primary structural reason Berkshire's intrinsic value substantially exceeds book value. See [intrinsic-value-vs-book-value](intrinsic-value-vs-book-value.md).

## Failure mode: float invested badly becomes a trap

The [berkshire-1980-letter](../sources/berkshire-1980-letter.md) revealed a structural pathology. P/C insurers that had loaded up on long-duration bonds through the 1970s found their assets worth far less at market than at book, at a ratio of 2–3× net worth. Recognizing losses would have wiped out stated net worth. The exit was blocked: letting premium volume shrink would force bond sales and loss recognition. So these insurers accepted underwriting at any price — what Buffett called "asset-maintenance underwriting" — simply to keep the float pool large enough to avoid selling the bonds. The float advantage inverted into a trap: unable to price honestly, unable to shrink, locked into the very assets that were destroying them. See [bonds-and-inflation](bonds-and-inflation.md).

## Failure mode: loss-reserving uncertainty conceals hollowed-out balance sheets

Float's size is imprecisely measured: it depends on estimates of losses incurred but not yet paid (or reported). The [berkshire-1984-letter](../sources/berkshire-1984-letter.md) documented how an insurer could be technically insolvent for years before running out of cash. "Insolvent insurers don't run out of cash until long after they have run out of net worth" — and those walking-dead operators continue writing at any price, dragging down industry pricing for solvent competitors. The same float that funds compounding can disguise a hollowed-out balance sheet for a long time. Conservative reserving is itself a competitive feature: a balance sheet honest about its liabilities is rare enough in long-tail insurance to become a moat, enabling Berkshire to attract structured-settlement and loss-reserve-assumption businesses specifically because its financial strength is credible.

## Structural variation: retroactive reinsurance extends float's duration

Retroactive reinsurance — assuming liability for losses already incurred, in exchange for a large upfront premium — produces float with very long duration. Berkshire's 2006 Equitas deal ($7.12B received for up to $13.9B in historic Lloyd's liabilities) illustrates the extreme: float that may not be fully paid out for fifty years. The accounting produces a recurring intentional "underwriting loss" (DCRA amortization) that is economically distinct from pricing failure; the economics are favorable if investment income on the float exceeds the annual amortization charge. ([berkshire-2002-letter](../sources/berkshire-2002-letter.md), [berkshire-2006-letter](../sources/berkshire-2006-letter.md))
