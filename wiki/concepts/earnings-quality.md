# Earnings Quality

## Definition

Earnings quality refers to the degree to which a company's reported earnings accurately reflect its true economic performance. Low earnings quality occurs when management systematically excludes real, recurring costs from reported figures — through labeling, adjustment, or reclassification — to present a misleadingly optimistic picture to shareholders.

## Why it matters

Reported earnings are the primary signal most investors use to assess a business. When those figures are manipulated — even within GAAP — investors overpay, misjudge competitive strength, and misread management character. The problem is not just valuation error: a culture of number-manufacturing is self-reinforcing. CEOs who optimize for reported figures foster subordinates who follow suit, and the most dangerous downstream consequence is insurer loss-reserve underestimation, a practice that has destroyed many industry participants. Earnings quality failures are therefore both a valuation risk and a governance risk.

## When GAAP itself misleads: unrealized investment gains (2017)

The 2016 letter focused on *management choices* that produce distorted figures — restructuring and SBC exclusions from "adjusted earnings." The 2017 letter introduces a distinct category: a GAAP rule that mandates distortion regardless of management intent.

A new FASB rule, effective beginning in 2018 reporting periods, requires that the net change in unrealized investment gains and losses on equity holdings be included in net income. For Berkshire, with $170B in equity securities that can swing $10B or more in a single quarter, Buffett assessed: "That requirement will produce some truly wild and capricious swings in our GAAP bottom-line." The GAAP bottom-line becomes "useless for analytical purposes."

The rule compounds an existing problem: realized gains were already excluded from Berkshire's preferred "operating earnings" figure because they fluctuate randomly and bear no relationship to underlying business performance in any given period. "We sometimes have reported substantial realized gains for a period when our portfolio, overall, performed poorly (or the converse)."

**The two categories of earnings-quality failure:**
1. *Management-originated*: voluntarily excluding real, recurring costs (restructuring charges, SBC) from reported "adjusted earnings" — the 2016 letter's case.
2. *Rule-originated*: GAAP requiring inclusion of non-cash, market-driven fluctuations that swamp operating performance — the 2017 letter's case.

Both produce the same effect — figures that mislead about underlying economic performance — but the remedies differ. For management-originated distortions, the fix is skepticism about adjustments. For rule-originated distortions, the fix is reading operating earnings rather than GAAP net income, and allowing analysis time before trading begins (Buffett's Friday-evening release practice). See [[berkshire-2017-letter]].

## How it's discussed in sources

- [[berkshire-2016-letter]] — Buffett catalogs the two favorite techniques managements use to inflate "adjusted earnings": restructuring cost exclusions and stock-based compensation exclusions. On restructuring: Berkshire has been restructuring since 1965, yet every year managements tell owners to ignore these costs. On SBC: excluding stock compensation from earnings is equivalent to saying compensation paid in equity is not a cost — a claim no CEO can honestly defend. The letter illustrates the absurdity via a hypothetical Berkshire "accounting laboratory" that reclassifies cash pay as "cash paid in lieu of SBC" to produce identically inflated adjusted figures. Buffett and Munger treat "always makes the numbers" as a red flag, not a compliment: businesses are too unpredictable for numbers to always be met, and a CEO focused on Wall Street will be tempted to manufacture them when surprises occur. Bad behavior is described as contagious — CEOs who overtly seek to report high numbers tend to cultivate a culture in which subordinates try to be "helpful."
- [[berkshire-2017-letter]] — a new FASB rule mandates unrealized investment gains/losses in net income; with $170B in equities, quarterly swings will produce "truly wild and capricious swings" in GAAP net income, rendering the bottom-line "useless for analytical purposes"; contrasted with the existing realized-gains exclusion already in place; the two-category framework (management-originated vs. rule-originated distortion)
