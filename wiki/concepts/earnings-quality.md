# Earnings Quality

**The degree to which a company's reported or adjusted earnings reflect what the business actually earns for its owners — versus what management's labels and accounting choices cause them to appear to earn.**

## Actionable takeaway

- When evaluating any "adjusted earnings" figure, identify each excluded item: if it recurs year after year (restructuring, SBC), it is a real cost regardless of what it is called.
- Distinguish amortization from depreciation when assessing non-cash add-backs. Acquisition-related amortization is a legitimate exclusion — the underlying intangible retains economic value. Depreciation is not — in capital-intensive businesses it *understates* true maintenance cost and must never be added back.
- Never add back stock-based compensation. It dilutes owners as surely as writing a check.
- Apply the four-rung hierarchy with independent skepticism at every step: GAAP net income → "adjusted"/EBITDA → operating earnings → owner earnings. No rung is manipulation-proof.
- Treat "always makes the numbers" as a red flag. Businesses are too unpredictable for figures always to be met; smooth reported earnings signal estimation, not performance.

## Definition

Low earnings quality occurs when management systematically excludes real, recurring costs from reported figures — through relabeling, reclassification, or adjustment — to present a misleadingly optimistic picture. The problem is not only valuation error: a culture of number-manufacturing is self-reinforcing, working its way down through an organization until "innocent fudge" becomes full-fledged fraud.

GAAP-rule-mandated distortions (unrealized investment gains forced through net income; acquisition-amortization requirements) are a related but distinct problem, handled under [gaap-distortion](gaap-distortion.md).

## Why it matters

Reported earnings are the primary signal most investors use to assess a business. When those figures are manipulated — even within GAAP — investors overpay, misjudge competitive strength, and misread management character. More insidiously, even corrective measures (operating earnings, "adjusted" figures) can themselves be shaded. The problem is not fixed by switching metrics; it requires independent judgment at every step.

## The costs that never disappear

Management-originated distortions take two recurring forms:

**Restructuring exclusions.** Berkshire has been restructuring since 1965. When "one-time" restructuring charges recur year after year, excluding them from "recurring" earnings is not an adjustment — it is a systematic omission of a real cost of doing business.

**Stock-based compensation.** "What else could it be — a gift from shareholders?" Compensation paid in equity dilutes owners as surely as a cash payment. The 2016 letter's accounting-laboratory illustration makes the absurdity concrete: relabeling cash pay as "cash paid in lieu of SBC" produces identically inflated adjusted figures. The relabeling changes nothing economic.

Abraham Lincoln's answer applies to both: if you call a dog's tail a leg, it still has four. Abe would have felt lonely on Wall Street.

## Depreciation vs. amortization: opposite distortions, not interchangeable

The most analytically precise contribution in the letter series is a distinction that runs counter to the standard "all non-cash charges are real" position:

**Acquisition-related amortization is a legitimate add-back.** Purchase accounting requires valuing and amortizing acquired intangibles — customer relationships, brand, non-competes. But where these assets retain their economic value, no real depletion is occurring; the charge is an accounting artifact. (Berkshire in 2018: $1.4B.)

**Depreciation of physical assets is not a legitimate add-back — it *understates* true economic maintenance cost** in capital-intensive businesses. Maintenance capex routinely exceeds the accounting depreciation charge, which is calculated on historical cost at acquisition time. Inflation and rising replacement costs compound this gap over decades. (Berkshire in 2018: $8.4B in GAAP depreciation that understated real maintenance requirements.)

EBITDA that adds back both depreciation and amortization without distinction conflates two opposite cases. The practical upshot: in acquisition-heavy businesses, GAAP earnings understate owner earnings (purchase-price amortization inflates non-cash charges above real maintenance); in infrastructure-intensive businesses, GAAP earnings *overstate* owner earnings (depreciation understates real replacement costs). See [owner-earnings](owner-earnings.md) for the full formula.

## No metric is immune: the four-rung hierarchy

The letters develop a hierarchy where each rung corrects a flaw in the one above — but each is still manipulable:

1. *GAAP net income* — distorted by rule-mandated items (see [gaap-distortion](gaap-distortion.md)) and management-chosen exclusions.
2. *"Adjusted earnings" / Adjusted EBITDA* — strips real recurring costs; misclassifies depreciation as a non-economic charge.
3. *Operating earnings* (Berkshire's preferred) — strips rule-mandated noise; closer to economic reality. But: "even the operating earnings figure that we favor can easily be manipulated by managers who wish to do so. … Bold imaginative accounting requires no talent — only a deep desire to deceive."
4. *Owner earnings* — Buffett's 1986 formula (reported earnings + non-cash charges − true maintenance capex); the closest approximation to economic cash generation, but requires independent capex estimation. See [owner-earnings](owner-earnings.md).

The honest analyst works down this hierarchy with independent judgment at each step rather than accepting any self-reported figure at face value.

## How it's discussed in sources

- [berkshire-2016-letter](../sources/berkshire-2016-letter.md) — catalogs the two management favorites: restructuring exclusions (recurring costs relabeled as one-time) and SBC exclusions (compensation treated as free); the accounting-laboratory illustration; "always makes the numbers" as a red flag; contagion of number-manufacturing culture downstream
- [berkshire-2017-letter](../sources/berkshire-2017-letter.md) — introduced the management-originated vs. rule-mandated framework for classifying earnings distortions; the FASB unrealized-gains rule itself is detailed in [gaap-distortion](gaap-distortion.md)
- [berkshire-2018-letter](../sources/berkshire-2018-letter.md) — Lincoln's dog-tail analogy as the sharpest rhetorical frame; the amortization/depreciation split as an analytical advance ($1.4B acquisition amortization a legitimate add-back; $8.4B GAAP depreciation an understatement of real cost); SBC distilled to a single parenthetical
- [berkshire-2021-letter](../sources/berkshire-2021-letter.md) — no new analytical content; the juxtaposition of BNSF's "old-fashioned sort of earnings" (after interest, taxes, depreciation, amortization, and all compensation) directly against the EBITDA critique is the series' clearest single teaching moment
- [berkshire-2022-letter](../sources/berkshire-2022-letter.md) — closes the logical gap: even operating earnings "can easily be manipulated by managers who wish to do so"; "bold imaginative accounting" is "one of the shames of capitalism"; completes the four-rung hierarchy
