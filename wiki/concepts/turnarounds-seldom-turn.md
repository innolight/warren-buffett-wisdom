# Turnarounds Seldom Turn

## Definition

Structurally broken businesses tend to stay broken, regardless of how cheaply they are acquired. Statistical cheapness (buying below book, below working capital, at low P/E) does not overcome structural defects. The same management effort invested in a sound business at a fair price produces far better outcomes than the same effort invested in a problem business at a bargain price.

Buffett's 1979 formulation: "Both our operating and investment experience cause us to conclude that 'turnarounds' seldom turn, and that the same energies and talent are much better employed in a good business purchased at a fair price than in a poor business purchased at a bargain price."

## The Waumbec case study

Buffett acquired Waumbec Mills in Manchester, New Hampshire to expand Berkshire's textile operations. By every statistical measure, the purchase was exceptional: the price was below working capital, and "in effect, got very substantial amounts of machinery and real estate for less than nothing."

The outcome: "While we labored mightily, new problems arose as fast as old problems were tamed." The original thesis did not prove out. The business remained structurally impaired despite energetic management.

The lesson Buffett draws is not that cheap is bad, but that **cheapness does not fix structure**. A below-working-capital price on a textile mill is a below-working-capital price on an inherently low-return, commodity-input, capital-intensive business with no pricing power — and that fundamental economics persists regardless of acquisition price.

## Why it matters

This concept sits at the intersection of two other frameworks:

- **[[tailwinds-vs-headwinds]]:** headwind businesses carry the problem structurally; management effort fights the tide rather than riding it.
- **[[acquisition-criteria]]:** criterion 2 (favorable long-term prospects) is not satisfied by cheapness. A broken business with a low price still fails the test.

The practical implication: **price is the last variable to negotiate, not the primary one**. A great business at a fair price dominates a poor business at a spectacular price — not just in outcomes, but in the quality of work required to generate those outcomes.

## The franchise-vs-turnaround distinction (1980)

The 1980 GEICO discussion sharpens the principle by identifying a class of situations that *look like* turnarounds but aren't — and where Buffett's caution would mislead.

> GEICO's problems at that time put it in a position analogous to that of American Express in 1964 following the salad oil scandal. Both were one-of-a-kind companies, temporarily reeling from the effects of a fiscal blow that did not destroy their exceptional underlying economics. The GEICO and American Express situations, extraordinary business franchises with a localized excisable cancer (needing, to be sure, a skilled surgeon), should be distinguished from the true "turnaround" situation in which the managers expect — and need — to pull off a corporate Pygmalion. ([[berkshire-1980-letter]])

The decision rule: **does the underlying economic franchise survive the crisis intact, or does it not?** Waumbec's textile economics were always going to be poor — there was no franchise to rescue. GEICO's direct-distribution cost advantage was structurally intact throughout the 1970s crisis — the franchise needed surgery (a skilled CEO, capital), not reinvention.

So "turnarounds seldom turn" is not "never invest after a crisis." It's "invest after a crisis only when the underlying economic franchise was always sound and the damage is localized and fixable."

## The 1983 corollary on capital expenditure

The 1983 owner's manual makes the rule operational at the *retention* level: "We react with great caution to suggestions that our poor businesses can be restored to satisfactory profitability by major capital expenditures. (The projections will be dazzling — the advocates will be sincere — but, in the end, major additional investment in a terrible industry usually is about as rewarding as struggling in quicksand.)"

The corollary: even when management is honest and competent, throwing capital at structurally poor economics rarely overcomes them. The same principle that prohibits buying a turnaround prohibits funding one from within. See [[dividend-policy]].
