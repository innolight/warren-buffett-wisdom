# Turnarounds Seldom Turn

**Structurally broken businesses tend to stay broken regardless of how cheaply they are acquired — and the same management effort in a sound business at a fair price produces far better outcomes.**

## Actionable takeaway

- **Screen structural economics before screening price.** Ask: would this business earn good returns at a fair price? If not, a deep discount changes nothing — it only makes the entry look attractive on a spreadsheet.
- **Distinguish localized damage from structural impairment.** A franchise temporarily hurt by a solvable crisis (intact cost advantage, fixable financial structure) is not a turnaround — it is a franchise rescue. Only invest after a crisis when the underlying economic franchise was always sound and the damage is excisable.
- **Opportunity cost is the real measure.** The same management energy deployed in a good business at a fair price dominates the same energy in a problem business at any price.
- **The corollary holds inside the portfolio.** Projections promising to restore poor-economics businesses through capital expenditure should be treated with the same skepticism as the original acquisition case.

## Definition

Statistical cheapness — buying below book, below working capital, at a low P/E — does not overcome structural defects. A below-working-capital price on a textile mill is still a price paid for an inherently low-return, commodity-input, capital-intensive business with no pricing power. The fundamental economics persist regardless of entry price.

Buffett's 1979 formulation: "Both our operating and investment experience cause us to conclude that 'turnarounds' seldom turn, and that the same energies and talent are much better employed in a good business purchased at a fair price than in a poor business purchased at a bargain price." ([berkshire-1979-letter](../sources/berkshire-1979-letter.md))

## Why it matters

This concept sits at the intersection of two other frameworks:

- **[tailwinds-vs-headwinds](tailwinds-vs-headwinds.md):** headwind businesses carry the structural problem built in; management effort fights the tide rather than riding it.
- **[acquisition-criteria](acquisition-criteria.md):** criterion 2 (favorable long-term prospects) is not satisfied by cheapness. A broken business with a low price still fails the test.

Price is the last variable to negotiate, not the first. A great business at a fair price dominates a poor business at a spectacular price — not just in outcomes, but in the quality of the work required to generate those outcomes.

## Cheapness cannot fix broken structure

Berkshire acquired Waumbec Mills in Manchester, New Hampshire to expand its textile operations. By every statistical measure the purchase was exceptional: the price was below working capital and Buffett "got very substantial amounts of machinery and real estate for less than nothing."

The outcome: "While we labored mightily, new problems arose as fast as old problems were tamed." The business remained structurally impaired despite energetic management. The lesson Buffett draws is not that cheap is bad, but that cheapness does not fix structure. A below-working-capital price on a textile mill is still a price paid for commodity-input, capital-intensive economics with no pricing power — and that persists regardless of what was paid.

## Injured franchises are not turnarounds

The 1980 GEICO discussion sharpens the principle by identifying a class of situations that *look like* turnarounds but are not — and where the caution would mislead:

> GEICO's problems at that time put it in a position analogous to that of American Express in 1964 following the salad oil scandal. Both were one-of-a-kind companies, temporarily reeling from the effects of a fiscal blow that did not destroy their exceptional underlying economics. The GEICO and American Express situations, extraordinary business franchises with a localized excisable cancer (needing, to be sure, a skilled surgeon), should be distinguished from the true "turnaround" situation in which the managers expect — and need — to pull off a corporate Pygmalion. ([berkshire-1980-letter](../sources/berkshire-1980-letter.md))

The diagnostic test: **does the underlying economic franchise survive the crisis intact, or does it not?** Waumbec's textile economics were always going to be poor — there was no franchise to rescue. GEICO's direct-distribution cost advantage was structurally intact throughout its 1970s near-bankruptcy — it needed surgery (a skilled CEO, capital), not reinvention. "Turnarounds seldom turn" is not "never invest after a crisis." It is "invest after a crisis only when the underlying economic franchise was always sound and the damage is localized and fixable."

## The corollary: the same logic governs capital reinvestment

The same principle that counsels against buying a turnaround also counsels against funding one from within. Buffett's 1983 formulation: "We react with great caution to suggestions that our poor businesses can be restored to satisfactory profitability by major capital expenditures. (The projections will be dazzling — the advocates will be sincere — but, in the end, major additional investment in a terrible industry usually is about as rewarding as struggling in quicksand.)" ([berkshire-1983-letter](../sources/berkshire-1983-letter.md))

Even when management is honest and competent, throwing capital at structurally poor economics rarely overcomes them. See [dividend-policy](dividend-policy.md) for the retention-side implications.
