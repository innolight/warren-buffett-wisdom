# Return on Equity as the Managerial Yardstick

## Definition

The argument that **return on beginning equity capital**, not earnings per share, is the right measure of managerial economic performance. EPS growth is misleading when equity itself is growing through retained earnings — a totally dormant savings account produces steadily rising interest each year purely through compounding.

## Why it matters

This is the framing Buffett opens the 1977 letter with, and it carries through every later letter. It puts capital allocation at the center of management evaluation: a CEO who grows EPS 5% while equity grows 10% has destroyed value relative to the compounding baseline. The metric strips out the artifact of retention and forces a return-on-capital lens.

## The "stopped clock" corollary (1979)

The 1979 letter strengthens the anti-EPS argument with a striking image. A company that retains all earnings produces rising EPS automatically — even if the underlying business is dormant. "'Earnings per share' will rise constantly on a dormant savings account or on a U.S. Savings Bond bearing a fixed rate of return simply because 'earnings' (the stated interest rate) are continuously plowed back and added to the capital base. Thus, even a 'stopped clock' can look like a growth stock if the dividend payout ratio is low."

The implication: consistently rising EPS, unaccompanied by a corresponding ROE, is noise. It signals capital retention, not value creation.

## The long-run record (1979)

Buffett reports the cumulative score: book value per share compounded at 20.5% annually from September 1964 ($19.46) to yearend 1979 ($335.85). This figure is *higher* than any single-year operating ROE calculation because it incorporates capital appreciation from equity investments — a reminder that reported operating ROE understates long-run performance when the equity portfolio is compounding unrealized gains.

But the 1979 letter immediately introduces the caveat: even 20.5% compounded nominal returns may produce near-zero real returns in a 14% inflation environment. See [[investor-misery-index]].

## How it's discussed in sources

- [[berkshire-1977-letter]] — opening framing; 1977 operating ROE of 19% on beginning equity. Caveat: "Except for special cases (for example, companies with unusual debt-equity ratios or those with important assets carried at unrealistic balance sheet values)" — so it's not a universal metric, just the default.
- [[berkshire-1978-letter]] — 1978 operating ROE of 19.4%, "within a fraction of our 1972 record"; emphasis on the difference between measuring ROE on cost-basis equity vs. market-basis equity (the latter inflates the denominator and distorts comparisons).
- [[berkshire-1979-letter]] — "stopped clock" corollary; long-run 20.5% compound since 1964; explicit acknowledgment that nominal ROE may produce zero real returns in a high-inflation environment.
