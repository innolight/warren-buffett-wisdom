# Return on Equity as the Managerial Yardstick

**Return on beginning equity capital — not earnings per share — is the right scorecard for managerial economic performance, because EPS can rise mechanically from retained earnings alone without any value being created.**

## Actionable takeaway

- **Replace EPS growth with ROE on beginning equity.** When evaluating a management team or screening a business, anchor on return on beginning-year equity capital. Rising EPS in isolation is uninformative — it is the compounding artifact of any retained-earnings base, even a dormant one.
- **Run the stopped-clock diagnostic.** If EPS rises but ROE is flat or declining, the company is retaining capital at a below-cost rate. Value is being destroyed even as the headline number climbs.
- **Watch the denominator.** ROE calculated on cost-basis (historical) book equity is comparable across years and businesses. ROE calculated on market-value equity deflates the ratio for any business trading above book — the distortion is worst when comparing businesses bought at different premiums to book.
- **Treat nominal ROE as a ceiling, not a result.** In inflationary environments, the inflation rate plus tax cost on nominal gains (the investor's misery index) can absorb the entire headline return. A 20% nominal ROE at 14% inflation produces near-zero real purchasing-power gain. See [investor-misery-index](investor-misery-index.md) for the full arithmetic.

## Definition

Return on beginning equity capital measures what a business earns on the capital shareholders had invested at the start of the year. It strips out the compounding artifact of retained earnings: any company that plows earnings back into its equity base will report rising EPS the following year — not because the business improved, but because the base grew.

Buffett's framing (1977): "Even a totally dormant savings account will produce steadily rising interest earnings each year because of compounding." The ROE lens eliminates this illusion by asking: what return did we actually earn on the capital employed?

One caveat stated from the start: the yardstick doesn't apply cleanly to companies with unusual debt-to-equity ratios or with important assets carried at unrealistic balance-sheet values. ([berkshire-1977-letter](../sources/berkshire-1977-letter.md))

## Why it matters

Capital allocation sits at the center of managerial evaluation. A CEO who grows EPS 5% while equity grows 10% has destroyed value relative to the compounding baseline — retained earnings earned less than their cost. The ROE lens makes this honest: it penalizes growth-for-growth's-sake and rewards genuine value creation.

## The stopped-clock failure mode: EPS growth without ROE

A company that retains all earnings produces rising EPS automatically, even if the underlying business is dormant. "'Earnings per share' will rise constantly on a dormant savings account or on a U.S. Savings Bond bearing a fixed rate of return simply because 'earnings' (the stated interest rate) are continuously plowed back and added to the capital base. Thus, even a 'stopped clock' can look like a growth stock if the dividend payout ratio is low." ([berkshire-1979-letter](../sources/berkshire-1979-letter.md))

Consistently rising EPS unaccompanied by a corresponding ROE is noise: it signals capital retention, not value creation. The diagnostic is the gap between EPS growth rate and equity growth rate — when equity grows faster, management is compounding at below the retention rate.

## The denominator problem: cost-basis vs. market-value equity

ROE comparisons break down when the denominator is inconsistent. Buffett reports Berkshire's ROE on cost-basis (historical book) equity — a stable, year-over-year-comparable figure. Inflating the denominator to market value depresses the apparent ROE whenever the stock trades above book, making a superbly efficient business look like an ordinary one.

The practical trap: two businesses earning identical absolute returns on similar asset bases can produce wildly different ROEs depending on how the denominator was constructed — especially when one was acquired at a large premium to book. Verify the denominator before comparing. ([berkshire-1978-letter](../sources/berkshire-1978-letter.md))

## The real-return ceiling: nominal ROE is not purchasing-power gain

Even a sustained 20%-plus ROE can produce near-zero real returns in a high-inflation environment — once taxes on nominal gains are factored in, a 14% inflation rate can consume the entire margin. This is the next layer of rigor above raw ROE measurement: the [investor-misery-index](investor-misery-index.md) (inflation + tax cost on nominal returns) must be cleared before any real wealth is created.
