# Acquisition Criteria

## Definition

Buffett's four-point test for evaluating an investment — applied identically to whole-company acquisitions and to purchases of marketable equity securities. The business must be:

1. **One that we can understand.**
2. **With favorable long-term prospects.**
3. **Operated by honest and competent people.**
4. **Available at a very attractive price.**

## Why it matters

This is the operating definition of "a Berkshire investment." Two implications worth holding onto:

- **Symmetry between whole and partial ownership.** "We select our marketable equity securities in much the same way we would evaluate a business for acquisition in its entirety." This is the philosophical bridge from private-business investing to public-market investing — and the reason Buffett can frame stocks as "pro-rata portions of … businesses."
- **The bargain in non-control.** Outstanding businesses "sometimes sell in the securities markets at very large discounts from the prices they would command in negotiated transactions involving entire companies." So a disciplined buyer can sometimes do better in public markets than in M&A.

## Concentration as a corollary

The 1978 letter adds an explicit portfolio-sizing principle: "We try to avoid buying a little of this or that when we are only lukewarm about the business or its price. When we are convinced as to attractiveness, we believe in buying worthwhile amounts."

Criterion 4 (attractive price) is not merely a threshold to clear — it is the signal to act with conviction. A lukewarm application of the criteria produces diversification without the benefits of concentrated ownership. The SAFECO position illustrates the outcome when all four criteria are met: Berkshire bought the best-run large P&C insurer in the country *at below book value*, while inferior companies in the same industry were selling for premiums in negotiated transactions.

## How it's discussed in sources

- [[berkshire-1977-letter]] — first formulation; introduced in the "Insurance Investments" section as the rationale for treating marketable equities as business-ownership decisions, not stock-price bets
