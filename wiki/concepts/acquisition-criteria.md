# Acquisition Criteria

**The discipline for evaluating whole businesses and partial equity stakes: a stable six-point filter, the three failure modes it resists, and a permanent-capital structure that turns the criteria themselves into a competitive moat.**

## Actionable takeaway

- **Run every candidate through the six-point filter first.** Large; demonstrated (not projected) earning power; good returns on equity with little debt; management in place; understandable business; offered at a specific price. A "no" on any point stops the process.
- **Publish what you want, then wait.** Announcing stable criteria publicly is itself a screening mechanism — the right sellers self-select in; the wrong ones never call.
- **Be suspicious of your own excitement.** All three failure modes (animal spirits, size worship, Pygmalion fantasy) feel like conviction from inside. If your corporate pulse is beating fast, treat that as a warning, not a green light.
- **Read the seller.** An owner who built the business, cares about its culture, and isn't maximizing price is signaling honest accounting and an intact operation. An auction-bound seller is signaling the opposite.
- **Pay cash.** Issuing shares transfers value from continuing shareholders to sellers unless your stock trades at or above intrinsic value. When in doubt, pay cash.
- **Concentrate when convinced.** Buying "a little of this or that" when lukewarm proves weakness of conviction, not prudence.

## Definition

Buffett's test for evaluating an investment — applied identically to whole-company acquisitions and to purchases of marketable equity securities.

**The four-point principle (1977):** the business must be (1) understandable, (2) with favorable long-term prospects, (3) operated by honest and competent people, (4) available at an attractive price.

**The six-point operational ad (first published 1982):**

1. Large purchases (at first publication: ≥$5M after-tax earnings; the threshold has risen with Berkshire's capital base).
2. Demonstrated consistent earning power — no projections, no turnarounds.
3. Good returns on equity while employing little or no debt.
4. Management in place (Berkshire cannot supply it).
5. Simple businesses (if there's lots of technology, we won't understand it).
6. An offering price (no preliminary talks without one).

Plus: no unfriendly transactions; complete confidentiality; "very fast answer — customarily within five minutes."

## Why it matters

Two implications of the four-point test are worth carrying:

- **Symmetry between whole and partial ownership.** Buffett applies the same criteria to buying 100% of a company or 1% via public markets. "Outstanding businesses sometimes sell in the securities markets at very large discounts from the prices they would command in negotiated transactions involving entire companies." The disciplined buyer can sometimes do better in public markets than in M&A auctions — the inverse of the [stock-as-acquisition-currency](stock-as-acquisition-currency.md) problem.
- **Price is the binding constraint, not a threshold.** Criterion 4 (attractive price) is not merely a box to clear — it is the signal to act with conviction. A great business at a poor price fails the criteria just as surely as a weak one.

## The three failure modes behind value-destroying acquisitions

The 1981 letter sharpens the discipline by inverting it — cataloging the motivations that make capable managers overpay:

1. **Animal spirits.** Leaders relish increased activity. "At Berkshire, the corporate pulse never beats faster than when an acquisition is in prospect." Excitement is a warning signal, not a green light.
2. **Size as the dominant yardstick.** Fortune-500 managers know their sales rank, rarely their profitability rank. Acquisitions that grow revenue while destroying per-share value satisfy this instinct perfectly.
3. **The Pygmalion fantasy.** "Their managerial kiss will turn this toad into a prince." Buffett: "We've observed many kisses but very few miracles."

## The six-point filter as a self-selecting advertisement

Published verbatim every year since 1982, the six-point ad functions as a filter in both directions: Berkshire rejects what doesn't qualify, and sellers who don't qualify self-screen out. By 1999, the result was the "carefully-crafted acquisition strategy": simply waiting for the phone to ring.

Repeating the criteria unchanged, year after year, is deliberate. "Repetition is a key to results." Sellers reading consecutive letters verify that the criteria are stable — not negotiable, not adjusted to Berkshire's current deal appetite. That consistency is itself a form of trust that no intermediated auction process can manufacture.

The operating mandate that makes the criteria credible once a deal closes — running each business as if the manager owns 100% and cannot sell for a century — is housed on [owner-orientation](owner-orientation.md).

## Permanence as the competitive moat

The dimension absent from the original six-point ad but central to Berkshire's M&A advantage: "Unlike LBO operators and private equity firms, we have no 'exit' strategy — we buy to keep. That's one reason why Berkshire is usually the first — and sometimes the only — choice for sellers and their managers." ([berkshire-2002-letter](../sources/berkshire-2002-letter.md))

A seller who built a culture and protected people will often accept a lower price from a permanent buyer than a higher one from an operator planning to re-engineer and exit. Berkshire's structure — no leverage, no fund life, no LP return schedule — is itself the moat. The 2006 TTI acquisition crystallizes the logic: the founder explained his choice plainly — a strategic buyer would pursue synergies and dismantle what he built; PE would load it with debt and flip it. That left Berkshire, no banker, no auction. See [berkshire-2006-letter](../sources/berkshire-2006-letter.md).

## Seller character as a quality signal

An owner who built a business, cares about its culture, and isn't maximizing price signals honest accounting, pride of product, and a loyal workforce. The reverse holds: when owners auction their business showing no interest in what follows, it has often been "dressed up for sale." Seller disposition is a leading indicator of business quality. ([berkshire-2000-letter](../sources/berkshire-2000-letter.md))

The McLane acquisition (2003) — agreed in a single two-hour meeting with no formal due diligence — is the operational proof: "We knew everything would be exactly as Wal-Mart said it would be — and it was." Trust eliminates verification friction. See [berkshire-2003-letter](../sources/berkshire-2003-letter.md).

## Concentration as a corollary

Conviction demands position size. "We try to avoid buying a little of this or that when we are only lukewarm about the business or its price. When we are convinced as to attractiveness, we believe in buying worthwhile amounts." ([berkshire-1978-letter](../sources/berkshire-1978-letter.md)) The Billy Rose formulation from 1984: "If you have a harem of forty women, you never get to know any of them very well."
