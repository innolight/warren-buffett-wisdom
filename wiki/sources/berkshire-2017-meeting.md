# Berkshire Hathaway 2017 Annual Meeting

Held May 6, 2017, at the CenturyLink Center in Omaha — and livestreamed for the second consecutive year — the 2017 meeting is dominated by four interlocking live developments, each producing some of the sharpest formulations in the corpus. Buffett's new Apple stake forced a public reckoning with what "circle of competence" actually means in the age of the smartphone consumer franchise — and with the painful admissions that Berkshire was a GEICO customer of Google from the beginning and never bought a share of Google or Amazon despite understanding both. The Wells Fargo fake-accounts scandal was in full swing, producing the most complete exposition in the meeting corpus on why institutional failure is always a failure of CEO action, not incentive system design alone. The $90B-plus cash pile and an aborted $143B Unilever bid by Kraft Heinz sharpened the recurring tension between Berkshire's liquidity-fortress philosophy and the mounting burden of proof on management when capital sits idle. And Buffett's diagnosis of healthcare costs as "the tapeworm of American economic competitiveness" — supported by the statistic that medical costs went from 5% to 17% of GDP while corporate taxes fell from 4% to 2% — may be the most quotable policy observation in the entire meeting corpus. The meeting is also the transcript where Munger, at 93, delivers "my God, we're still learning" as the encapsulation of Berkshire's durable edge. This page keeps the wiki's two-layer convention: a distilled thematic top, then a comprehensive ★-marked Q&A digest as the home for detail.

## How to read the Q&A digest

- **★** marks a timeless, load-bearing exchange worth citing in the future; unmarked entries still carry investor value but are secondary or period-specific.
- Answers are attributed separately to **Buffett** and **Munger** — a Munger line appears only when he added a distinct lesson (he said "Nothing to add" to most).
- **M#/A#** map to the numbered sections in the raw morning/afternoon transcript for traceability.

## Key themes

### Apple is a consumer franchise — and that is a different kind of circle

For years Buffett declined tech stocks on the grounds he lacked an edge in predicting their competitive future. Apple changed his framing. The iPhone, he concluded, is not a technology product to be analyzed through a technology lens; it is a consumer franchise product where the relevant question is behavioral: will people want this next year and the year after, and how high are the switching costs? "I think Apple is much more of a consumer products business, in terms of … sort of analyzing moats around it, and consumer behavior." The same intellectual toolkit that valued Coke and See's — revealed consumer loyalty, pricing power derived from habit rather than feature comparison — transfers to a device whose users store their lives on it. This is not "tech investing"; it is an extension of the consumer-franchise method into a new product form. The honest corollary: Buffett simultaneously acknowledged IBM was a different, harder kind of analysis — predicting which enterprise technology stacks and cloud services would win — and admitted he was wrong. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [apple](../entities/apple.md). *(M12, A12)*

### Google and Amazon: the cost of watching from the sidelines

The 2017 meeting contains the most explicit admissions of omission in the corpus. Berkshire was an early GEICO advertiser on Google, paying "$10 or $11 a click" from the beginning, watching the economics of zero-marginal-cost search advertising in real time — and never bought a share. "I think we were smart enough to figure out Google," Munger said; "so I would say that we failed you there." Amazon was even more explicit: "Because I was too dumb to realize what was going to happen," Buffett said. He had read Bezos's 1997 letter and admired the man, but "I did not think that he could succeed on the scale he has." On building two dominant businesses from a standing start simultaneously — retail and cloud — Buffett asked Munger if he could think of another case. Munger could not, and called Bezos "a different species." The lesson is not that the circle should never expand; it is that the cost of a circle-of-competence error is not just the stocks you sell — it is the ones you never buy despite having unusually good information. See [mistakes-of-omission](../concepts/mistakes-of-omission.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md). *(M12, A15)*

### Wells Fargo: bad incentives plus inaction equals institutional failure

The Wells Fargo fake-accounts scandal distilled a principle that goes beyond any single bank: bad incentive systems are predictable and correctable; CEO inaction when the problem surfaces is neither. Buffett's diagnosis was precise. First, the incentive design error: rewarding "services per customer" was the wrong metric, and people were paid and promoted based on it. But "we've made similar mistakes" — an honest admission that bad metrics are common. The decisive factor was the second error: "the CEO has to act" the moment the problem is known. The Salomon parallel was explicit — CEO Gutfreund knew about Paul Mozer's Treasury fraud on April 28, said he would report it, didn't, and a month later Mozer did it again. "A pound of cure, promptly applied, is worth a ton of cure that's delayed." The third error — measuring seriousness by the dollar fine ($185M "signaled a less offensive practice than something involved two billion" — "they were totally wrong on that") — rounds out the anatomy. Berkshire's own system: a hotline receiving ~4,000 annual contacts, reviewed by internal audit chief Becki Amick, with anything serious escalating to Buffett directly, has uncovered real problems. The architecture of the system matters less than what happens at the top when a problem surfaces. See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md), [wells-fargo](../entities/wells-fargo.md), [institutional-imperative](../concepts/institutional-imperative.md). *(M6)*

### Capital-light tech giants: a structural shift in what capitalism requires

A shareholder's question about why Berkshire now buys capital-intensive businesses (BNSF, BHE) rather than capital-light ones produced the meeting's sharpest observation on the structural transformation of the U.S. economy. The five largest U.S. companies by market cap at the time of the meeting — approximately $2.5 trillion in aggregate — could "be run with no equity capital at all. None." This is "a very different world than when Andrew Carnegie was building a steel mill and using the earnings to build another steel mill." For the first century of American industrial capitalism, "growing and earning large amounts of money required considerable reinvestment of capital." The platform era changed the equation: enormous market value creation without tangible assets, at speed. "Andrew Mellon would be absolutely baffled." The investment implication: Berkshire loves capital-light, high-return businesses but can no longer buy them at scale; forced into capital-intensive businesses "earning 11–12% on capital versus the 100%-per-annum returns of early acquisitions," it accepts the trade-off with open eyes. See [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md), [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md). *(M23)*

### Healthcare as the real tapeworm of U.S. competitiveness

Buffett's most statistically precise policy observation: in 1960, corporate taxes were ~4% of GDP; now ~2%. Healthcare costs were ~5% of GDP then; they are ~17% now. "So when American business talks about taxes strangling our competitiveness, they're talking about something that, as a percentage of GDP, has gone down from four to two, while medical costs, which are borne to a great extent by business, have gone from five to 17 percent." The comparable countries that began near 5% are now at 10–11%, giving them a five-to-six-point competitiveness advantage. "Medical costs are the tapeworm of American economic competitiveness." Munger's complement: "A lot of it is deeply immoral. If you have a group of hospital people and doctors that are feasting like a bunch of jackals on the carcass of some dying person, it's not a pretty sight." The Redding, California cardiologist story — a hospital system that recommended heart surgery for every patient, developed enormous volume with excellent "outcomes" (because people who don't need surgery recover well), and was then held up by management as a model for other hospitals — is the most vivid illustration of incentive-induced institutional delusion in the corpus. "They thought that what they were doing was good for people. That is why it's so hard to fix these things." *(A23)*

### The $90B cash pile and the honest admission of pressure

With cash and T-bills approaching $100 billion against float of $105 billion, Buffett delivered the most candid statement of the burden-of-proof principle in the corpus: "There is no way I can come back here three years from now and tell you that we hold $150 billion or so in cash or more, and we think we're doing something brilliant by doing it." The arithmetic was damning on its own terms: "We've got almost a hundred billion invested in a business where we're paying almost a hundred times earnings. And it's kind of a lousy business." The Kraft Heinz/Unilever bid had been real — $15B of additional Berkshire equity committed informally — but had died when Unilever rejected a friendly approach. Buffett was "still optimistic enough about deploying the capital" to resist raising the buyback threshold above 1.2× book, but acknowledged this optimism rested on historical faith rather than a live opportunity. Munger: "The answer is maybe." The episode crystallizes the honest cost of the liquidity-fortress principle: optionality has carrying costs. See [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md), [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md). *(M2, A2, A8)*

## Q&A digest — Morning session

**M2 · Q1 earnings: float surges, GEICO hits record policy growth.** *Buffett:* float grew $14 billion in Q1 alone (total float now above $100B); GEICO added 700,000 net policyholders in the first four months, the highest figure in memory, in part because major competitors publicly announced they were pulling back on new business to avoid first-year underwriting losses. Berkshire's response: "we just put our foot to the floor." Unrealized gains in the portfolio exceed $90B, making reported earnings malleable but irrelevant. Preference this year to realize losses before year-end in case corporate tax rates fall (reducing the tax benefit of losses). See [float](../concepts/float.md), [geico](../entities/geico.md).

**★ M3 · Tribute to Jack Bogle: "saved tens and tens of billions for investors."** *Buffett:* introduced Jack Bogle (present in the audience) as "the man who has probably done more for the American investor than any man in the country." The index fund was against Wall Street's interests, was derided at launch, and now represents trillions of assets at a few basis points of cost versus hundreds of basis points elsewhere. Buffett estimated Bogle has, at minimum, left tens of billions — and eventually hundreds of billions — in investors' pockets without any sacrifice of gross performance. The principle: in investment management, the question is never only about gross return; the net return after fees is all that matters for the client, and a product that guarantees market-minus-near-zero dominates market-minus-100-200bp structurally. See [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md), [investment-friction](../concepts/investment-friction.md).

**★ M6 · Wells Fargo: three mistakes, one fatal.** *Buffett:* catalogued the failure with surgical precision. Mistake one: an incentive system rewarding "services per customer" that predictably generated the wrong behavior (shared error — "we've made similar mistakes"). Mistake two — dwarfs the others — not acting when the CEO learned about it. The Salomon analogy: [Gutfreund](../people/john-gutfreund.md) was told April 28, said he'd report it to the Fed, didn't, and a month later the same employee committed the same fraud again. "A pound of cure, promptly applied, is worth a ton of cure that's delayed." Mistake three: measuring the seriousness of the problem by the size of the fine ($185M), dramatically underestimating public reaction. Berkshire's countermeasure: ~4,000 hotline contacts annually, reviewed by Becki Amick, with serious items escalated directly. "Problems don't go away." *Munger:* "An ounce of prevention is worth a pound of cure — he [Franklin] understated it." *Supersession flag:* despite this critique, Berkshire continued to hold WFC as its largest equity position; it later reduced the stake dramatically starting in 2020 as the scandal's institutional depth became clearer. See [wells-fargo](../entities/wells-fargo.md), [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md), [institutional-imperative](../concepts/institutional-imperative.md).

**M7 · Autonomous vehicles: threat to both BNSF and GEICO.** *Buffett:* driverless trucks would narrow the cost advantage of rail over trucking; driverless cars, if they succeed, do so by reducing accidents — which directly reduces GEICO's premium income. Both businesses would be hurt by widespread autonomous-vehicle adoption. Personal view: autonomous vehicles "will certainly come" but "may be a long way off." Honest about the dependence on early real-world performance data rather than test results. See [bnsf](../entities/bnsf.md), [geico](../entities/geico.md).

**M8 · See's Candies: the first "wonderful business" and what it taught.** *Buffett:* recounts buying See's in 1972 for $25M net of cash (then earning $4M pretax; has produced close to $2B in cumulative pretax earnings). The test: would people still give and eat this candy over alternatives? Not a low-bid purchase. Key insight: if Munger had not persuaded the seller (who "was more interested in girls and grapes") to sell, Buffett would have walked at $5M more — so in retrospect the decision to buy was narrower than the legend suggests. *Munger:* "We were very lucky that, early, the habit of buying horrible businesses because they were really cheap gave us a lot of experience trying to fix unfixable businesses as they headed downward toward doom. And that early experience was so horrible, fixing the unfixable, that we were very good at avoiding it, thereafter." The learning: you cannot make a silk purse out of a sow's ear, but you need to learn that by trying. See [sees-candies](../entities/sees-candies.md), [turnarounds-seldom-turn](../concepts/turnarounds-seldom-turn.md).

**★ M9 · "There are going to be marauders" — the moat philosophy.** *Buffett:* addressed concerns about Wells Fargo, AmEx, United, and Coca-Cola simultaneously: "If you've got a wonderful business, you basically have an economic castle. And in capitalism, people are going to try and take away that castle from you. So you want a moat around it... and you want a knight in the castle that's pretty darn good at warding off marauders. But there are going to be marauders. And they'll never go away." Distinguishes between transient competitive pressure (expected, survivable) and secular obsolescence (requires reassessment). (Airlines are mentioned only in passing here as "much more of an industry thought"; the detailed aviation thesis is at M13.) See [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**M10 · AIG retroactive reinsurance deal: $10.2B for up to $20B exposure.** *Buffett:* AIG transferred to Berkshire the liability for 80% of losses above $25B on business written before year-end 2015, in exchange for $10.2B. Berkshire's edge: it was "almost certainly the only insurance operation in the world" that AIG would have trusted to honor a $20B commitment decades into the future. Munger: "He means 'one.'" Conservative projections on payout timing; optimism dampened by the fact that the float sits in a near-zero rate environment. The deal is less attractive at near-zero rates because the incremental $10.2B "is earning us peanuts at the moment." See [float](../concepts/float.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md), [national-indemnity](../entities/national-indemnity.md).

**★ M11 · "A life properly lived is just learn, learn, learn."** *Munger:* reflecting on the greatest business Berkshire ever encountered: "The one that probably did us the most good as a learning experience was See's Candy. It's just the power of the brand, the unending flow of ever-increasing money with no work." Then: "I'm not sure we would have bought the Coca-Cola if we hadn't bought the See's." The principle: "A life properly lived is just learn, learn, learn all the time." And the compounding of experience: "Every time you appoint a new person that's never had big capital allocation experience, it's like rolling the dice. And I think we're way better off having done it so long." *Munger:* "The first rule of fishing is to fish where the fish are. And the second rule of fishing is to never forget the first rule." See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md).

**★ M12 · IBM, Google, and Amazon: one error, two admissions, one rethink.** *Buffett:* IBM investment (six years old at the time) had not met expectations: "I thought it would do better." Sold roughly one-third of Berkshire's position shortly before the meeting. Viewed IBM as a harder analytical problem — predicting which enterprise technology stacks and cloud players would win — than Apple, which he sees as a consumer-franchise analysis. "I regard them as being in quite different businesses." On Google: "We were their customer very early on with GEICO... we were paying them $10 or $11 a click... any time you're paying somebody $10 or $11 bucks every time somebody just punches a little thing where you've got no cost at all, you know, that's a good business." Berkshire never bought a share. "I blew it." On Bezos simultaneously building retail and AWS from a standing start: "I asked Charlie whether he could think of a situation like it, where one person has built an extraordinary economic machine in two really pretty different industries, almost simultaneously." *Munger:* "I think we were smart enough to figure out Google. … So I would say that we failed you there." *Supersession flag:* Buffett sold virtually all IBM shares in 2017, completing the exit; the Apple stake has become Berkshire's largest equity position. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [mistakes-of-omission](../concepts/mistakes-of-omission.md), [apple](../entities/apple.md).

**★ M13 · Airlines: not a cinch, but the conditions have changed.** *Buffett:* defends the ~$10B four-airline position ("we're the largest holder of the four largest airlines") against skepticism. Honest about history: "If anybody'd really been thinking about investors, they should have had Wilbur Wright shoot Orville down." The industry had "suicidally competitive" economics through ~100 airline bankruptcies. The change: operating at 80%+ of available seat miles, stable labor from post-bankruptcy bargaining, likely pilot shortage, and all four carriers buying back stock at low multiples. "If the airline companies are only worth, five or 10 years from now, what they're worth now, in terms of equity, we'll get a pretty reasonable rate of return, because they're going to buy in a lot of stock." Acknowledgment: "It is no cinch, by a long shot." *Munger:* "The railroads were a terrible business for decades and decades and decades, and then they got good." *Supersession flag:* Berkshire sold its entire airline portfolio in April 2020 at significant losses when COVID-19 destroyed air travel demand, with Buffett admitting "I was wrong about that" at the 2020 meeting. See [commodity-business-economics](../concepts/commodity-business-economics.md), [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md).

**★ M15 · Intrinsic value depends on interest rates above all else.** *Buffett:* estimated Berkshire has compounded intrinsic value at ~10% over the prior decade, "but I think that's going to be tough to achieve, in fact almost impossible to achieve, if we continued in this interest rate environment." If asked for one statistic before forecasting Berkshire's next 10 years: not GDP growth, not presidential outcome, but the 10-year average interest rate. Comfortable with 10% as aspirational given "somewhat higher rates than the last seven years." Reference to Japan: "25 years ago, we couldn't see how their interest rates could be sustained. And we're still looking at the same thing." *Munger:* "The future, with our present size, is, in terms of percentages of rates of return, going to be less glorious than our past. And we keep saying that. And now we're proving it." And: "A lot of other people are trying to be brilliant. And we're just trying to stay rational. It's a big advantage. Trying to be brilliant is dangerous, particularly when you're gambling." See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [bonds-and-inflation](../concepts/bonds-and-inflation.md).

**M16 · Corporate tax reform: who benefits depends on industry.** *Buffett:* utility customers receive 100% of any tax-rate reduction (commissions compute returns on after-tax equity; they would claw back the benefit). The $90B+ of unrealized gains in securities: shareholders receive 100% of any rate change, dollar for dollar. For operating businesses: some benefit gets competed away, some stays with shareholders — it is "very industry and company specific." Tax reform does not change Berkshire's investment or operational decisions: "I've never instructed anybody to do anything different because of investment tax credits or accelerated depreciation." Exception: renewables projects are tax-credit-dependent and represent deliberate public policy. *(This entry also folds raw M21 — "tax law changes don't change our decisions.")* See [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md).

**★ M18 · Why Buffett tells his wife to own an index fund, not Berkshire.** *Buffett:* all Berkshire shares will go to philanthropy at death — his wife will hold no Berkshire. For her, the S&P index provides certainty that she "will have all the money she possibly can use" with no one able to pressure her to change course. "The important thing is that she never worries about money the rest of her life." The deeper point: "I've seen too many people, as they get older, being susceptible and just having to listen to the arguments of people coming along." A single large holding invites well-meaning (and not-so-well-meaning) suggestions; a diversified index is a position easy to defend and harder to be talked out of. Not a commentary on Berkshire's quality — purely about the behavioral context of a non-investment-professional managing wealth in later life. *Munger:* "I recognize the logic of the fact that the S&P algorithm is very hard to beat. But I'm just more comfortable with the Berkshire." See [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md), [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**M19 · Kraft Heinz / Unilever: $15B committed, deal died on "friendly" condition.** *Buffett:* confirmed Berkshire had informally committed $15B of additional equity (3G would put in another $15B) for a friendly acquisition of Unilever at ~$143B. The transaction was conditioned on Unilever's board supporting it: "It only was approved on the basis that it be a friendly deal." When Unilever rejected the approach, Kraft Heinz withdrew. "So it would have been 15 billion of additional money, in all probability." No commentary on the economics of the rejected deal itself. See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md), [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**M20 · Speculation is inevitable — it creates opportunity for the disciplined.** *Buffett:* quoting Keynes (Chapter 12 of the General Theory): speculation is always present, waxes when "social proof" that speculation worked recently circulates. "There's nothing more agonizing than to see your neighbor who you think has an IQ about 30 points below you getting richer than you are by buying stocks." Corollary: "Wild speculation actually offers the investor more opportunities if they can keep their wits about them." China's relatively new equity market likely to have more extreme speculative episodes, not because its participants are less rational, but because they have not been through cycles. *Munger:* "We've done a lot of preaching, Warren, without much effect. And that's probably good, from our standpoint." See [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md), [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

**M22 · BNSF coal: secular decline, pace varies with natural gas price.** *Buffett:* coal is ~18% of BNSF revenue (down from ~24% over prior decade) and will decline further — "essentially certain" over a 10–20 year horizon. Year-to-year fluctuations driven by natural gas prices: at $3.15–3.20/MMBtu, utilities can produce electricity cheaper with coal; at $2, they switch. Administration optimism about coal revival is inconsistent with underlying market forces. Longer-term volumes partly offset by intermodal and agricultural bulk. *Munger:* longer-range view that "all hydrocarbons will be used, including all the coal" as chemical feedstocks, though agrees near-term volumes decline. See [bnsf](../entities/bnsf.md), [commodity-business-economics](../concepts/commodity-business-economics.md).

**★ M23 · The five largest companies require no capital at all — a structural shift.** *Buffett:* the five largest U.S. companies by market cap (Apple, Alphabet, Amazon, Facebook, Microsoft at the time) had a combined market value above $2.5 trillion and "you could run them with no equity capital at all. None." For the entire history of American industrial capitalism — Carnegie, Rockefeller, the railroads — growing large required enormous and continuous capital reinvestment. "That world has really changed, and I don't think people quite appreciate the difference." The implication for Berkshire: it loves such businesses but cannot access them at scale; its capital intensity in utilities and railroads is an honest acknowledgment of what the market will sell. *Munger:* "The chemical companies of America, at one time, were wonderful investments. Now most chemical products are sort of commoditized. In comes Apple and Google and they're just on top of the world." See [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md), [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**★ M24 · "Management by abdication" — the Berkshire value proposition.** *Buffett:* responds to the question of what Berkshire adds if it practices "delegation just short of abdication." Two concrete benefits: (1) liberating the CEO from ~20% of their time spent on analyst calls, bank negotiations, press, and quarterly-earnings management — time now fully devoted to the business; (2) providing capital certainty for any attractive investment that arises, no matter the macroeconomic backdrop. Example: GEICO could step on the gas when competitors pulled back on new business, because Berkshire's model allows short-term underwriting losses without earnings pressure. "We are thinking about nothing but where GEICO's going to be in five or 10 years." A public company's management faces a different constituency. *Munger:* "We're trying to be a good example for the world. Stay sane. Be honest." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**M25 · Structured settlements: patience and trust as competitive moat.** *Buffett:* Berkshire issues structured settlements — long-dated periodic payment annuities for accident victims — at ~$20–30M per week, at a blended rate of ~4.1% across the book, targeting the longest maturities. The competitive advantage: courts and families specifically request Berkshire as counterparty because they are confident it will still exist and be paying in 30–50 years. "The court, or the lawyer, or the family, they want to be very, very sure that whoever makes that promise is going to be around to keep it." Total book now $6–7B. At current rates, the economics are tight; the business bets on earning above that rate over the long term. See [float](../concepts/float.md), [berkshire-hathaway](../entities/berkshire-hathaway.md).

**M26 · USG: honest "not great, not a disaster" assessment.** *Buffett:* USG (large ownership stake, ~27–30%) has been disappointing because gypsum economics have been weak — over-capacity repeatedly; housing starts post-2009 have not recovered to prior levels. "Just put that one down as not one of my great ideas." *Munger:* "It hasn't been terrible." Candor about a mid-tier mistake without catastrophizing. See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**★ M27 · Ajit Jain: "terrific squared" — and the institution behind him.** *Buffett:* nobody could replace Ajit, but Berkshire has a "terrific operation in insurance outside of Ajit, and it's terrific-squared with Ajit." Highlights Guard Insurance (workers' comp, Wilkes-Barre, PA — "a gem"), Medical Protective (Tim Kenesey — "a great insurance manager all by himself"), BHSI (Peter Eastwood), U.S. Liability (Tom Nerney). With $100B+ of investable float: "If you can get somebody to hand you $104 billion and pay you to hold it while you get to invest and get the proceeds, it's a good business." *Munger:* on a California workers' comp regulatory change that Ajit instantly recognized as a pricing opportunity — "he went from a tiny percent of the market to 10 percent of the market, and he just grasped a couple billion dollars out of the air, like snapping his fingers. And when it got tough, he pulled back." See [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md), [float](../concepts/float.md), [national-indemnity](../entities/national-indemnity.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**M29 · Subsidiary CEOs love their jobs — succession isn't internal competition.** *Buffett:* subsidiary managers are "not competing for my job"; they have the jobs they want in life. "Tony Nicely loves running GEICO." This is categorically different from typical corporations where subordinates angle for the top seat — creating counterproductive dynamics. "It's a much different system than exists at most American corporations." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**★ M30 · "There just isn't anyone else" — Berkshire's acquisition niche.** *Buffett:* explains why Berkshire wins deals against private-equity buyers who can finance much more cheaply: for a certain class of seller — those who built the business and care about employees, culture, and continuity — private equity's "dress up and resell" model is exactly what they don't want. A seller (61, financially set): "I thought about selling it to a competitor, but their CFO becomes the CFO. I can sell to a leveraged buyout firm, but they'll leverage it to the hilt and resell it. He said, 'I don't want to do that.' So he said, 'It isn't because you're so special. There just isn't anyone else.'" Buffett: "If you're ever proposing to a potential spouse, don't use that line." The niche is real but narrow: "if all you care about is getting the highest price for your business, we are not a good call." *Munger:* "The people who do the leveraged buyouts, they can finance practically anything in about a week. It's very, very hard to buy businesses." See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**★ M31 · Successor compensation: "If the board hires a compensation consultant after I go, I will come back."** *Buffett:* ideal successor is already wealthy and motivated by doing the job well rather than by incrementally more money. Option B: very modest salary plus options that increase in strike price annually by the retained-earnings rate — "why should somebody retain a bunch of earnings and then claim they've actually improved the value, simply because they withheld the money from shareholders?" Shares should be held post-retirement to prevent cherry-picking exercise timing. *Munger:* "I have avoided, all my life, the compensation consultants. I hardly can find the words to express my contempt." And: "Capitalism is the golden goose that we all live on. And if people generally get so they have contempt for it because they don't like the pay arrangements in the system, your capitalism may not last as well." See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md), [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

**M32 · BNSF: a good business, but physical volume unlikely to grow.** *Buffett:* carload volumes peaked in 2006 — the four Class I railroads have invested tens of billions beyond depreciation since then and carry less freight than eleven years ago. "It would be more fun if we had something where you could expect aggregate car loadings to increase two or three or four percent a year, but I don't think that's going to happen." Still: great territory, decent returns on capital, intermodal positioned well. Chicago bottleneck remains structural. Panama Canal widening may divert some West Coast intermodal traffic but total system displacement is modest. See [bnsf](../entities/bnsf.md).

**★ M33 · "A money mind": what the next CEO must have.** *Buffett:* the Berkshire CEO role is fundamentally a capital-allocation job — over the next decade, the CEO will allocate more capital than everything previously invested combined. "You need a very sensible capital allocator." Described the "money mind" as a distinct cognitive wiring: "People can have 120 IQs or 140 IQs — very similar scoring abilities — and some of them have minds that are good at one kind of thing and some of them another. I've known very bright people that do not have money minds. They can make very unintelligent decisions. And I've known other people that would not do that brilliantly on an SAT test but have never made a dumb money decision in their life." Buyback discipline is an easy test: "How they think about something like buying in stock, because it's not a very complicated equation." The board will weight capital-allocation track record above all other qualities. See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md), [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**★ M34 · Hedge fund bet year 9: "it can't be anything but that."** *Buffett:* the bet is now in year nine (final year). Extended the structural argument beyond data: "You cannot possibly sell superior performance to all investors in aggregate, because in aggregate, they are the market." On the specific bet opponent: "All kinds of people didn't make the bet with me because they knew better. There were hundreds — at least a couple hundred — underlying hedge funds. These guys were incented to do well. The fund-of-fund manager was incented to pick the best ones. And tons of money went to pay managers for subnormal performance over a long period of time. And it can't be anything but that." On the sales dynamic: "The big money is in selling people the idea that you can do something magical for them. If you even have a billion-dollar fund and get two percent of it for terrible performance, you make $20 million. In any other field, you know, it would just blow your mind." On pay-for-performance: Combs and Weschler "are paid a million dollars a year, plus the amount by which they beat the S&P. How many hedge fund managers have said, 'I only want to get paid if I do something for you?'" *Munger:* "The investing world is just a morass of wrong incentives, crazy reporting, and a fair amount of delusion." See [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md), [investment-friction](../concepts/investment-friction.md).

**M35 · Precision Castparts: "a very good business at a fair price."** *Buffett:* reiterates love for the acquisition — aircraft engine customers care enormously about supplier reliability, and Donegan's reputation for delivery quality is unmatched. Intangible amortization of $400M+ per year is non-cash, non-economic: "I do not regard the economic goodwill of Precision Castparts being diminished at that rate annually." 3-D printing watch but no current concern. *Munger:* "It's no screaming bargain like the old days. For quality businesses, you pay up now a lot more than we used to." See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), [economic-goodwill](../concepts/economic-goodwill.md).

**M36 · Mark-to-market on equities: "a really stupid" accounting rule.** *Buffett:* starting in 2018, GAAP will require marking equity holdings to market through the income statement — so changes in the value of Berkshire's Coke and AmEx positions will flow through quarterly earnings. "GAAP earnings, as reported, will become even more meaningless, if looking only at the bottom line, than they are now." Berkshire will continue to give shareholders audited figures, explain their shortcomings, and indicate what to use and what to ignore. "It's going to get confusing." *Munger:* "You can blame the audit profession for that one. That was really stupid." See [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md), [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md).

**M37 · Munger: "The Chinese stock market is cheaper than the American market."** *Munger:* matter-of-factly: "I do think that the Chinese stock market is cheaper than the American market." Also: "China has a bright future." Berkshire's general stance is opportunistic regardless of geography. Buffett: "Charlie's delivered a headline anyway: 'Munger Predicts China Market Will Outperform U.S.'"

## Q&A digest — Afternoon session

**★ A1 · 3G's job cuts: productivity is pro-social, but Berkshire prefers to avoid it.** *Buffett:* productivity gains are the mechanism by which living standards rise — "Nobody has figured out a way to double people's consumption per capita without improving productivity per capita." 3G does it "very fast and very intelligently." But: "Personally, we have been through the process of buying into a textile business that employed a couple thousand people and went out of business over a period of time, or a department store business that was headed for oblivion. It is just not as much fun to be in a business that cuts jobs rather than one that adds jobs." Berkshire "would probably forego, personally, having Berkshire directly buy businesses where the main benefits would come from increasing productivity by actually having fewer workers." Milton Friedman's shovel-vs-spoon parable illustrates the macro logic without negating the individual pain. *Munger:* "I don't see any moral fault in 3G at all, but I do see some political reaction that doesn't do anybody any good." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**★ A2 · Cash near $100B: the burden of proof shifts.** *Buffett:* with cash and Treasury bills approaching $100B, the pressure to act or return capital is explicit: "There is no way I can come back here three years from now and tell you that we hold $150 billion or so in cash or more, and we think we're doing something brilliant by doing it." The arithmetic: "We've got almost a hundred billion invested in a business where we're paying almost a hundred times earnings. It's kind of a lousy business." On the buyback threshold (currently 1.2× book): could raise it if capital genuinely cannot be deployed, but not yet because "we're still optimistic enough about deploying the capital." Dividends also on the table at some point. The Kraft Heinz/Unilever commitment ($15B at informal commitment stage) shows the appetite is real. *Munger:* "The answer is maybe." See [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md), [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md), [dividend-policy](../concepts/dividend-policy.md).

**A4 · Combs and Weschler: "mindset is 100% what can I do for Berkshire."** *Buffett:* both now managing ~$20B combined; allocation has grown roughly in proportion to Berkshire's overall size. "They would agree that it's tougher to run $10 billion than $1–2 billion" — adding more capital would not improve their performance. Cultural distinction: "It's hard to find people young, ambitious, very smart, that don't put themselves first. And I would — every experience we've had, they did not put themselves first. They put Berkshire first." *Munger:* "They both think like shareholders. That is not the normal way headquarters employees think. It's a pretense that everybody takes on, but the reality is different. And these people really, deeply think like shareholders." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).

**A6 · Bank of America warrant: will exercise if dividend reaches 11¢/quarter.** *Buffett:* BofA warrant (expiring 2021) is worth exercising if the stock stays above $7. Trigger for early exercise: if BofA pays 11¢/quarter in dividends, the income on converted common exceeds the $300M annual preferred coupon, and Berkshire would convert from the preferred + warrant into ~700M+ shares of common. As of the meeting: "If it were expiring tomorrow, we would use the preferred to buy 700M+ shares of common. And we would keep the common." The bathtub anecdote: the original investment idea came to Buffett while in the bathtub; nothing useful has happened there since. See [berkshire-hathaway](../entities/berkshire-hathaway.md).

**★ A8 · $20B cash minimum: an absolute floor, not a target.** *Buffett:* the $20B minimum cash holding is non-negotiable — "I'm not going to operate with $21 billion any more than I'm going to see a highway sign that says maximum load 30,000 pounds and drive 29,800 across it." Practical floor closer to $24–25B. Berkshire could nevertheless execute a very large acquisition: "We spent $16 billion in a period of three weeks in fall 2008 and never got to a point where it was any problem sleeping at night." *Munger:* upper limit to a deal he'd be comfortable with: "$150 billion." Buffett: "I'll call you." See [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md), [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**A10 · Online retailing: not yet affecting Berkshire's retailers.** *Buffett:* Nebraska Furniture Mart, RC Willey, Jordan's Furniture all showing good same-store gains; Fruit of the Loom not materially affected. But: "anybody who thinks they're totally insulated from [online] is not correct." NFM Omaha is now seeing ~10% of volume ordered online, though many of those customers still pick up in-store. The evolution of retail from department stores to shopping centers to internet follows a structural logic: "the department store is online now, basically, except much expanded in assortment, much more convenient, and lower prices." Decisions about brand-building, Amazon partnership, and legacy-channel management remain live questions for every Berkshire brand. *Munger:* "It would certainly be unpleasant if we were in the department store business. Just think of what we avoided, Warren." See [moat](../concepts/moat.md), [direct-distribution-moat](../concepts/direct-distribution-moat.md).

**★ A11 · Book value: "a whole lot less" relevant as operating businesses dominate.** *Buffett:* book value "has some relevance, but a whole lot less relevance than it used to." Securities portion of book is worth no more than carrying value (and less, given taxes); operating businesses (GEICO, BHE, BNSF) are worth multiples of book with no mark-up in GAAP. Some small businesses worth "10 times or so" what they could be carried for. The 1.2× buyback trigger is therefore a very conservative floor relative to intrinsic value. *Munger:* "I like it when our marketable securities go down and our own businesses go up. We have replaced a lot of marketable securities with unmarketable securities that are worth a lot more." See [intrinsic-value-vs-book-value](../concepts/intrinsic-value-vs-book-value.md), [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md).

**★ A12 · Apple: consumer behavior, not tech expertise, is the insight.** *Buffett:* "I make no pretense whatsoever of being on the intellectual level of some 15-year-old that's got an interest in tech." The Apple investment rests on a different competence: "I think I may know — have some insights into consumer behavior. I can get a lot of information on consumer behavior and try to draw inferences about what that means about what consumer behavior is likely to be in the future." Apple market research: literally asked his grandchildren what it would take for them to give up their tablets. The switching cost is embedded in life management. *Munger:* "I think it's a very good sign that you bought the Apple. It shows either one of two things. Either you've gone crazy or you're learning. I prefer the learning explanation." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [apple](../entities/apple.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**A13 · AI: beneficial for society, disruptive for employment, politically unpredictable.** *Buffett:* AI will likely reduce employment in many areas, which is good for society (more output per person) but potentially very disruptive politically if it comes fast. The trade analogy: "the people that see the benefits of trade don't see a price at Walmart on socks that says 'you would pay X-plus-so-many-cents if you bought this domestically.'" Visible harm to specific workers; invisible diffuse benefit to many. "If 36,000 people at GEICO could be done by five or 10,000 people, and it came on quickly in a great many areas, I don't think we've ever experienced anything quite like that." *Munger:* "I don't think you have to worry about that — it's not going to come that quickly." *Buffett:* "Well, that's because I'm 86." See [american-tailwind](../concepts/american-tailwind.md).

**A14 · Renewables: "huge appetite" for both wind and solar.** *Buffett:* Berkshire has no preference between wind and solar — purely a function of geography, project economics, and what comes available. Iowa approaching self-sufficiency in wind (~35% capacity factor, plus storage problem). Berkshire's consolidated-tax-return advantage makes it the most efficient deployer of renewable tax credits among utilities: "We pay lots of taxes, and we've got lots of money." Iowa electricity rates frozen through 2029; already lower than any bordering state. "I'd be surprised if 10 years from now we don't have significantly more money in not only wind and solar, but probably more utility systems than we own now." See [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md).

**★ A15 · "I underestimated the brilliance of the execution" — on Bezos.** *Buffett:* "I've admired Jeff for a long, long time and watched what he was doing. But I did not think that he could succeed on the scale he has. And I certainly didn't even think about the possibility of doing anything with Amazon Web Services or the cloud." Read the 1997 Bezos letter — he laid out a roadmap and executed it in spades. The lesson: admiring a business and investing in it are different decisions; without conviction on scale, you sit on the sidelines even with good information. *Munger:* "It was easy. What was done there was very difficult, and it was not at all obvious that it was all going to work as well as it did. I don't feel any regret about missing Amazon. But other things were easier." Then: "Luckily, we don't miss everything, Warren. That's our secret. We don't miss them all." See [mistakes-of-omission](../concepts/mistakes-of-omission.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**A16 · "If I died tonight, I think the stock would go up tomorrow."** *Buffett:* addresses the question of whether the board would buy back stock if shares fell on a succession event. His honest assessment: the stock would more likely go up than down on his death, due to speculation about breakup value and activism — "This guy that's obstructed breaking up something where some of the parts might temporarily sell for more than the whole." But if stock did fall to an attractive price, the board's duty is clear: repurchase without false signaling or manipulation. "I think it's obvious what they would do." *Munger:* "You or I might suddenly get very stupid very quickly, but I don't think our board is going to have that problem." See [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md), [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

**A18 · Valuation is not reducible to any formula.** *Buffett:* market-cap-to-GDP and CAPE are "not paramount at all in our valuation of securities." "People are always looking for a formula. There is an ultimate formula — the present value of all the cash a business will ever distribute — but the trouble is you don't know what to stick in for the variables." Caution: every metric "can be very important sometimes and almost totally unimportant" in another context. The most important single variable: future interest rates. "The 30-year bond should tell you what people are willing to accept for a risk-free 30-year commitment. But what better figure can you come up with? I'm not sure I can. But that doesn't mean I want to use the current figure, either." See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md).

**★ A19 · Learn more from a lousy business than a great one.** *Buffett:* "If you want to be a good evaluator of businesses, you really ought to figure out a way, without too much personal damage, to run a lousy business for a while. You learn a whole lot more about business by actually struggling with a terrible business for a couple of years than you learn by getting into a very good one where the business itself is so good that you can't mess it up." Department store in Baltimore, Dempster in Beatrice, textile mill — the pain of those experiences made Berkshire what it is. *Munger:* "There's nothing like personal, painful experience if you want to learn." See [turnarounds-seldom-turn](../concepts/turnarounds-seldom-turn.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**A20 · WMD as the only external threat to Berkshire.** *Buffett:* "The only thing that can harm Berkshire in a material, permanent way except weapons of mass destruction — and I don't regard that as a low probability" — is a successful nuclear, chemical, biological, or cyber-attack that disrupts society to such an extent that normal commerce fails. Normal recessions, depressions, hurricanes: "some cases, we might even do better because of them." *Munger:* subsidiary independence is a buffer — "one horrible accident in one subsidiary... we are not [crippled]." See [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md), [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**A21 · Berkshire Hathaway Specialty Insurance: "destined to be a world leader."** *Buffett:* BHSI wrote $1.3B of premium in 2016, up 40%. The trajectory mirrors the early reinsurance build under Ajit: "We were destined to become a very important reinsurer throughout the world and, in certain ways, almost the only reinsurer for certain types of risks." BHSI has the capital, reputation, and talent. Growth will be muted in a soft market; could accelerate dramatically in a hard market. See [national-indemnity](../entities/national-indemnity.md), [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**★ A22 · "My God, we're still learning" — scuttlebutt, Phil Fisher, Apple research.** *Buffett:* on the question of what he has learned recently, credits Phil Fisher's Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (c. 1958) for the "scuttlebutt method" — talking to industry participants, competitors, and customers to triangulate a business's competitive position. Used it for American Express in the salad-oil scandal, and used it for Apple (asking grandchildren what it would take to give up their tablets). *Munger:* "We've learned so damn much. There are all kinds of things we've done over the last 10 years we would not have done 20 years ago." Then: "We would never have bought ISCAR if it had come along 10 years earlier. We would never have bought Precision Castparts if it had come along 10 years earlier. We are learning. And, my God, we're still learning." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**★ A23 · Healthcare as "the tapeworm of American economic competitiveness."** *Buffett:* In 1960, corporate taxes were ~4% of GDP; now ~2%. Healthcare costs: 5% of GDP then; 17% now. "So when American business talks about taxes strangling our competitiveness, they're talking about something that has gone down from four to two, while medical costs, borne to a great extent by business, have gone from five to 17 percent. Medical costs are the tapeworm of American economic competitiveness." Comparable countries have controlled costs at 10–11%, giving them a five-to-six-point advantage. Unrelated to which party governs. *Munger:* "A lot of it is deeply immoral... If you have a group of hospital people and doctors that are feasting like a bunch of jackals on the carcass of some dying person, it's not a pretty sight." The Redding, CA cardiologist story: doctors systematically recommended heart surgery to every patient, developed enormous "success" rates (healthy people who didn't need surgery recover perfectly), made millions, and the hospital chain held them up as a model. When the doctors were finally sanctioned: "Did they think they were doing anything wrong?" — "No, Charlie. They thought that what they were doing was good for people. That is why it's so hard to fix these things." See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md), [american-tailwind](../concepts/american-tailwind.md).

**A24 · BHE: Berkshire a buyer of choice with regulators; utility acquisition likely.** *Buffett:* Greg Abel and his team have built a record on safety, reliability, price, and renewables that makes Berkshire a preferred acquirer in the eyes of many state utility commissions. "People want us to come to their state in many cases." Current utility valuations are very high (consequence of low interest rates), so near-term acquisitions at attractive prices are scarce. But: "I'd be surprised if 10 years from now, we don't have significantly more money in not only wind and solar, but probably more utility systems." *Munger:* "Our utilities are not normal. The way Greg has run those things, they're so much better run in every way than normal utilities." See [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md).

**A25 · McLane: $45B revenue, 1% pretax margin — a high-turnover capital business.** *Buffett:* McLane (food and consumer distribution, largest single customer is Walmart) operates on ~6% gross margin and ~5% operating expenses, producing ~1% pretax margin on $45B+ of sales. The business only works economically because inventory, receivables, and payables turn 30–35 times per year — "every tenth of a cent is important." Return on capital is "very decent"; revenue is disproportionate to intrinsic value. Grady Rosier (CEO since Walmart ownership) is exceptional. McLane bought from Walmart because Berkshire could move from handshake to money in days — faster than any deal Walmart had closed before. *Munger:* "That reputation for being quick and simple, and doing what we promised, has helped at Berkshire time after time." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**★ A29 · EBITDA is "depreciation horror squared."** *Buffett:* "Depreciation is where you spend the money first and record the expense later. It's reverse float. And it's not a good thing." To exclude it from earnings and then capitalize on an EBITDA multiple systematically overstates the earnings power of capital-intensive businesses. "It's a mass delusion. And it's in the interests of Wall Street, enormously, to focus on something called EBITDA because it results in higher borrowing power, higher valuations, and all of that." *Munger:* "I think you've understated the horrors of the subject and the disgusting nature of the people that brought that term into the valuation of business... Nobody in his right mind would think that depreciation is not an expense. It made the multiple seem lower. That's why they did it." And: "Now they use it in the business schools. That is horror squared. It's bad enough that a bunch of thieves start using a term. But when it gets so common that the business schools copy it, that is not a good result." See [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md), [owner-earnings](../concepts/owner-earnings.md), [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md).

**★ A30 · Free trade, the roadkill problem, and society's obligation.** *Buffett:* trade benefits society overall ("enormous") but is "brutal to people that have the bad luck to be skilled or develop their skills for decades" in an industry that gets disrupted. The investor can diversify; the 55-year-old textile worker in New Bedford cannot. "If they get destroyed by something that's good for society, they get destroyed, unless government puts in some policies that takes care of people like that." The solution: an "educator-in-chief" to explain aggregate benefits clearly, plus policies that compensate the specific losers — not restricting trade but not abandoning the roadkill either. "Nobody should be roadkill in this sort of society" — with $57,000 per capita GDP, we have the resources. *Munger:* "A capitalist system is always going to hurt some people as it modifies and improves. There's no way to avoid it." See [american-tailwind](../concepts/american-tailwind.md).

## Connections to other sources

- The Apple consumer-franchise thesis (M12, A12) is the 2017 articulation of the same consumer-behavior lens Buffett applied to Coca-Cola (first discussed in [berkshire-1989-letter](berkshire-1989-letter.md) and at the 1994 meeting) and to See's in 1972. The "circle of competence expands" framing is new and differs from prior years' blanket tech avoidance — a genuine Munger-credited learning.
- The Wells Fargo post-mortem (M6) is the 2017 sequel to the GEICO incentive-design case study at [berkshire-2016-meeting](berkshire-2016-meeting.md) A21, which focused on design; 2017 adds the CEO-inaction dimension and explicitly invokes the Salomon crisis — itself discussed in [berkshire-1991-letter](berkshire-1991-letter.md) — as the archetype of "CEO heard the problem and did not act."
- The Google/Amazon omission admission (M12, A15) resolves the ambiguity of the Amazon/disruption discussion at [berkshire-2016-meeting](berkshire-2016-meeting.md) M12 ("We are not going to out-Bezos Bezos") — in 2016, it was framed as a competitive observation; in 2017, it is an admission of error. The IBM supersession completes the arc begun in [berkshire-2016-meeting](berkshire-2016-meeting.md) A32, where Munger called IBM "obviously coping with a considerable change." Buffett sold virtually all IBM shares in 2017.
- The $90B+ cash pile and burden-of-proof honesty (A2) extends a recurring theme from [berkshire-1994-meeting](berkshire-1994-meeting.md) M56 ("cash piles up by failure, not choice") — the 1994 version was aspirational discipline; the 2017 version is the admission that the principle has real carrying costs at extreme scale.
- The capital-light-tech-giants observation (M23) extends the capital-intensity discussion from [berkshire-2016-meeting](berkshire-2016-meeting.md) M7 ("one of the problems of prosperity") from a focus on Berkshire-specific constraints to a broader structural observation about capitalism itself.
- The EBITDA critique (A27/A28) is the meeting-floor companion to the owner-earnings concept introduced in the [berkshire-1986-letter](berkshire-1986-letter.md), here with Munger's most direct moral critique of the practitioners who promoted the metric.
- The healthcare-tapeworm analysis (A23) stands alone in the corpus as a quantitatively precise policy diagnosis; it anticipates Buffett's 2018 announcement (with Amazon and JPMorgan) of a joint health-insurance venture, though the meetings corpus does not yet reach that event.
- The airline stake defense (M13) sets up its own supersession: Berkshire sold all four positions at significant losses in April 2020 following COVID-19's destruction of air travel demand, with Buffett saying at the 2020 meeting that the world had changed for airlines in a way he had not anticipated.
