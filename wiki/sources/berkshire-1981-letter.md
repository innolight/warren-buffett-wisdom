# Berkshire Hathaway 1981 Shareholder Letter

Operating earnings $39.7M, 15.2% on beginning equity (down from 17.8%). 17-year book-value compound: 21.1% from $19.46 to $526.02. Two original essays anchor the letter: the **"kissing toads" critique** of high-premium acquisitions, and the **passive-return crossbar / inflation-as-corporate-tapeworm** framework for thinking about when equity capital actually adds value. Munger appears as a visible co-author (the shareholder-designated-contributions program is credited to him explicitly).

## Key themes

- **Kissing toads: three motivations behind high-premium acquisitions.** (1) Animal spirits — leaders thrill to activity. (2) Size as the dominant yardstick — Fortune-500 managers know their sales rank, not their profitability rank. (3) The Pygmalion fantasy — confidence that "our managerial kiss will turn this toad into a prince." Buffett: "We've observed many kisses but very few miracles." See [[acquisition-criteria]].
- **The two categories of acquisition success.** (1) Buying inflation-friendly businesses: those that can raise prices easily and accommodate higher dollar volume with little new capital. (2) Managerial superstars — Ben Heineman at Northwest Industries, Henry Singleton at Teledyne, Erwin Zaban at National Service Industries, Tom Murphy at Capital Cities. "These champs have made very few deals in recent years, and often have found repurchase of their own shares to be the most sensible employment of corporate capital."
- **The passive-return crossbar.** Decades back, a 10% ROE business was a "good" business — long-term taxable bonds yielded 5%, tax-exempts 3%, and stocks traded at 1.5x book on average. By 1981, taxable bonds yielded 16%+, tax-exempts 14%; American business as a whole earned ~14% on equity, taxed before reaching the individual. Most American businesses had become "bad" businesses *not because they were jumping lower, but because the crossbar of passive return had been elevated faster.* See [[passive-return-crossbar]].
- **Inflation as corporate tapeworm.** Even at flat unit volume, businesses must keep funding receivables, inventory, and fixed assets at higher nominal dollar levels. Low-return businesses must retain *every* nickel — not because retention is attractive, but because survival demands it. The tapeworm "preemptively consumes its requisite daily diet of investment dollars regardless of the health of the host organism." Hence dividend reinvestment plans and Peter-paying-Paul stock issuances that disguise the underlying problem.
- **Reasoning by parable of the talents.** A high-return business should retain earnings; a low-return business should distribute them. "In the parable of the talents, the two high-earning servants are rewarded with 100% retention of earnings… the non-earning third servant is not only chastised — 'wicked and slothful' — but also is required to redirect all of his capital to the top performer." (Matt 25:14–30.) Inflation, perversely, inverts this — forcing low-return businesses to retain.
- **GEICO drives the year's gain.** Over half of the $124M (31%) net-worth increase came from GEICO's market appreciation; market gain considerably outstripped intrinsic-value gain. Buffett expects the gap to close in either direction over time.
- **Insurance forecast: 1982 will be the worst year in recent memory.** The combined ratio rose from 100.6 (1979) → 103.1 (1980) → 105.7 (1981); the premium-written growth of only 3.6% locks in further deterioration. Buffett breaks with the conventional "cycle returns to mean" view: "the best underwriting years in the future decade may appear substandard against the average year of the past decade."
- **Shareholder-designated contributions debut.** 95.6% of eligible shares participated. Buffett credits Munger explicitly: "The designated-contributions idea, along with many other ideas that have turned out well for us, was conceived by Charlie Munger, Vice Chairman of Berkshire and Chairman of Blue Chip."

## Notable quotes

> Investors can always buy toads at the going price for toads. If investors instead bankroll princesses who wish to pay double for the right to kiss the toad, those kisses had better pack some real dynamite.

> Predicting rain doesn't count, building arks does.

> For inflation acts as a gigantic corporate tapeworm. That tapeworm preemptively consumes its requisite daily diet of investment dollars regardless of the health of the host organism.

> Most American businesses pay out a significant portion of their earnings and thus fall between the two examples. And most American businesses are currently "bad" businesses economically — producing less for their individual investors after-tax than the tax-exempt passive rate of return on money.

> Like virginity, a stable price level seems capable of maintenance, but not of restoration.

> Beware of "dividends" that can be paid out only if someone promises to replace the capital distributed.
