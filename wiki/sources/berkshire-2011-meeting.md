# Berkshire Hathaway 2011 Annual Meeting

Held April 30, 2011, the meeting opened under the shadow of the David Sokol / Lubrizol insider-trading affair — Sokol, until weeks earlier a leading succession candidate, had bought Lubrizol stock days before recommending the company to Buffett, then resigned. Buffett gave a long opening statement, Munger followed, and the first hour of Q&A was governance and ethics. The other live backdrop was the year's catastrophe wave (Japan earthquake/tsunami, New Zealand and Australia events) that would hand Berkshire's reinsurance its first likely underwriting loss in nine years. The durable teaching that survives the year is mostly about **reputation and governance under pressure** (the anti-anger discipline, the trust-based compliance culture, the independent-chairman safety valve, the "leave a bailed-out CEO dead broke" rule), plus a cluster of investing evergreens: the three-category map of all assets (why gold loses to productive assets), "forget about goodwill" when judging a business's quality, why Berkshire won't spend its undervalued stock as acquisition currency, why it won't join an auction, and why it hates investment-banker projections. This page keeps the wiki's two-layer convention: a distilled thematic top, then a comprehensive ★-marked Q&A digest as the home for detail.

## How to read the Q&A digest

- **★** marks a timeless, load-bearing exchange worth citing in the future; unmarked entries still carry investor value but are secondary or period-specific.
- Answers are attributed separately to **Buffett** and **Munger** — a Munger line appears only when he added a distinct lesson (he answered "Nothing to add" to most).
- **M#/A#** map to the numbered items in the raw morning/afternoon transcript for traceability.

## Key themes

### Don't make important decisions in anger — but never mistake calm for weakness

The most-scrutinized decision of the year was Buffett's *tone*: shareholders wanted outrage in the Sokol press release and got a factual recital that also listed Sokol's virtues. Munger's defense is the transferable lesson: "you don't want to make important decisions in anger. You want to display as much ruthlessness as your duty requires, and you do not want to add one single iota because you're angry" — Tom Murphy's "you can always tell a man to go to hell tomorrow if it's such a good idea." The substance was ruthless (minimum severance, evidence handed to the SEC the same day), even if the packaging wasn't. The lesson generalizes past PR: separate the *action* your duty requires from the *emotion* the moment provokes. See [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md). *(M4, M6)*

### A trust-based culture beats a big compliance department

Berkshire runs 260,000 employees — "about the number of households in the greater metropolitan Omaha" — on a two-page code and a biennial letter from Buffett, not a swelling compliance bureaucracy. Munger's claim is counter-intuitive and durable: "your best compliance cultures are the ones which have this attitude of trust, and some of the ones with the biggest compliance departments, like Wall Street, have the most scandals." The Sokol case is not a refutation but the tail risk you accept: rules can't stop a determined insider, so the payoff is in selecting trustworthy people and making the spirit — not just the letter — the norm. Pair it with the structural safety valve Buffett proposed: an **independent, non-CEO chairman** holding a large block, so a bad CEO can be replaced without dislodging someone who "controls the agenda." See [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md). *(M22, M8)*

### All assets fall into three buckets — and only the productive one reliably compounds

Buffett's cleanest live statement of the taxonomy: (1) currency-denominated claims (bonds, cash) — "a bet on how government now, and in the future, will behave"; (2) non-productive assets you buy hoping someone pays more later — the classic case being gold; (3) productive assets valued on what they deliver (a farm, a business). The gold-vs-productive contrast is the vivid part: all the world's gold forms a ~67-foot cube worth ~$8 trillion that "isn't going to do anything," versus the same money buying every acre of U.S. farmland plus ten ExxonMobils with a trillion left over. "I would bet on good-producing businesses to outperform something that doesn't do anything over any period of time." See [investment-categories](../concepts/investment-categories.md). *(M16)*

### "Forget about goodwill" when judging a business — remember it when judging yourself

A precise two-lens rule for return on capital. To judge whether a business is *good*, look at return on **net tangible assets** and ignore goodwill — that's why an indestructible brand like See's or Coke shouldn't be amortized. To judge whether *you* allocated capital well, you must include the goodwill you paid: Lubrizol earns ~$1B pretax on ~$2.5B of tangible equity (a wonderful business), but Berkshire is paying ~$9B for it, so "you have to judge us based on close to a $9 billion investment." Amortizing goodwill makes no sense; *writing it off* when a purchase proves a mistake does. See [economic-goodwill](../concepts/economic-goodwill.md), [return-on-equity-yardstick](../concepts/return-on-equity-yardstick.md). *(A14)*

### An undervalued stock is a currency you refuse to spend

Berkshire walked away from a "very nice" international elephant because it could only be done with a lot of stock: "unless it's fully valued, it's a big mistake to use it as a currency." Issuing shares means "divesting ourselves of a portion of every wonderful business we have, from GEICO to ISCAR" — you'd be trading away See's to buy something you like less. Buffett paid ~$9B cash for Lubrizol and told James Hambrick "right off the bat" that a stock deal was out. The same logic condemns J&J's mostly-stock purchase of Synthes: paying in your own shares signals you think they're not undervalued. See [stock-as-acquisition-currency](../concepts/stock-as-acquisition-currency.md), [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md). *(M11, A35, A36)*

### The best inflation asset earns high returns on little tangible capital

Asked whether a hard asset with pricing power (a railroad, a dam) beats a high-return brand in inflation, Buffett was unequivocal: "The first group is superior." See's proves it — bought at ~$25-30M revenue on $9M of tangible assets, now well over $300M revenue on ~$40M of tangible assets: only ~$30M reinvested to grow, having thrown off ~$1.5B pretax. A capital-intensive utility earning a bond-like regulated return does *worst* in inflation. The purest form is a royalty: sales-based income with no receivables, inventory, or capex. Berkshire buys railroads and utilities only because it can't find See's-type businesses in the size it now needs. See [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md), [bonds-and-inflation](../concepts/bonds-and-inflation.md). *(M13, A11)*

### Every deal stands alone — don't anchor on your best deal ever, and never bet the company

Two behavioral disciplines. First, opportunity cost is judged fresh each day: "The goal is not to make a better deal than you've ever made before. The goal is to make a satisfactory deal that's the best deal you can make at the time" — the error that "takes themselves out of the game" is measuring every deal against your all-time best. Second, risk control is centralized and non-negotiable: "I regard myself as the Chief Risk Officer of Berkshire, and that is not something to be delegated to a committee." Berkshire will carry nuclear plants and toxic-cargo railroads but "not doing anything that … threatens me losing a night's sleep over Berkshire's well-being." See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md), [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md). *(A21, A24)*

## Q&A digest — Morning session

**M3 · Q1 catastrophes, GEICO's hidden goodwill, and OTTI accounting.** *Buffett:* the Asian catastrophes (Japan, New Zealand) cost the reinsurance industry ~$50B, Berkshire ~$1.67B pretax (40%+ via the Swiss Re quota-share, which Swiss Re won't renew); 2011 will likely be the first underwriting loss in nine years, but "if insurance breaks even, we get the free use of float." GEICO's ~$1B book goodwill understates a ~$14B economic value — each policyholder is worth ~$1,500, so adding ~318,000 policies adds ~$500M of unrecorded goodwill "every bit as real" as the balance-sheet numbers. Ignore quarterly securities gains/losses: the $337M Wells Fargo "other-than-temporary impairment" markdown, while a $3.7B unrealized gain sat unrecorded, shows how capricious the accounting is (specific-identification vs. average-cost, both GAAP). See [float](../concepts/float.md), [geico](../entities/geico.md), [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md).

**★ M4 · Sokol/Lubrizol — "inexcusable" and "inexplicable."** *Buffett:* Sokol violated the code of ethics and insider-trading rules by buying Lubrizol stock days before pitching it, never disclosing his Citigroup contact. What is *inexplicable*: he made no attempt to hide the trades, and this was the same man who a decade earlier voluntarily handed $12.5M of a comp award to junior partner Greg Abel for no credit. *Munger:* "it's generally a mistake to assume that rationality is going to be perfect, even in very able people"; asked for a cause, "I think hubris contributes to it." See [dave-sokol](../people/dave-sokol.md), [greg-abel](../people/greg-abel.md).

**★ M6 · Why not fire Sokol on the spot — and the "inadequate" press release.** *Buffett:* walks the timeline (Jan 14 first mention, learned of Citi's role Mar 14 from John Freund, resignation Mar 28); a resignation vs. firing preserved minimum severance and cut litigation risk, and Berkshire phoned the SEC enforcement chief the same day. He owns the tone failure — he listed Sokol's genuine good deeds because the facts he was disclosing "are going to create lots of problems for him for years to come." *Munger:* "that press release was not the cleverest … in the history of the world," but "you don't want to make important decisions in anger … you do not want to add one single iota because you're angry" (Tom Murphy: "you can always tell a man to go to hell tomorrow"). See [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

**M7 · Fed ending QE2/POMO.** *Buffett:* the taper is "the most advertised open market purchase in history" — fully known, therefore already discounted; a huge buyer withdrawing changes the market, "but … a different market that's already been anticipated." *Munger:* "I have nothing to add." A clean statement that fully-telegraphed events don't move efficient markets on the date.

**★ M8 · The next chairman should be independent of the CEO.** *Buffett:* proposes his son Howard as a non-executive, unpaid chairman holding (through the estate) a large block, so a mis-hired CEO can be replaced — "it's not an easy job to displace a sitting CEO who also holds the chairman's position and controls the agenda." Directors will weigh "the person as a human being" as heavily as managerial skill. *Munger:* the Rockefellers left Standard Oil's management but intervened once, on moral grounds, to remove the head of Standard of Indiana — "you have put another string in our bow." See [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

**M9 · If you could add one sector to your circle.** *Buffett:* tech — a large field where "the degree of disparity in results among larger tech companies … is likely to be very, very dramatic," so skill in picking winners pays off far more than among interchangeable oil majors. *Munger:* tech or energy, but "we're the wrong people to develop the expertise … if we were going to do it, it would have already happened" — though "we might identify someone else who has abilities that we lack." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ M10 · How Buffett got comfortable with Lubrizol: the ease-of-entry test.** *Buffett:* he never understood the chemistry and didn't need to — what mattered was industry economics. He ran his standard test: "when we bought See's Candy in 1972, I said to myself, if I had a hundred million dollars and I wanted to go in and take on See's Candy, could I do it?" For Lubrizol — patents plus deep engine-maker customer relationships in a small (~$10B) market — he "decided that there was a pretty good-sized moat around this," like ISCAR's carbide tools. *Munger:* "ISCAR and Lubrizol … are sisters under the skin. You've got very small markets that aren't really too attractive to anybody with any sense to enter, and fanaticism in service." See [moat](../concepts/moat.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ M11 · Won't spend undervalued stock as acquisition currency.** *Buffett:* walked from a "very nice" international elephant that would have required a lot of stock — "unless it's fully valued, it's a big mistake to use it as a currency." Issuing shares means "giving away part of the businesses we already own" (Burlington used ~30% stock and "it was painful"). *Munger:* "the businesses you already own are so good it's not wise to part with them to get a business we don't own. Ordinarily." See [stock-as-acquisition-currency](../concepts/stock-as-acquisition-currency.md).

**★ M12 · Relentless optimism about American capitalism.** *Buffett:* answering Dick Holland's "why so glowing" — since his 1930 birth (Dow 42, 25% unemployment, dust bowl ahead) the average American's living standard rose "six-for-one"; the country survived a civil war and ~15 recessions. "The resuscitative power of capitalism" — not fiscal/monetary policy — is the real engine, and it "doesn't do it in a straight line." *Munger:* "Europe survived the Black Death … The world is going to go on," and "the politicians are never so bad you don't live to want them back." See [american-tailwind](../concepts/american-tailwind.md).

**★ M13 · Best asset for inflation: high return on tangible capital, low reinvestment.** *Buffett:* a product that grows dollar volume with inflation without needing proportional capital is ideal (See's: ~$30M reinvested, ~$1.5B pretax generated); the worst are businesses "with tons of receivables and inventories" that must double their capital to do the same real volume. The ultimate example is a royalty — no receivables, inventory, or fixed assets. Utilities/railroads, with bond-like returns, do worse in inflation. *Munger:* "we didn't always know this … continuous learning is absolutely required." See [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md), [bonds-and-inflation](../concepts/bonds-and-inflation.md).

**★ M14 · No dividend — sell a slice instead.** *Buffett:* every dollar retained has created more than a dollar of market value, so a holder needing income should let it compound and "sell off a little piece," selling at 120-130 cents on the dollar rather than taking out a taxed dividend. "The day that Berkshire declares a dividend, the stock will go down … it should go down — because it's an admission … that a compounding machine has lost its ability to continue." *Munger:* "there's nothing wrong with selling a little Berkshire stock to buy jewelry if you do it in the right place." See [dividend-policy](../concepts/dividend-policy.md).

**M15 · Still bullish on Wells Fargo and U.S. Bancorp.** *Buffett:* both among the best large banks, but post-crisis returns on equity will be structurally lower because permitted leverage is down — "probably a good thing for society." Banking "if you just keep out of trouble on the asset side" is a very good business given cheap, near-guaranteed deposits. *Munger:* praises M&T's Bob Wilmers, who "sounds like an Old Testament prophet" for disliking trading against your own customers. *Supersession flag:* Buffett's confidence here predates Wells Fargo's 2016 fake-accounts scandal; Berkshire exited its entire WFC stake by 2020–2022. See [wells-fargo](../entities/wells-fargo.md).

**★ M16 · Three categories of assets; productive beats gold.** *Buffett:* (1) currency claims — "a bet on how government … will behave"; (2) non-productive assets bought hoping for a higher resale (gold — the ~67-foot, ~$8T cube that "isn't going to do anything," and more is mined each year); (3) productive assets valued on output (a farm, a business). The same $8T buys all U.S. farmland plus ten ExxonMobils with change to spare. "In God We Trust … it should say, 'In Government We Trust.'" *Munger:* gold only pays off "if the world really goes to hell," and "you would also need an army to defend the gold." See [investment-categories](../concepts/investment-categories.md).

**M17 · Attracting the first investors.** *Buffett:* started in 1956 with seven family/friends, refusing to disclose holdings so he could invest "in a room all by myself," compounding trust slowly. Warns that "with the present fee structure, just attracting money, rather than performing with it, can be enormously lucrative." *Munger:* "it helps if you conducted yourself in life so that other people trust you … and then it helps even more if other people are right to trust you." See [warren-buffett](../people/warren-buffett.md).

**★ M18 · A conglomerate, but not a '60s go-go stock machine.** *Buffett:* Berkshire is a conglomerate — the tax-efficient movement of capital from cash-generating to cash-needing businesses is a real advantage. The ITT/Teledyne/Gulf & Western model was a "semi-Ponzi scheme": issue high-multiple stock to buy low-multiple earnings and juice EPS until "somebody says, 'The emperor has no clothes.'" Teledyne's twist — issuing when overpriced, buying back when underpriced — was the honorable version. Berkshire instead buys businesses "to keep forever." *Munger:* one such CEO said "I know what I'm going to report, I just don't know how I'm going to do it." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [stock-as-acquisition-currency](../concepts/stock-as-acquisition-currency.md).

**M20 · The dollar will decline, but inflation won't destroy the economy.** *Buffett:* the purchasing power of most currencies will erode; a short is just a bet on relative speed, and he lacks conviction. The 1930 dollar is worth ~6 cents now — "16-to-1 in terms of depreciation. And as he points out, we've both done pretty well." *Munger:* "the world muddles through" — Greece is dysfunctional yet survives; "A great civilization has a lot of ruin in it" (Adam Smith).

**M21 · Berkshire vs. an index fund.** *Buffett:* for anyone with a day job, "the average individual will do better buying an index fund consistently over time than almost anything else" — "a perfectly satisfactory investment"; he'd personally still pick Berkshire at current prices. *Munger:* "reduced expectations are the best defense any investor has" — the next 50 years of skilled-investor returns won't match the last 50. See [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md).

**★ M22 · Rules aren't meant to be danced around; trust beats bureaucracy.** *Buffett:* Berkshire relies on a code, insider-trading rules, and his biennial letter emphasizing "not only the letter … but the spirit"; with 260,000 people there will always be violations (they've sent employees to jail), and the Sokol case "hit very, very high up." *Munger:* "the greatest institutions … select very trustworthy people, and they trust them a lot … your best compliance cultures are the ones which have this attitude of trust, and some of the ones with the biggest compliance departments, like Wall Street, have the most scandals." See [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

**★ M25 · The bet against funds of funds.** *Buffett:* three years into his 10-year, S&P-500-vs-five-funds-of-funds wager, the hedge funds lead (they should, in a down market) but "the only people that are ahead so far are the investment managers." A live demonstration that fees, not skill, are the reliable winner in the fund-of-funds structure. See [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md).

## Q&A digest — Afternoon session

**A2 · BYD still attractive?** *Munger:* the price is well above Berkshire's cost, so "by definition it's not quite as cheap"; delays and glitches are inevitable in a company moving that fast — "they tried to double auto sales every year for six years, and it worked the first five times." *Buffett:* "I have nothing to add." See [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md).

**A3 · Trading oil is too hard.** *Buffett:* he knows people with a real edge in stocks and even distressed bonds, but nobody with a durable edge timing oil, natural gas, copper, or cotton. On BNSF's diesel hedging: "if we could guess the price of oil, we didn't need to run the railroad." *Munger:* "if we'd done nothing but oil from the very beginning … we would not have done nearly as well"; "trading oil worked best of all for the people who bribed Nigeria. That's not our milieu." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [commodity-business-economics](../concepts/commodity-business-economics.md).

**A5 · What did you learn last year? Go to bed wiser.** *Munger:* despite ignoring high-tech all his life, he read *In the Plex* about Google and enjoyed it — "if I enjoy learning it, I regard it as important, because … that's what you're here for, is to go to bed every night a little wiser than you were when you got up." *Buffett:* "I'm going to have Charlie write the next press release." A compact statement of lifelong learning as the point of a career. See [charlie-munger](../people/charlie-munger.md).

**★ A6 · Leave a bailed-out CEO "dead broke."** *Buffett:* some institutions can't be allowed to collapse (Fannie/Freddie, the auto rescue was right), so reduce the propensity to fail: any institution that "requires society to come and bail it out … should have a system in place that leaves their CEO, basically, and his spouse, dead broke," and directors should forfeit years of fees — because "the upside and downside incentives are vastly different." *Munger:* panics start in speculation and bad behavior; expect "a new mess or two before your career is over," and blames academic finance/economics — "not hard sciences." See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).

**A7 · No Washington Post shares will be sold.** *Buffett:* leaving the board (like Melinda Gates) after 1974 was about saving directors' fees and travel at age 80 — "I am … available a lot cheaper than before"; "we will not be selling any Post shares," an unusual explicit promise on a marketable security. *Munger:* serving on many boards "is for the birds." *Supersession flag:* in 2014 Berkshire swapped its entire Graham Holdings (WaPo successor) stake for a Miami TV station, cash, and Berkshire shares — the "won't sell" pledge was overtaken by a tax-efficient exchange. See [washington-post](../entities/washington-post.md).

**A8 · Will Berkshire outpace the Australian dollar?** *Munger:* "The answer is no." *Buffett:* he can't forecast relative currency moves 10 years out, so no promise — the only promise is that they try every day to raise Berkshire's intrinsic value, and "our interests are 100 percent aligned … We know we can't do remotely as well in the future as we have in the past." See [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md).

**A9 · The stock portfolio isn't an afterthought.** *Buffett:* he and Munger "spend more time thinking about the portfolio" than about the rarer big acquisitions; on $150B+ of cash and securities, "the performance of that particular segment is going to have a lot to do with how well Berkshire does." Size means a small edge at best. *Munger:* he prefers the current game — "it's more fun to create permanent partners doing constructive things than just outsmart other people … shuffling little pieces of paper." See [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md).

**★ A10 · "We have an unbelievable insurance operation."** *Buffett:* nothing to trade it for — GEICO (now #3, gaining, ~8-point Q1 underwriting profit), Ajit Jain's from-scratch reinsurance book ("there's really nobody else to call"), Tad Montross's disciplined Gen Re. National Indemnity, bought for $7M in 1967, now has more net worth than any insurer in the world. *Munger:* "it's not that great a business as a business, casualty insurance … there are temptations to be stupid in it. It's like banking … but if you're in it, I think we've got the best one." See [float](../concepts/float.md), [national-indemnity](../entities/national-indemnity.md), [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md).

**A11 · Why See's thrives in inflation.** *Buffett:* since purchase the dollar has lost 80–85% of its value; See's sells 75% more pounds but earns ~10× the revenue on little added capital — the template for the best inflation businesses. Utilities, with bond-like returns, do worst. *Munger:* the capital-intensive railroad and utilities are nonetheless "one of the best" of their kind. (Re-articulates M13.) See [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md).

**A12 · No A-share split.** *Buffett:* "in effect, we've already split it … 1500-for-one by having the B available"; the B tracks the A in any transaction, with only less voting power, so "he really should not count heavily on the A stock getting split." *Munger:* Buffett used to cheer up old friends with "may you live until the A stock splits." See [berkshire-hathaway](../entities/berkshire-hathaway.md).

**★ A13 · Buffett's best deal: hiring Ajit Jain.** *Buffett:* "I can't think of any decision he's ever made that I think I could have made better … you'd be better off voting with him than with me." Ajit repeatedly refused to monetize himself into hundreds of millions via Bermuda start-ups — "it doesn't cross his mind" — and "thinks of Berkshire first." "I know what my best deal was, which was hiring him." *Munger:* Osler's rule — "the secret of success in a field is getting very interested in it." (Asked the reverse, Buffett names retailing, especially Dexter, as Berkshire's worst.) See [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md).

**★ A14 · Forget goodwill when judging a business; count it when judging yourself.** *Buffett:* to assess a business's quality, look at return on **net tangible assets** and ignore goodwill (don't amortize a brand like See's/Coke that's worth more each year); to assess your own capital allocation, include the goodwill you paid. Lubrizol earns ~$1B pretax on ~$2.5B tangible equity (great business) but Berkshire pays ~$9B (judge Berkshire on that). Write goodwill off when a purchase proves a mistake; never amortize it. *Munger:* even at ~10% pretax on purchase price, "it's not all bad" when much of the money is costless insurance float. See [economic-goodwill](../concepts/economic-goodwill.md), [return-on-equity-yardstick](../concepts/return-on-equity-yardstick.md).

**★ A15 · Teaching business through case histories — and how success can kill you.** *Munger:* Costco became the best in its category through meritocracy and an ethical duty to pass cost savings to customers, creating "ferocious customer loyalty"; General Motors became the most successful of its kind and "wiped out its common shareholders" — "to prevent wealth from killing you, your success turning into a disadvantage, is a big problem in business." He'd teach with Value-Line graphs tracing whole company histories, a method business schools abandoned to protect disciplinary turf. See [berkshire-hathaway](../entities/berkshire-hathaway.md).

**A18 · Societal issues rarely change investment decisions.** *Buffett:* water scarcity and food stocks are important but don't move the analysis — "we are looking at what we expect their earning power to be three, five, 10, 15 years down the road." *Munger:* "with enough energy … the water problem goes away" (desalination); and conserving hydrocarbons matters because "modern agriculture won't work without them" — "use up the other fellow's hydrocarbon while preserving our own."

**★ A17 · Compensation for the next CEO.** *Buffett:* the next CEO should be paid well, likely via options with two twists — the strike should equal what the whole business would fetch if sold (never a depressed market price), and it should rise annually by a hurdle (e.g., 7–8% minus the dividend yield) so the executive is rewarded only for value created *above* what mere retention would produce. Operating managers already "make well into eight figures" for real excess value, "not because of any phony targets." *Munger:* hopes his successor will be "a very rich man who has adopted Warren's system of pay … somebody in America has to be the exemplar for not grabbing all that you can." See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).

**★ A19 · We don't participate in auctions.** *Munger:* on whether Lubrizol's board breached its duty by not running a go-shop — "The answer is no … because we were not going to participate in the transaction if they didn't do it our way." *Buffett:* an auction winner "will not pay them what we would have … if they stepped up"; a negotiated deal gives the seller certainty and a full price up front — "if they had said we want to conduct an auction, we would have said good luck." See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**★ A20 · How to judge whether Buffett allocated capital well.** *Buffett:* the market-value test over time — "unless the market value of Berkshire is significantly greater than the amount of capital that we have kept from you, retained … the verdict is against us"; retained earnings must translate into the stock selling at a premium over cumulative capital retained. *Munger:* "We have continued to beat the market averages. We just aren't beating our own past record." See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md).

**★ A21 · Don't measure every deal against your best-ever deal.** *Buffett:* the five crisis-era financings (Goldman, GE, Dow, Wrigley/Mars, Swiss Re) had different terms because each faced a different opportunity cost that day — "past deals we've made don't really make any difference." A common, sometimes huge error is refusing any deal that isn't as good as your best ever: "The goal is … to make a satisfactory deal that's the best deal you can make at the time." *Munger:* "There's no other rational way to make deals." See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md).

**A22 · Read — speed is overrated.** *Buffett:* he reads five papers and countless 10-Ks/10-Qs but isn't fast; fast reading would be "a huge advantage," yet the point is that "there's hardly anything more pleasurable … than reading and reading and reading." *Munger:* a flawless-but-slower Caltech roommate beat him on accuracy — "What the hell difference does it make?" See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**A23 · The debt ceiling — and borrowing in your own currency.** *Buffett:* refusing to raise the ceiling would be "the most asinine … act that Congress has ever performed" (like Indiana's 1890s bill to round pi to 3); having a fixed ceiling at all is a mistake for a growing country. The durable point: "The United States is not going to have a debt crisis of any kind as long as we keep issuing our notes in our own currency" — the only risk is the printing press and inflation, unlike euro members or anyone forced to borrow in a foreign currency (he once declined a Chicago bank purchase financed only in Kuwaiti dinars). *Munger:* misses bipartisan eras; "both parties are trying to compete to see who can be the most stupid."

**★ A24 · Nuclear power, and Buffett as Chief Risk Officer.** *Buffett:* nuclear is "important" and "safe" and belongs in the world's long-term energy mix, though public reaction to Fukushima will stall U.S. development; MidAmerican's exposure is nothing like Tokyo Electric's. The transferable line: "I regard myself as the Chief Risk Officer of Berkshire, and that is not something to be delegated to a committee" — Berkshire carries pipelines, toxic rail cargo, and cat risk but nothing that "threatens me losing a night's sleep over Berkshire's well-being." *Munger:* "we can't be so risk averse that things that have a very tiny chance of making a big dent in one subsidiary are unendurable … We have to have a certain reasonable amount of courage." See [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md), [super-cat-insurance](../concepts/super-cat-insurance.md).

**A25 · Why the shareholder-directed charity program ended.** *Buffett:* the beloved 20-year program (shareholders directed Berkshire's contributions to their own 501(c)(3)s) survived See's boycotts, but once the Pampered Chef's ~50,000 independent contractors — mostly women depending on the income — were targeted in pro-choice/pro-life boycotts, "it just became apparent … that it was unfair to continue it." *Munger:* "We don't want the parent company involved in distracting arguments about the social issues of the times." A reputation-management lesson: the parent shouldn't impose collateral damage on innocent third parties.

**★ A26 · Why we hate projections.** *Buffett:* "I've never seen a projection from an investment banker that didn't show the earnings going up over time, and believe me, the earnings don't always go up." When Sokol offered Lubrizol's management projections, "I told him no"; the old rule — "don't ask the barber whether you need a haircut." (He bought Scott Fetzer from a letter to Ralph Schey, never opening First Boston's book; Munger offered "$2 million if you don't show me the book.") They do rough projections in their heads, never on paper. *Munger:* formal computer projections create "an enormous false precision … I really think they do more damage than they do good"; students should "learn to do it our way, but … pretend to do it their way" until they graduate. See [mistakes-of-omission](../concepts/mistakes-of-omission.md), [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md).

**A28 · Evaluating a company outside the U.S.** *Buffett:* same principles — value the business, trust the managers, buy at an attractive price — but with an added allowance for not knowing foreign tax law, customs, and shareholder attitudes. He chose PetroChina over the equally cheap Yukos on comfort, and was impressed PetroChina committed to paying out 45% of earnings — "more than any … oil company in the United States would tell me." *Munger:* "we make so few investments in China that trying to draw general lessons from us would be hard" (the professor who saw one Indian walking and concluded all Indians walk single file). See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ A29 · Short-term cash sits in Treasurys — a parking place, not an investment.** *Buffett:* all short-term options are lousy, "but we don't play around with short-term money" — no commercial paper or money-market funds in the big accounts; "it's a parking place where we know we'll get our car back when we want it." Liquidity is what let Berkshire show up with $6.5B for Wrigley on October 6, 2008, and close pipeline deals over a weekend. *Munger:* "it's really stupid to try and maximize returns on short-term money if you're in an opportunist game the way we are." See [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md).

**A30 · Wind power needs the subsidy — and Berkshire's tax capacity is an asset.** *Buffett:* wind is clean but "only when the wind blows" (~35% of the time in Iowa), so it can't be base load, and the economics work only with the federal tax credit. Berkshire's unusual ability to *use* those credits — it pays ~2% of all U.S. corporate taxes — lets it build more wind than tax-constrained utilities, helping hold Iowa rates flat for a decade. See [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md).

**A31 · Berkshire would never have let NetJets go bankrupt.** *Buffett:* his praise of Sokol for rescuing an operation "destined for bankruptcy" meant *absent Berkshire's ownership* — the parent has backstopped two insurance subsidiaries that would have failed standalone and "it never crossed our mind" to walk away. *Munger:* "No comment." *Supersession/context flag:* the exchange re-litigates the Sokol praise that opened the year's controversy; it reads awkwardly given the same-meeting scandal. See [netjets](../entities/netjets.md).

**A32 · Invest in your own skills.** *Buffett:* "anything you do to improve your own skills … you never know where it's going to pay off"; the one diploma on his office wall is a $100 Dale Carnegie course from 1951 — "it's incalculable how much value I got." Communication skills first, then find your passion and improve at it. *Munger:* master the easy, agreed-upon parts of a field before the contested frontier. See [warren-buffett](../people/warren-buffett.md).

**★ A33 · Reinsurance is much harder than it looks.** *Buffett:* Berkshire "really didn't succeed at all in the reinsurance business in the first 15 years" (from ~1970) until Ajit — "it looks easy most of the time," like giving wrong odds on dice: you win many bets until the boxcars arrive. Price for the infrequent event, and don't be fooled by one, two, or four good years. *Munger:* investment banks now sell derivative products where "most of the time the customer wins but when the customer loses, he really loses big … Just beware of the salesman who's selling a new derivative product." See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md), [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md).

**A34 · Meeting Todd Combs.** *Munger:* Combs "sent me a letter … like Warren's letter to … Ralph Schey," they shared a meal, and Munger flagged him to Buffett. *Buffett:* results are judged over five years, not months; Berkshire will likely end up with more than one investment manager, but the right person "means a lot of things," not just IQ or record. See [warren-buffett](../people/warren-buffett.md).

**A35 · J&J's mostly-stock deal for Synthes.** *Buffett:* would like it "a whole lot better if it was all for cash" — J&J has the cash, and paying in stock signals either that they think their own shares are fully valued or that the seller is making a mistake. *Munger:* J&J also strayed from what it knows — "neither of us knows much about medical devices." (Parallels his opposition to Kraft's stock purchase of Cadbury.) See [stock-as-acquisition-currency](../concepts/stock-as-acquisition-currency.md).

**A36 · Why Berkshire won't issue stock to make an acquisition.** *Buffett:* cash will build unless deals appear, and Berkshire won't borrow heavily, won't sell a business to buy another, and will issue shares only in "some minor amount"; issuing means "divesting ourselves of a portion of every wonderful business we have, from GEICO to ISCAR." Confetti-for-real-assets (the late-'60s and dot-com waves) "usually ends in some kind of a fiasco." *Munger:* recounts a socialist executive delighted to get a business "for nothing" by issuing controlled-corporation shares. See [stock-as-acquisition-currency](../concepts/stock-as-acquisition-currency.md).

**★ A37 · Housing is terrible now — buy the cyclical cheap and wait.** *Buffett:* Shaw, MiTek, Acme Brick, Johns Manville, and HomeServices are all flatlined, yet Berkshire bought Alabama's largest brick operation for cash — because over 20 years housing must track household formation. *Munger:* "one advantage of buying these very cyclical businesses is a lot of people don't like them … what do we care if it's lumpy, as long as it's a good business? Nobody else was bidding for a brick plant in Alabama with no customers." *Buffett:* like See's losing money eight months a year — a known seasonal (or cyclical) pattern shouldn't scare a long-term owner. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**A43 · Greenhouse-gas shareholder motion (formal meeting).** *Buffett (recommending a "no"):* the climate resolution poses no *material* risk to Berkshire's insurance operations per the 10-K, and unilaterally closing coal generation wouldn't cost Berkshire shareholders — regulated utilities recoup accelerated depreciation through higher rates, so "the burden would fall on customers," and that judgment "quite properly should be made by the public utility commissions." A clean illustration of regulated-utility economics: the owner earns an allowed return regardless, ratepayers bear the cost of the generation mix. (The substantive climate-policy debate itself is dropped as off-domain.) See [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md).

## Connections to other sources

- The three-category asset map (M16) is the spoken twin of the extended gold-vs-productive-assets argument in [berkshire-2011-letter](berkshire-2011-letter.md); the meeting adds the all-farmland-plus-ten-ExxonMobils comparison.
- The "forget about goodwill" two-lens rule (A14) restates for a live audience the tangible-capital-return distinction Buffett has drawn since the 1983 goodwill appendix; here anchored to the fresh Lubrizol math.
- The governance material (M4, M6, M8, M22) is the meeting-floor sequel to the 2010 discussion of Goldman/Abacus and Sokol's rising profile in [berkshire-2010-meeting](berkshire-2010-meeting.md); the 2011 scandal turns those abstractions into a case study, and the SEC ultimately closed its Sokol investigation without charges in 2013.
- The "won't spend undervalued stock" theme (M11, A35, A36) extends the acquisition-currency reasoning from the Burlington deal and the Kraft/Cadbury criticism of prior years — the durable rule that share issuance is a partial sale of everything you already own.
- The reinsurance-is-hard admission (A33) and the "unbelievable insurance operation" praise (A10) bracket the catastrophe-loss year (M3), showing the discipline that turns a worst-ever-quarter into a still-profitable year — the applied form of the [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md) developed across the letters.
