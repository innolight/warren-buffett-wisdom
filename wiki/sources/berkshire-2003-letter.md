# Berkshire Hathaway 2003 Annual Letter

The 2003 letter advances four durable lessons: that securitization severs the accountability link between lenders and their lending decisions, ultimately destroying any industry built on it; that true governance alignment cannot be manufactured by formal independence rules but only by shared financial stakes; that the cost of known inaction compounds — and arrives concentrated at the worst possible moment; and that float's power is entirely a function of underwriting discipline, which is managerial rather than structural. The Clayton Homes acquisition, the Berkshire board restructuring, the Gen Re Securities confession, and the $44.2B float record are vehicles for these lessons, not the lessons themselves. Per-share book value rose 21%; net worth gained $13.6B.

## Key themes

**How securitization destroys a viable industry.** Manufactured housing had accounted for more than 15% of U.S. homes built for decades — a real product delivering genuine value. What destroyed it was an incentive-misalignment structure: an originate-and-distribute model where retailers and manufacturers could "unload terrible loans on naive lenders," and where securitization in the 1990s further widened the gap between who made lending decisions and who bore their consequences. "Much of its volume a few years back came from buyers who shouldn't have bought, financed by lenders who shouldn't have lent." Berkshire's prior entry via distressed Oakwood Homes junk debt — Oakwood went promptly bankrupt — was the tuition that made Clayton recognizable as the class act of the industry. The structural lesson: when the originator of a loan doesn't hold it, the loan's terms reflect the originator's economics (maximize volume) rather than the lender's economics (minimize default). Buffett's 2003 diagnosis is the template for understanding the 2008 housing crisis five years before it arrived. Post-acquisition, Berkshire restructured Clayton's financing on "every tub on its own bottom" principles: Clayton borrows through Berkshire at cost plus one percentage point, with no parent subsidy — because having a rich parent can lead to sloppy decisions.

**True independence requires skin in the game, not statutory designation.** Two complementary governance case studies in the same letter. The mutual-fund case: statutory-independent directors had routinely rehired incumbent management regardless of performance, mindlessly approved above-market fees, and after scandals broke none terminated a single offending manager's contract. "Boardroom atmosphere almost invariably sedates their fiduciary genes." The root cause is structural: a director receiving 100% of income from director fees has a larger financial conflict of interest than Berkshire director Ron Olson receiving 3% from legal fees — yet the rules call only the first "independent." Buffett's concrete reform: two annual affirmations that directors (1) reviewed alternative managers and chose among the better operators, and (2) negotiated a fee comparable to what equivalent third-party clients receive. "If you don't know whose side someone is on, he's probably not on yours."

Berkshire's own board, by contrast: 11 directors each owning $4M+ of Berkshire personally (six with holdings of at least hundreds of millions), all purchased in the open market over 30+ years, no options, no restricted shares, no directors-and-officers liability insurance. Director downside exceeds shareholder downside. "You win, they win big; you lose, they lose big." The operative principle: financial alignment cannot be rule-written into existence; it must be quantified, real, and asymmetric. Four new directors elected in 2003 — including Tom Murphy — were chosen from 20+ qualified applicants who responded to a self-nomination call. The board's primary stated job: select Buffett's successor.

**The compounding cost of known inaction.** At the time of the 1998 General Reinsurance merger, Buffett and Munger both knew Gen Re Securities' derivatives book had "illusory" reported profits, carried risks that could not be measured or limited, and — crucially — would consume Berkshire's capital exactly when it could otherwise be deployed to greatest advantage. Munger would have shut it down immediately. Buffett dithered. The three-year wind-down cost $272M pre-tax — in a benign, orderly market with no credit losses, starting from 23,218 outstanding tickets and ending 2003 with 7,580 still open. This is the minimum-scenario cost of inaction on a known problem. The counterfactual: a financial crisis forcing simultaneous unwinding across multiple dealers would have multiplied that cost and consumed Berkshire's capital at precisely the wrong moment. The 1974 insurance-fraud precedent makes the mechanism visible in miniature: holding excess cash to measure fraud impact meant missing an "extraordinarily cheap" stock market. The principle transfers to any situation where a problem is identified, its nature understood, and action deferred — the delay multiplies the cost and concentrates it at the worst moment.

**Float — the engine, confirmed at scale.** Float reached $44.2B at yearend 2003, up from $20M when Berkshire entered insurance in 1967. The underwriting result: $1.7B pre-tax profit across all segments, meaning Berkshire was paid to hold $44B of other people's money. In 18 of 37 years Berkshire has operated at an underwriting profit. The meta-lesson: insurance has no structural moat. "Insurers sell a non-proprietary piece of paper containing a non-proprietary promise. Anyone can copy anyone else's product. No installed base, key patents, critical real estate or natural resource position protects an insurer's competitive position. Typically, brands do not mean much either." The competitive position is entirely managerial — discipline, brains, integrity. GEICO's decade under Tony Nicely (CEO from 1992): premium volume from $2.2B to $8.1B, market share from 2.1% to 5.0%, with outstanding underwriting maintained throughout. Ajit Jain runs the equivalent of the world's largest reinsurance operation with a staff of 23. The moat is the human discipline — which is why it is so rare and so durable when genuine.

**Handshake acquisitions and the sufficiency of honesty.** McLane — a $23B-revenue wholesale food and nonfood distributor — was acquired from Wal-Mart in a single 2-hour meeting, no formal due diligence, 29 days to close. The principle: due diligence is not a ritual; it is a mechanism to compensate for uncertainty about the seller's honesty. When you have high confidence in the seller's integrity — because they have decades of reputation for saying exactly what they mean — formal verification adds no information. "We knew everything would be exactly as Wal-Mart said it would be — and it was." The three-condition acquisition screen (favorable and enduring economics, talented and honest management, sensible price) is sufficient; due diligence only moves the needle when honesty is in doubt.

**Capital discipline when nothing is cheap.** The core equity portfolio was effectively frozen: major positions last changed between 1973 (Washington Post) and 2003 (Wells Fargo). Buffett admitted the portfolio was "fully priced" and capital underutilized. The discipline: "It's a painful condition to be in — but not as painful as doing something stupid." The mirror failure acknowledged explicitly: "If these stocks are fully priced now, you may wonder what I was thinking four years ago when their intrinsic value was lower and their prices far higher. So do I." Not selling into the bubble is named as a large mistake — the same contrarian discipline that commands patience when nothing is cheap should have enforced selling when valuations were absurd.

## Notable quotes

> Much of its volume a few years back came from buyers who shouldn't have bought, financed by lenders who shouldn't have lent.

> Charlie would have moved swiftly to close down Gen Re Securities – no question about that. I, however, dithered. As a consequence, our shareholders are paying a far higher price than was necessary to exit this business.

> No matter how financially sophisticated you are, you can't possibly learn from reading the disclosure documents of a derivatives-intensive company what risks lurk in its positions. Indeed, the more you know about derivatives, the less you will feel you can learn from the disclosures normally proffered you.

> Float is wonderful – if it doesn't come at a high price.

> Insurers sell a non-proprietary piece of paper containing a non-proprietary promise. Anyone can copy anyone else's product. No installed base, key patents, critical real estate or natural resource position protects an insurer's competitive position. Typically, brands do not mean much either.

> The bottom line for our directors: You win, they win big; you lose, they lose big. Our approach might be called owner-capitalism.

> Despite the lapdog behavior of independent fund directors, we did not conclude that they are bad people. They're not. But sadly, "boardroom atmosphere" almost invariably sedates their fiduciary genes.

> If you don't know whose side someone is on, he's probably not on yours.

> We did no "due diligence." We knew everything would be exactly as Wal-Mart said it would be – and it was.

> Our capital is underutilized now, but that will happen periodically. It's a painful condition to be in – but not as painful as doing something stupid. (I speak from experience.)
