# Berkshire Hathaway 1983 Shareholder Letter

Book value per share up 32% to $975.83; 19-year compound 22.6%. The shareholder base roughly doubles from ~1,000 a few years prior to ~2,900 after the Blue Chip merger completed mid-year. This is one of the most concentrated letters in the canon — four landmark essays in one document: the **owner's manual** (13 business principles), **intrinsic value vs. book value**, the **Goodwill appendix**, and the **stock-splits / shareholder-eugenics** treatise. Plus the acquisition of **Nebraska Furniture Mart** and introduction of Mrs. B.

## Key themes

- **The owner's manual — 13 business principles, first articulation.** A consolidated statement to the (now-doubled) shareholder base. Core elements: corporate form, partnership attitude — "We do not view the company itself as the ultimate owner of our business assets but, instead, view the company as a conduit through which our shareholders own the assets." Directors eat their own cooking (>50% of family net worth in Berkshire for ≥4 of 5). Long-term goal: maximize per-share intrinsic value, not size. Conservative debt. Issuance only at business-value-for-business-value. Candid reporting. The retention test: $1 of market value per $1 retained, judged on five-year rolling basis. See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).
- **Intrinsic value vs. book value.** Formal distinction: book value = accumulated financial input (contributed capital + retained earnings); intrinsic value = future cash output discounted to present. "Book value tells you what has been put in; intrinsic business value estimates what can be taken out." The college-tuition analogy. See [intrinsic-value-vs-book-value](../concepts/intrinsic-value-vs-book-value.md).
- **Economic vs. accounting Goodwill (appendix).** Buffett's escape from Graham's tangible-asset bias. Businesses with enduring economic Goodwill and minimal tangible capital are the high-return-on-incremental-capital engines that compound through inflation. The Appendix explains why economic and accounting Goodwill diverge — and praises businesses that "possess large amounts of enduring Goodwill and that utilize a minimum of tangible assets." Keynes: "The difficulty lies not in the new ideas but in escaping from the old ones." See [economic-goodwill](../concepts/economic-goodwill.md).
- **Nebraska Furniture Mart — Mrs. B and the $55M handshake deal.** 90% acquired for $55M; no audit, no inventory, no title check — "we gave Mrs. B a check for $55 million and she gave us her word. That made for an even exchange." NFM does over $100M of sales from a single 200,000 sq-ft store — more than all Omaha competitors combined. Mrs. B, age 90, on the sales floor seven days a week. The Blumkin family: Louie + three grandsons. The business as canonical low-cost operator. See [nebraska-furniture-mart](../entities/nebraska-furniture-mart.md) and [rose-blumkin](../people/rose-blumkin.md).
- **Buffalo News penetration as moat.** Among the 100 largest U.S. dailies, the News is #1 in weekday penetration and #3 in Sunday penetration. Sunday circulation 376,000 (up from the Courier-Express's 270,000 pre-1977). Three factors: stable population, editorial reputation, and an unusual 50% news-hole ratio (vs. industry high-30s). See [buffalo-evening-news](../entities/buffalo-evening-news.md).
- **See's Candies: pricing power but flat units.** 12-year table showing sales 4x (from $31M to $134M) and after-tax profits 7x ($2M to $13.7M) while pounds sold grew only ~45% (16.9M → 24.7M). Same-store volume down 0.8% in 1983 — best in four years. "In candy, as in stocks, price and value can differ; price is what you give, value is what you get."
- **Stock splits / shareholder eugenics.** The argument against splits: Berkshire seeks rational shareholders, attracted via consistent owner-oriented communication. "Were we to split the stock… we would attract an entering class of buyers inferior to the exiting class of sellers." Splits would increase trading frictional cost — "a hyperactive stock market is the pickpocket of enterprise." Estimates: Berkshire's per-year frictional cost of ownership transfer ~0.06% of market value; typical 100%-turnover stock with 1% spreads costs ~2%. "What is good for the croupier is not good for the customer."
- **Capital-allocation acknowledgment.** Insurance combined ratio of 121; "I made some serious mistakes a few years ago that came home to roost." GEICO (which Buffett does not operate) wrote at 96. The contrast itself is the lesson.
- **Blue Chip merger completes.** 1,146,909 shares outstanding after merger vs. 1,137,778 at the start of present management's tenure in 1965 — ~1% net dilution over 19 years to add everything outside the original textile business.
- **Reinsurance / structured settlements / loss-reserve assumptions.** New lines being entered, leveraging Berkshire's premier financial strength as a competitive advantage. The strategic insight: in long-tail businesses, balance-sheet strength *is* the product.
