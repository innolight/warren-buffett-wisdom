# Berkshire Hathaway 1996 Annual Letter

Dated February 28, 1997. Net worth up $6.2B (36.1%); per-share book value up 31.8% to $19,011. The headline 1996 events: **GEICO's first full year as a wholly-owned subsidiary** delivering a "stunning" 10% voluntary policy-count gain (best in 20 years); two acquisitions — **Kansas Bankers Surety** ($75M, placed at Wesco) and **FlightSafety International** (~$1.5B, the company's first capital-intensive acquisition); the canonical **"Inevitables" essay** on Coca-Cola and Gillette; the **two-column intrinsic-value table** introduced in 1995 updated and extended; the **Class B offering** completed in May; and a partial **Salomon stake monetization** via exchangeable notes. The letter also reproduces the Owner's Manual in full (pp. 58–67), and offers a fresh, harder-edged restatement of the [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md) rule for individual investors.

## Key themes

- **The "Inevitables."** A small set of businesses — Buffett names only [coca-cola](../entities/coca-cola.md) and [gillette](../entities/gillette.md) — whose worldwide competitive dominance over a 10–20 year horizon is, in practical terms, certain. Distinguished from "Highly Probables" and from a much larger universe of "Impostors." The 1896 Coke report (Asa Candler) is invoked to show the 100-year continuity of the franchise. The companion warning: even Inevitables can be ruined by management losing focus (Coke once growing shrimp; Gillette once exploring for oil).
- **GEICO's first wholly-owned year exceeds high expectations.** Voluntary auto policies +10% (vs. previous 20-year best of 8%, hit only once). The [moat](../concepts/moat.md) articulated as a virtuous circle: low costs → low prices → policyholder retention → referrals (>1M/year, >50% of new business) → still-lower acquisition costs. New compensation plan: bonuses for all dozens of top executives and the profit-sharing plan for all 9,000 associates tied to exactly two variables — voluntary policy growth and seasoned underwriting profitability. The chart "went off the chart"; the answer is "enlarge the chart," not raise the bar.
- **FlightSafety acquired (~$1.5B).** Founder Al Ueltschi, 79, piloted Charles Lindbergh; founded the company in 1951 while still at Pan Am; 41 locations, 175 simulators (priced up to $19M each). Half of revenue from corporate-pilot training. **First major capital-intensive Berkshire acquisition** — the letter explicitly flags that simulators are not cheap. Tax-efficient deal structure nudged 51% to take cash, 41% to A shares, 8% to B shares — minimizing Berkshire share issuance.
- **Kansas Bankers Surety acquired ($75M).** Sourced from a birthday party seating chart — Buffett's nephew's wife's father (Roy Dinsdale) sat next to him; mentioned the company in passing; letter on Feb 12; offer of $75M on Feb 13. Placed at Wesco (80%-owned).
- **Circle-of-competence sharpened for the individual investor.** "Most investors … will find that the best way to own common stocks is through an index fund that charges minimal fees." For active investors: "How to Value a Business" and "How to Think About Market Prices" are the only two courses needed. Famous formulation: "If you aren't willing to own a stock for ten years, don't even think about owning it for ten minutes."
- **Industries unlikely to experience major change.** The investment posture is explicit: search for businesses "virtually certain to possess enormous competitive strength ten or twenty years from now." Fast-changing industries "preclude the certainty we seek." Analogized to space exploration: "We applaud the endeavor but prefer to skip the ride."
- **Overpayment risk explicitly flagged.** "The overpayment risk … may now be quite high for the purchasers of virtually all stocks, The Inevitables included." A direct caution about late-1996 market levels.
- **Float jumps to $6.7B.** With GEICO consolidated, average float more than doubles from $3.6B (1995) to $6.7B (1996); fourth consecutive year of negative cost of float. 1967–1996 annual float growth: 22.3%.
- **Super-cat: largest exposures ever.** Mid-1996 Allstate Florida-hurricane contract believed to be (then) "the largest single catastrophe risk ever assumed by one company for its own account"; later 1996 California Earthquake Authority contract covered "more than twice" that exposure. Worst-case after-tax loss still ~$600M (under 3% of book, 1.5% of market). The volatility framework restated: "Charlie and I would much rather earn a lumpy 15% over time than a smooth 12%."
- **Computer-model skepticism.** On super-cat pricing: "the precision they project is a chimera … such models can lull decision-makers into a false sense of security." Cites portfolio insurance / 1987 crash as the cautionary precedent.
- **Invert, always invert.** Munger's dictum invoked as the planning principle: "All I want to know is where I'm going to die so I'll never go there." Why Berkshire doesn't borrow big and caps its super-cat exposures.
- **Two-column intrinsic-value table updated.** Investments per share $28,500 (+29.0%); pre-tax non-investment earnings per share $421.39 (+63.2%). Methodology continues from 1995.
- **USAir reversal of fortune.** Stephen Wolf's turnaround triggered payment of $47.9M in penalty-rate arrearages (penalty dividend ran at 13.25–14% — prime + 5%). Preferred now trading near par. Buffett tried twice to dump the position (in 1995 at 50% of par; in early 1996 at ~$335M of $358M par) and "failed in my attempt to snatch defeat from the jaws of victory."
- **Salomon partial monetization.** $500M five-year exchangeable notes issued for $447.1M, exchangeable into 17.65 Salomon shares per $1,000 note. 3% yield to maturity if held; 1.1% effective interest if exchanged. Praise of Terry Fitzgerald at Salomon for the B-share offering structure (1.5% commission — the lowest Buffett had ever seen; open-ended size to repel flippers).
- **Class B offering — what happened.** 517,500 B shares sold in May for $565M net. Trading volume immediately after issuance "far below the norm for a new issue" — confirming the long-term-holder shareholder base. ~40,000 new shareholders added.
- **Owner's Manual reproduced in the report (pp. 58–67).** Reintroduced after distribution to new B-share holders in June 1996.

## Notable quotes

> Charlie and I would much rather earn a lumpy 15% over time than a smooth 12%. (After all, our earnings swing wildly on a daily and weekly basis - why should we demand that smoothness accompany each orbit that the earth makes of the sun?)

> In our super-cat operation, our customers are insurers that are exposed to major earnings volatility and that wish to reduce it. The product we sell - for what we hope is an appropriate price - is our willingness to shift that volatility to our own books.

> The economies of scale we enjoy should allow us to maintain or even widen the protective moat surrounding our economic castle.

> GEICO's sustainable cost advantage is what attracted me to the company way back in 1951, when the entire business was valued at $7 million. It is also why I felt Berkshire should pay $2.3 billion last year for the 49% of the company that we didn't then own.

> If you bat .350 at Berkshire, you can be sure you will get paid commensurately even if the rest of the team bats .200.

> We continue to make more money when snoring than when active.

> Inactivity strikes us as intelligent behavior.

> The art of investing in public companies successfully is little different from the art of successfully acquiring subsidiaries. In each case you simply want to acquire, at a sensible price, a business with excellent economics and able, honest management. Thereafter, you need only monitor whether these qualities are being preserved.

> To suggest that this investor should sell off portions of his most successful investments simply because they have come to dominate his portfolio is akin to suggesting that the Bulls trade Michael Jordan because he has become so important to the team.

> A fast-changing industry environment may offer the chance for huge wins, but it precludes the certainty we seek.

> As investors, however, our reaction to a fermenting industry is much like our attitude toward space exploration: We applaud the endeavor but prefer to skip the ride.

> Companies such as Coca-Cola and Gillette might well be labeled "The Inevitables." … no sensible observer … questions that Coke and Gillette will dominate their fields worldwide for an investment lifetime.

> But I would rather be certain of a good result than hopeful of a great one.

> Thus, for every Inevitable, there are dozens of Impostors, companies now riding high but vulnerable to competitive attacks.

> The overpayment risk surfaces periodically and, in our opinion, may now be quite high for the purchasers of virtually all stocks, The Inevitables included.

> A far more serious problem occurs when the management of a great company gets sidetracked and neglects its wonderful base business while purchasing other businesses that are so-so or worse.

> Most investors, both institutional and individual, will find that the best way to own common stocks is through an index fund that charges minimal fees. Those following this path are sure to beat the net results (after fees and expenses) delivered by the great majority of investment professionals.

> What an investor needs is the ability to correctly evaluate selected businesses. Note that word "selected": You don't have to be an expert on every company, or even many. You only have to be able to evaluate companies within your circle of competence. The size of that circle is not very important; knowing its boundaries, however, is vital.

> investment students need only two well-taught courses - How to Value a Business, and How to Think About Market Prices.

> If you aren't willing to own a stock for ten years, don't even think about owning it for ten minutes.

> "All I want to know is where I'm going to die so I'll never go there." [Charlie's dictum.]

> Inverting really works: Try singing country western songs backwards and you will quickly regain your house, your car and your wife.

> "I don't know. I've never sold any." [Robert Woodruff, asked when it might be a good time to sell Coke stock.]

> "There's really nothing to it. Start as a billionaire and then buy an airline." [Richard Branson, on how to become a millionaire.]

> If history supplied all of the answers, the Forbes 400 would consist of librarians.

> I again failed in my attempt to snatch defeat from the jaws of victory.

> "If you're so rich, why aren't you smart?"

> In the securities business, whatever can be sold will be sold.

> We are here to make money with you, not off you.

> It's difficult to teach a new dog old tricks. [On Berkshire's preference for older managers.]

> we shun "lottery ticket" arrangements, such as options on Berkshire shares, whose ultimate value - which could range from zero to huge - is totally out of the control of the person whose behavior we would like to affect.

> In insurance, it is essential to remember that virtually all surprises are unpleasant.

## The "Inevitables" essay

Buffett's clearest statement of the certainty-of-dominance criterion. Two companies named outright: [coca-cola](../entities/coca-cola.md) and [gillette](../entities/gillette.md). "Survival of the Fattest" as a near-natural law in these industries.

The Asa Candler 1896 quote ("we have not lagged in our efforts to go into all the world teaching that Coca-Cola is the article, par excellence …") is invoked to show the franchise's 100-year continuity. Coke's 1896 syrup sales: 116,492 gallons. 1996: ~3.2 billion gallons.

Critical distinction:
- **Inevitables** — durable global dominance, certainty across 10–20 years.
- **Highly Probables** — added to the portfolio when truly inevitable cases are scarce.
- **Impostors** — "companies now riding high but vulnerable" (GM, IBM, Sears as historical reminders).

Two failure modes for Inevitables flagged explicitly:
1. **Overpayment at the security level.** "It may often take an extended period for the value of even an outstanding company to catch up with the price they paid." (And: 1996 prices "may now be quite high.")
2. **Loss of management focus.** Coke once grew shrimp; Gillette once explored for oil. "Loss of focus is what most worries Charlie and me when we contemplate investing in businesses that in general look outstanding."

## GEICO — first wholly-owned year

The "virtuous circle" articulated:
- Low costs → low prices → policyholder attraction and retention → referrals (>1M/year, >50% of new business) → still lower acquisition costs.

Strategic choice: "Our goal, however, is not to widen our profit margin but rather to enlarge the price advantage we offer customers." Compounding-the-moat behavior.

Compensation plan installed immediately after acquisition:
- Two variables only: (1) voluntary policy growth, (2) seasoned (>1 year) underwriting profitability.
- Same yardsticks govern the 9,000-associate profit-sharing plan.
- 1996 contribution: 16.9% ($40M) vs. 5-year average <10% under prior plans.

[lou-simpson](../people/lou-simpson.md)'s portfolio outperformed the S&P 500 by 6.2 percentage points in 1996. Lou's comp is tied to investment performance over a four-year period — not to underwriting, and not to overall GEICO results. Rationale: "We think it foolish for an insurance company to pay bonuses that are tied to overall corporate results when great work on one side of the business … could conceivably be completely neutralized by bad work on the other."

The 1995 letter's accounting-arcana footnote: the GEICO step-acquisition required Berkshire to write down its original 51% by $478.4M (1995 restated), since the prior 51% must be carried at less than the 49% just bought. Buffett notes the economic value rose even as book fell.

## FlightSafety International

- ~$1.5B acquisition. Initiated by long-time Berkshire shareholder Richard Sercer (wife Alma Murphy bought the Berkshire shares in 1990); routed through Bob Denham at Salomon.
- Al Ueltschi (79) founded FlightSafety in 1951 while still working for Juan Trippe at Pan Am. Piloted Charles Lindbergh.
- 41 locations, 175 simulators (Cessna 210s to Boeing 747s); simulators up to $19M each. ~50% of revenue from corporate pilots; rest from airlines and military.
- **Capital-intensive — explicitly flagged as unusual for Berkshire.**
- Deal structured to favor cash takers (tax-indifferent holders): 51% cash, 41% A shares, 8% B shares. Berkshire shares issuance minimized.

## Kansas Bankers Surety

- $75M, placed at Wesco. CEO Don Towle.
- Sourced via a birthday-party seating arrangement (Roy Dinsdale, Buffett's nephew's wife's father, KBS director). Letter Feb 12; offer Feb 13. Specialty: bankers' insurance in 22 states.

## Super-cat positioning — three competitive advantages

1. **Certainty of payment under adverse conditions.** "When it's Berkshire promising, insureds know with certainty that they can collect promptly." (And: "Indeed, one reason we never 'lay off' part of the risks we insure is that we have reservations about our ability to collect from others when disaster strikes.")
2. **Capacity available exactly when peers withdraw.** Post-event, when peers are damaged or reluctant, Berkshire is positioned to write — and existing clients have first call. "Stand-by" fees from reinsurers nailing down access were already being collected.
3. **Dollar-size of coverage.** "A single call to Berkshire will produce a firm and immediate offering."

1996 exposures:
- Allstate Florida-hurricane contract (mid-year) — claimed largest single-company catastrophe exposure ever assumed.
- California Earthquake Authority contract (April 1, 1997 effective) — Berkshire's ~$1B layer triggered if Authority losses 12/1/1996–3/31/2001 exceed ~$5B. CEA covers only 72% of insurer share, hence the smaller figure than initially press-reported.

Pricing methodology: target ~90% of premiums eventually paid out in losses + expenses.

Worst-case after-tax: ~$600M (≤3% book, ≤1.5% market). Buffett notes correlated damage (See's, Wells Fargo, Freddie Mac would all be hit by a major California quake) but Berkshire can handle the aggregation.

## USAir reversal

- Stephen Wolf turnaround triggered $47.9M payment in penalty-rate arrearages in H2 1996.
- The 1989 preferred contract had a **prime + 5% penalty dividend on arrearages** — so the 9.25% suspended dividend compounded at 13.25–14%. The structural feature Buffett "got right" in an otherwise admitted mistake.
- Buffett tried to sell the position twice during the trough: 1995 at 50% of face; early 1996 at ~$335M (of $358M par). Both attempts failed; preferred subsequently rallied to ~par.
- Cumulative dividends received: $240.5M (including $30M received in 1997).
- Cautions: industry tailwind "may be cyclical"; "company still has basic cost problems that must be solved."

See [usair](../entities/usair.md).

## Operating businesses — 1996 recovery

All three 1995 problem children (Buffalo News, Shoe Group, World Book) reported earnings gains. Only disappointment: Helzberg's (Jeff Comment) — expense levels geared to growth that didn't materialize. Borsheim's fine.

World Book is notable: the only remaining direct-sales encyclopedia after Encyclopedia Britannica exited the field; new IBM-partnered CD-ROM product launched early 1997; distribution method revamp and HQ overhead cuts.

## Internet distribution

Berkshire begins posting quarterly and annual reports at berkshirehathaway.com — always on a Saturday. Buffett asks shareholders' preference on whether to discontinue mailed quarterlies.

## Taxes

Berkshire paid $860M in federal income taxes in 1996. "If an equal amount had been paid by only 2,000 other taxpayers, the government would have had a balanced budget in 1996."
