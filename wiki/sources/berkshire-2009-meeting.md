# Berkshire Hathaway 2009 Annual Meeting

Held May 2, 2009 — barely seven weeks after the March 9 market bottom, in the depths of the financial crisis — this is Berkshire's crisis meeting. The format changed (three journalists screening ~5,000 shareholder questions to keep the meeting on Berkshire), and the mood is sober but not defensive: Buffett and Munger use the wreckage as a teaching lab. The load-bearing lessons are the timeless ones restated under fire: that **leverage, not volatility, is what actually destroys investors** — "you never want to be in a position where somebody can pull the rug out from under you… never want to be emotionally in a position where you pull the rug out from under yourself"; that you **price, you don't time** — "picking bottoms is basically not our game; pricing is our game"; that **false precision and higher mathematics are dangerous** ("if you need to use a computer or a calculator… you shouldn't buy it"; LTCM and the rating agencies as exhibits); that a **moat is the incumbents' inability to react, and moats decay** (GEICO widening its lead as frightened consumers hunt for value; newspapers "filling up with sand"); and that **culture — decentralization, buy-to-keep, a "seamless web of trust" — is the durable competitive advantage** that outlasts the two men on stage. It is also the year of the great crisis deals (Goldman, GE, Swiss Re, Dow, Constellation) and the year Berkshire lost its triple-A rating.

Because it is a transcript, this page keeps the wiki's two-layer convention: a distilled top of durable themes, then a **comprehensive Q&A digest** capturing every value-bearing exchange.

## How to read the Q&A digest

- **★** marks a timeless, load-bearing exchange worth citing in the future; unmarked entries carry investor value but are secondary or period-specific.
- Answers are attributed separately to **Buffett** and **Munger** — a Munger line appears only when he added a distinct lesson (he answered "Nothing to add" to most).
- **M#/A#** map to the numbered items in the raw morning/afternoon transcript for traceability.

## Key themes

### Leverage is what kills you; volatility is only dangerous if it can force your hand

The crisis distilled Buffett's risk creed to its operational core: the investors who were destroyed were destroyed by leverage and by being forced (or scaring themselves into) selling at the bottom, not by the price decline itself. Wells Fargo ticked below $9 "at a time when spreads on business were never better… when their advantage in relation of costs of funds versus other large banks had never been better" — the business was strengthening while the quote collapsed. The rule that generalizes: "you never want to be in a position where somebody can pull the rug out from under you. And you also never want to be emotionally in a position where you pull the rug out from under yourself." Own the farm 30 miles from Omaha that gives you no daily quote, and you judge it by what it produces, not by a ticker. See [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md), [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md).

### Price, don't time — and buy fast when you're getting a lot for your money

Buffett wrote his October 2008 "Buy American" op-ed not as a forecast but because "I do know when you're starting to get a lot for your money." He never predicts the bottom: "picking bottoms is basically not our game. Pricing is our game." The hamburger test — if McDonald's cut prices tomorrow "I'm happy… I'm going to be buying hamburgers the rest of my life" — reframes a falling market as a sale for a lifelong net buyer. And when the price is right you barrel in: "our idea is not to tiptoe into anything." Munger's crisis coda: the great buying windows (1973–74) arrive precisely when you have the least money to deploy — "that's why those times occur." See [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

### False precision and higher mathematics are dangerous

The rating agencies "eagerly sought stupid assumptions that enabled them to do clever mathematics" (Munger) — they built the near-universal belief that house prices could not fall into a formal model, then "believed their own baloney" and bought the triple-As themselves. Buffett's positive rule: valuation should be obvious. "If you need to use a computer or a calculator to make the calculation, you shouldn't buy it… It should scream at you." The discount is Aesop's — "a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush" — dressed up with a Ph.D. only "to impress the laity." LTCM in 1998 and September 2008 both prove that market breaks "arise much more often than people think" and cannot be captured by a standard deviation. See [model-risk](../concepts/model-risk.md), [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md).

### The moat is the incumbents' inability to react — and moats decay

GEICO is the live proof: when the crisis "rang a bell" and consumers turned value-conscious, the phones rang off the hook (665,000 added in 2008 and 505,000 in the first four months of 2009) — because GEICO is the low-cost producer, an advantage "built up over decades" that State Farm and Allstate watched grow without being able to match. But the same crisis exposed the other edge: newspapers have lost the "essentiality" that made them the ultimate business, and Berkshire "would not buy them at any price." Munger's summary verdict: "a lot of moats have been filling up with sand lately… all these castles with their lovely moats. The moats are filling up." A moat is a fact about competitors' constraints, and those facts change. See [moat](../concepts/moat.md), [direct-distribution-moat](../concepts/direct-distribution-moat.md), [media-economics](../concepts/media-economics.md).

### Culture — not the two men on stage — is the durable competitive advantage

Asked whether Berkshire's edge is simply Buffett and Munger and therefore unsustainable, Buffett rejected the premise: "our sustainable competitive advantage is we have a culture and a business model, which people are going to find very, very difficult to copy." Its parts are concrete: a shareholder base that turns over ~20% a year against 100% for the S&P 500; a buy-to-keep promise that lets private owners "continue to run their business, not bothered by bankers or lawyers"; tax-free reallocation of See's-style cash to better uses inside a consolidated return; and a decentralization so radical that near-nobody sits at headquarters. Munger: "Our model is a seamless web of trust that's deserved on both sides. The Hollywood model, where everyone has a contract, and no trust is deserved on either side, is not what we want at all." And the moat widens by comparison — "the stupidity of management practice in the rest of the corporate world will likely remain ample enough to give this company some comparative advantage way into the future." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

### Compensation and boards: the honor system

Buffett's most caustic governance session in the corpus. The CEO effectively sets his own pay because he shapes the comp committee: "people are not looking for Dobermans. They're looking for Cocker Spaniels… wagging their tails, very friendly." The consultant, hired by the CEO's HR head, never upsets the apple cart. The result: "the shareholders have the honor and the executives have the system." Legislation makes it worse — the Clinton-era $1M deductibility cap was "the most counterproductive piece of legislation that Congress has ever come up with," ratcheting pay upward. The fix is embarrassment: three or four of the largest investors speaking out on the egregious cases, because "the way you get big shots to change their behavior is to embarrass them." Munger goes further: directors "would be managed better if the directors weren't paid at all," and a director with much to lose from the seat cannot be independent. In capital-intensive subsidiaries, rational pay must include an explicit capital charge. See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md), [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

### Inflation is coming — defend with earning power and capital-light franchises

Buffett to an 11-year-old questioner: "it's certain we will have inflation over time." The bill will be paid not by taxpayers (taxes weren't raised) but by holders of fixed-dollar investments — "probably the Chinese" — via debased purchasing power, "the easiest thing to do" and therefore "the most likely thing to have happen." The two defenses: first, "your own earning power is the best, by far" — the best surgeon or teacher commands their share of output "whether it's seashells, or reichsmarks, or dollars"; second, "a wonderful business that does not require heavy capital investment," like Coca-Cola's trademark. A capital-hungry business "gets killed in inflation." Behind it all sits the American tailwind: a 7-for-1 improvement in living standards across the 20th century despite a civil war, two world wars, and the Depression — progress "in fits and starts." See [bonds-and-inflation](../concepts/bonds-and-inflation.md), [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md), [american-tailwind](../concepts/american-tailwind.md).

## Q&A digest — Morning session

**M3 · Negative Treasury yields — money under the mattress beat T-bills.** *Buffett:* Berkshire sold $5M of T-bills in December 2008 for $5,000,090.07 — buyers accepted a negative yield rather than hold cash; "I'm not sure you'll see that again in your lifetime." A vivid marker of crisis-era fear-driven mispricing.

**★ M6 · Our stock-index derivatives aren't dangerous.** *Buffett:* the equity puts pose no capital risk — collateral posting on March 31 was under 1% of marketable securities; Berkshire holds the ~$4.9B premium for 15–20 years and only pays if indexes are lower at expiry, so "the odds are extremely good" the equity puts make money. The high-yield credit-default swaps, by contrast, will likely lose money before investment income — "We've, in effect, had a financial hurricane… we insure against a financial hurricane." Berkshire recently modified two puts, on one cutting the strike from 1514 to 994 in exchange for shortening the term. *Munger:* there is "some limit to the amount of those things we should do. But… we stayed well short of the limit." See [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md).

**M7 · Financial literacy.** *Buffett:* the current generation, not just future ones, has a literacy problem; borrowing at 18–21% on credit cards is a losing game — but the more people do foolish things with money, "the more people think that borrowing money on credit cards is intelligent… we'll go our way and they'll go their way." Others' folly is the value investor's opportunity. *Munger:* state lotteries and high-cost credit-card debt mean "we've been going in the wrong direction."

**★ M8 · Buffett "commends" Washington's crisis response.** *Buffett:* mid-September 2008 was "as close to a total meltdown throughout the financial system as I think you can imagine" — a couple hundred billion left money-market funds in days, commercial paper froze, "we really were looking into the abyss." Given 20-hour days and imperfect information, "overall, they did a very, very good job." Recommends Jamie Dimon's shareholder letter. *Munger:* a government "reacting to the biggest financial crisis in 70 years… is entitled to be judged more leniently"; separately calls mark-to-market gains on your own collapsing credit "insane accounting."

**★ M9 · Higher mathematics can be dangerous.** *Buffett:* all investing is "laying out cash now to get more cash back at a later date" — Aesop's bird in the hand; the questions are how many birds, how sure, when, and the discount rate. "If you need to use a computer or a calculator to make the calculation, you shouldn't buy it… It should scream at you." False precision (two- and three-standard-deviation claims) blew up LTCM and "only happens to people with high IQs." *Munger:* "some of the worst business decisions I've ever seen are those that are done with a lot of formal projections… you get to believe the figures." See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [model-risk](../concepts/model-risk.md).

**★ M10 · Moody's wasn't alone in its mistake.** *Buffett:* the rating agencies' failure was analytical, not primarily conflict-of-interest — they, like Congress, bankers, borrowers and buyers, built the belief that house prices couldn't fall significantly into their models. Berkshire keeps Moody's because it's a low-capital business, but "Charlie and I don't pay any attention to ratings… we don't believe in outsourcing investment decisions." The one reason never acceptable at Berkshire for any action: "everybody else is doing it." *Munger:* the agencies "eagerly sought stupid assumptions that enabled them to do clever mathematics… To a man with hammer, every problem looks pretty much like a nail." See [model-risk](../concepts/model-risk.md), [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md).

**M11 · Housing is beginning to improve.** *Buffett:* a transferable supply/demand frame — ~1.3M households form annually; two million housing starts created a glut of perhaps 1.5M units, now being absorbed as starts fell to ~500,000. "You could blow up a million and a half houses… or we can produce less than the natural demand increase." South Florida "tough for a long, long time." *Munger:* in Omaha, with low rates and good credit, "I would buy it tomorrow." (Period forecast; the household-formation math is the durable tool.)

**M12 · 2008 humbled the investment-manager candidates.** *Buffett:* all four succession candidates for the investment role remain on the list but "did no better than match the S&P" (−37%) — "I didn't cover myself with glory, either." Their 10-year records are modestly-to-significantly above average. Note: three CEO candidates are all internal; investment managers may be multiple and from outside. *Munger:* "practically every investment manager that I know of… got creamed last year," and a manager who claimed he could go to cash on macro calls and hop back in would be excluded — "they're not dumb enough for us."

**M14 · Why we don't train or name the next CEO.** *Buffett:* the CEO candidates already run major businesses and allocate capital daily; parking one at headquarters "would be a waste of talent." The successor comes from within; the only new skill is building relationships with sellers and shareholders, which "is nothing that really needs to be hastened." Naming a crown prince risks losing the runners-up (cf. GE after Immelt). *Munger:* "you're more likely to be qualified to be a CEO by running a subsidiary with an enormous amount of discretion than you are to being around headquarters" — the decentralized Johnson & Johnson model, not a headquarters hothouse.

**★ M15 · Buffett's business school: only two courses.** *Buffett:* "how to value a business" and "how to think about markets" — no modern portfolio theory, beta, or efficient markets ("we'd get rid of that in the first 10 minutes"). Stay in your circle of competence; you don't have to value 4,000 companies, or 40. Beyond a basic level of intelligence, "your emotional make-up's more important" than raw IQ — "if you're in the investment business and you have an IQ of 150, sell 30 points to somebody else." It's "simple, but not easy." On why nutty ideas (like efficient-market theory) persist, he quotes Max Planck: "Science advances one funeral at a time." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md).

**★ M16 · Ajit Jain's successor won't get the same authority.** *Buffett:* Ajit is irreplaceable, so Berkshire "wouldn't give the latitude… that we give to Ajit" — "the authority goes with the individual" not the position. Giving your pen away in insurance "is extraordinary dangerous": Mutual of Omaha lost half its net worth in a short time by handing a reinsurance pen to the wrong person. *Munger:* the "invest in a business a fool can run" maxim restated — "if it won't stand a little mismanagement it's not much of a business" — but you still prefer one managed magnificently. See [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**★ M17 · Berkshire was cheaper at the end of 2008.** *Buffett:* value Berkshire as two parts — the marketable investments (carried "at least fairly priced, or maybe even undervalued") plus the earning power of the operating businesses (leaving out insurance underwriting on the theory that break-even underwriting still yields free float). By that measure Berkshire was cheaper relative to intrinsic value at year-end 2008 than 2007 — but so was almost everything, because "every stock is affected by what every other stock sells for." *Munger:* "last year was a bad year for a float business" but long-term float "at a cost of less than zero" is a big advantage; Berkshire's casualty business, utility and ISCAR are best-in-world assets not easily replicated. See [intrinsic-value-vs-book-value](../concepts/intrinsic-value-vs-book-value.md), [float](../concepts/float.md).

**★ M18 · How GEICO benefited from the crisis.** *Buffett:* when the meltdown "rang a bell," consumer behavior changed overnight — it hurt jewelry, carpet, NetJets, and cut American Express's average ticket ~10% — but suddenly saving $100–150 on auto insurance mattered, and the phones rang. GEICO added 665,000 policyholders in 2008 and 505,000 in the first four months of 2009, becoming the fastest-growing large auto insurer and the third largest (~8.5% share, up from ~2% when Tony Nicely took over in 1993). The low-cost producer wins when customers turn value-conscious. See [geico](../entities/geico.md), [direct-distribution-moat](../concepts/direct-distribution-moat.md), [tony-nicely](../people/tony-nicely.md).

**M19 · No dividend despite a tough five years.** *Buffett:* a questioner showed that, measured on the December 31, 2008 price, five years of retained earnings had not produced a dollar of market value each — "he's absolutely right" at that market point, but Berkshire has never had a 5-year stretch underperforming the S&P on the book-value proxy for intrinsic value. The retention test still holds. *Munger:* "I don't get too excited about these oddball things that happen once every 50 years… I don't think we deserve any salt tears." See [dividend-policy](../concepts/dividend-policy.md).

**★ M20 · A price plunge shouldn't trigger selling — the Wells Fargo lesson.** *Buffett:* Wells ticked below $9 while its business (spreads, deposit flows, funding-cost advantage) had "never been better," and its Wachovia purchase was a great opportunity. Why anyone sells at $9 what they held at $25, when the business is stronger, "is one of the strange things about the way markets behave." Own it like the farm you don't get a daily quote on. "You never want to be in a position where somebody can pull the rug out from under you… never want to be emotionally in a position where you pull the rug out from under yourself." Read chapter 8 of *The Intelligent Investor*. *Munger:* "Wells Fargo's going to come out of this mess way stronger." *Supersession flag:* the fake-accounts scandal (surfacing 2016) badly damaged Wells; Berkshire exited the position entirely by 2022 — the "way stronger" thesis did not survive the culture failure. See [wells-fargo](../entities/wells-fargo.md), [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md), [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md).

**M22 · Hard to compete with government-guaranteed debt.** *Buffett:* Berkshire is at a real disadvantage where rivals fund with a government guarantee — its raw material (money) costs more, hurting Clayton's ~$10–11B manufactured-housing mortgage book despite excellent credit quality. "There are the blessed who have government guarantees. And there're the ones that aren't." But Berkshire funds mostly with float — ~$58B at less-than-zero cost — cheaper than even Wells's 1.12% cost of funds, just not as plentiful. *Munger:* "we aren't regulated like a bank… we'd be pretty ungrateful if we took this one disadvantage… and obsessed on it." See [clayton-homes](../entities/clayton-homes.md), [float](../concepts/float.md).

**★ M23 · Would Graham approve of derivatives?** *Buffett:* he'd dislike the system — derivatives turned Depression-era margin rules (50% limit) into "a laughingstock" via total-return swaps allowing 100% borrowing, and pushed settlement dates out to where "you get more and more defaults" — but he'd buy a mispriced one he could handle himself. *Munger:* the derivative dealer takes two advantages of the client — a "croupier-style mathematical advantage" like the house in Vegas, plus being "a better player" with knowledge of the client's positions. "This is basically a dirty business." See [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md), [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md).

**M24 · Bailouts shouldn't hurt senior debt holders.** *Buffett:* capital-structure logic is institution-specific — at Fannie and Freddie the preferred was effectively wiped out because there was no equity left; but at Wells, U.S. Bancorp and Goldman "there's lots of equity there… no reason for senior debt to give up anything," any more than a 70% home mortgage should be impaired because neighbors are struggling. Chrysler is the opposite: no competitive advantage, no common equity worth anything. Analyze the equity cushion, not the headline distress.

**M25 · Ignore your original cost when reviewing a portfolio.** *Buffett:* run "the half dozen or so stocks we like best" on price-versus-value alone — "It doesn't make any difference what the cost is," whether a holding doubled or fell 50%. Concentrate in the few you know very well. *Munger:* to the questioner proposing to trim winners into laggards — "he's tactfully suggesting that you adopt a different way of thinking." See [concentration-vs-diversification](../concepts/concentration-vs-diversification.md).

**★ M26 · Berkshire's competitive advantage no longer requires us.** *Buffett:* the edge is culture and business model, "very, very difficult to copy, even semi-copy" — a ~20%-turnover shareholder base, a buy-to-keep home for private owners who want to keep operating free of bankers and lawyers, and a deeply embedded culture that successor CEOs inherit. Sellers like ISCAR "really won't have a good second choice." *Munger:* while most corporations are "run stupidly from headquarters," forcing every division to beat last year's quarter, "the stupidity of management practice in the rest of the corporate world will likely remain ample enough to give this company some comparative advantage way into the future." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**M27 · We don't keep every stock, but we do keep every business.** *Buffett:* stocks get sold when the competitive advantage disappears, when faith in management is lost, or when the original analysis was wrong — "it's no inviolable rule." But wholly-owned businesses are for keeps, with only two exceptions: unending losses or major labor problems. In late September 2008 he sold ~$2B of J&J — not a negative view, just to keep the cash buffer up while funding Wrigley, Goldman and GE. See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**M28 · We run Berkshire as if we owned all of it.** *Buffett:* asked what they'd do differently if they owned the whole place, most CEOs give a list; at Berkshire "there is no list." Sellers know they've made one decision, won't be leveraged up or spun off by some consultant, and get to keep running what they built. *Munger:* "Berkshire Hathaway's system has legs."

**M30 · No quarrel with copycats.** *Buffett:* plenty of people buy the securities Berkshire buys — fine, though they lack the $58B of interest-free float (and have some tax advantages Berkshire doesn't). Buffett himself studied Graham-Newman's semiannual reports at 21 for ideas. *Munger:* "it's quite smart… to identify some investors you regard as very skilled… and copy what you please."

**★ M31 · Your best inflation protection.** *Buffett:* inflation is "certain… over time," paid not by taxpayers but by holders of fixed-dollar investments via lost purchasing power — "the easiest thing to do… the most likely thing to have happen." Defenses, in order: "your own earning power is the best, by far" (the best surgeon or teacher commands their share of output "whether it's seashells, or reichsmarks, or dollars"), then "a wonderful business that does not require heavy capital investment." Capital-hungry businesses "get killed in inflation." *Munger:* "become a brain surgeon and invest in Coca-Cola instead of government bonds." See [bonds-and-inflation](../concepts/bonds-and-inflation.md), [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md).

**★ M32 · Newspapers are fading — but we're keeping the Buffalo News.** *Buffett:* the decline is evolutionary, not merely cyclical — papers have lost the "essentiality" (Walter Annenberg's word) that once made them monopoly must-haves for readers and advertisers alike; "for most newspapers in the United States, we would not buy them at any price," with the possibility of "unending losses." Yet Berkshire keeps the Buffalo News per its economic principles (no unending losses, no major union problems), even though "it would be a mistake if you were acting as a trustee." *Munger:* their disappearance "is really a national tragedy" — they "kept government more honest." *Supersession flag:* Berkshire sold its newspaper group (BH Media) to Lee Enterprises in 2020, reversing the keep-forever stance as the losses became structural. See [media-economics](../concepts/media-economics.md), [buffalo-evening-news](../entities/buffalo-evening-news.md), [washington-post](../entities/washington-post.md).

**M33 · Expect weak consumer spending for "quite a bit longer."** *Buffett:* the behavioral shift toward saving "could last quite a long time"; retail (especially high-end) and retail real estate will be tough — shopping centers sold at "premiere cap rates of 5 percent… going to look very silly." Service businesses are generally better — "they require less capital." Construction-linked businesses (carpet, brick, insulation, paint) recover only as housing reaches equilibrium.

**★ M34 · We'll never buy back Berkshire at a "silly" price.** *Buffett:* his old pro-repurchase advocacy dates to the 1970s–80s when stocks were cheap relative to intrinsic value; "probably 90 percent of the repurchase activity I've seen in the last five years" was foolish overpaying, a disguised buy recommendation. Berkshire would repurchase only if demonstrably below conservatively-estimated intrinsic value — but the required disclosure would be self-defeating. "We may make a mistake by not buying it at a cheap price. But we'll never make a mistake… by buying it at a silly price." See [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md).

**★ M35 · Opportunity cost got much harder in the crisis.** *Buffett:* when prices and intrinsic values both move fast, "calibrating A versus B, versus C" is tougher — and more profitable. In September–October 2008, with commitments outstanding (Wrigley $6.5B, Constellation $5B, Dow $3B) and the Goldman call arriving that "couldn't have been done the previous Wednesday," he sold J&J with a floor price to guarantee the cash buffer: "We want to have billions and billions and billions around. And then we'll think about what we do with the surplus." Selling billions is harder than buying billions in chaos. See [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md).

**M36 · GEICO advertising: we'll "never stop."** *Buffett:* Berkshire will spend ~$800M on GEICO advertising — more than State Farm or Allstate despite being smaller — because "a brand is a promise," and the message that you can save real money must be lodged in every American mind for when they finally become price-sensitive. "If I thought we could get anything like the same return by spending 2 billion next year, we'd spend 2 billion." *Munger:* the key insight — if GEICO would stay the same size without the spend, and new subscribers are worth more than the $800M, then GEICO "is earning $800 million more pre-tax in a way that doesn't even show." See [direct-distribution-moat](../concepts/direct-distribution-moat.md), [geico](../entities/geico.md).

**★ M37 · Wells is better than many banks — and my Irish-bank mistake.** *Buffett:* separate banks by business model, not price — "It's like comparing a copper producer whose costs are $2.50 a pound with a copper producer whose costs are $1 a pound." The figures are in the 10-Ks (e.g. Wells's $600M non-cash core-deposit amortization charge), but "people don't seem to look at them very carefully." On the Irish banks: "I couldn't have been more wrong… I just plain wasn't paying attention" to their land-development loan exposure. *Munger:* GAAP "allow[s] a conservative, sensible bank to show vastly increased earnings if it changes its practices to make a lot of extremely dumb loans" — that seduces bankers into ghastly decisions. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [lending-discipline](../concepts/lending-discipline.md), [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md).

## Q&A digest — Afternoon session

**A2 · Preliminary Q1 2009 figures.** *Buffett:* operating earnings ~$1.7B after-tax (vs $1.9B); insurance and utilities relatively recession-proof, most other businesses down. Float rose ~$2B on a Swiss Re adverse-loss-development cover (very long-duration). Book value down ~6% (markets plus worsening CDS marks). Cash equivalents ~$22.7B, less ~$3B spent next day on Dow preferred — "We always keep a significant amount of cash at the parent company… so that whatever comes along, we're prepared for." See [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md), [float](../concepts/float.md).

**★ A3 · "Value" investing — what other kind is there?** *Buffett:* asked whether BYD was a value or speculative bet — "there is no other kind of investment than a value investment… Value relates to getting a lot for the expectable flow of cash in the future." The value/growth dichotomy is a category error. See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md).

**A4 · Munger: BYD is a "damn miracle."** *Munger:* BYD went from zero to a top lithium-battery and cell-phone-component maker and China's best-selling car model under Wang Chuanfu, with 17,000 engineers "selected from a billion, 300 million people." Batteries are "totally needed in the future of the world… we have to use the direct power of the sun." Getting more optimistic near "the age of death," he foresees harnessing solar as "the main technical problem of mankind… about to be fixed" (A21). *Buffett:* "BYD was Charlie's last year. The Irish banks were mine." (No BYD page yet; largely vindicated by events.)

**A5 · The dollar will buy less.** *Buffett:* relative exchange rates are unpredictable — everyone is running large deficits — but in purchasing-power terms "units of currency [will] buy a lot less over time. You can bet on inflation." A euro/dollar hedge is possible for a worried foreign holder, but Berkshire doesn't normally do it. *Munger:* "a little inflation is not going to ruin the lives of any of us. The trick is to avoid the runaway inflation."

**A6 · Buffett "irritated" by the loss of the triple-A.** *Buffett:* the Moody's downgrade "doesn't have any material effect on borrowing costs" (double-A vs triple-A spreads are tiny) but "it still irritates me." A rating box can't capture management's attitude toward creditors, which at Berkshire is "sacred… a lot more important than increasing earnings per share" — witness 50-year workers'-comp obligations. *Munger:* "the next change at Moody's will be in the opposite direction… because we deserve a higher rating and they're smart." *Supersession flag:* Berkshire never regained the Moody's triple-A, and S&P also downgraded it (2010) — Munger's predicted upgrade did not materialize.

**A7 · "Crazy" prices for Berkshire's own credit-default swaps.** *Buffett:* a second-order effect he didn't anticipate — because equity-put counterparties book a rising receivable from Berkshire (no collateral posted, due in 15 years), their credit departments buy CDS protection on Berkshire, and the more profit they show the more protection they must buy. At one point that drove Berkshire's CDS to ~5% a year. "It doesn't cost us anything. But it does result in kind of a crazy market." See [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md).

**A8 · MidAmerican, Iowa and wind.** *Buffett:* Berkshire is the largest US utility owner of wind capacity; Iowa is a net electricity exporter with rates unchanged for over a decade, achieved through efficiencies and wind. A built-in fair return plus a 1.8-cent/kWh production tax credit — and Berkshire is a big taxpayer, so it can use the credits directly. *Munger:* "in practically anything that makes sense in utilities, the Berkshire subsidiaries will be leaders." See [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md).

**★ A9 · Speed as a durable competitive advantage.** *Buffett:* Dave Sokol and Greg Abel handed Constellation Energy a firm, all-cash bid in Baltimore the same evening its troubles surfaced, within a day or two of a likely downgrade that would have forced it toward bankruptcy — "that's one of the advantages of Berkshire… a durable competitive advantage." Same with the Dynegy pipeline bought "in about two hours" in 2002 ("We don't ask the lawyers before we do it"). The prerequisites: "we've always got the money" and "the managers that can deliver on the properties." See [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md), [dave-sokol](../people/dave-sokol.md), [greg-abel](../people/greg-abel.md).

**★ A13–A14 · Post-mortems in private; "I was dead wrong" on Gen Re.** *Buffett:* Berkshire strongly believes in acquisition post-mortems ("it's easy to propose a deal… much harder to account for it later on") but keeps them private so as not to embarrass managers cleaning up the acquirer's mistake. On Gen Re: "I was dead wrong, in 1998… in thinking that it was the Gen Re of 15 years earlier"; reserving and underwriting had slipped. Tad Montross and Joe Brandon fixed it — "Gen Re is the company now that I thought it was." "Most of [the dumb decisions] have been mine." *Munger:* "you have to be very tough minded to fix General Re… if capitalism has any heroes in that transaction, why, Joe's the hero." *Supersession flag:* Joe Brandon had resigned from Gen Re in April 2008 amid the AIG–Gen Re finite-reinsurance prosecution (convictions later vacated on appeal) — the "hero" praise sits against a departure the meeting doesn't mention. See [general-re](../entities/general-re.md), [joe-brandon](../people/joe-brandon.md).

**★ A15 · "We are not big believers in contracts."** *Buffett:* Berkshire hands sellers hundreds of millions or billions and the only question that matters is whether they'll keep the same passion after the sale — "if we're wrong on that, no contract is going to save us." *Munger:* "Our model is a seamless web of trust that's deserved on both sides. The Hollywood model, where everyone has a contract, and no trust is deserved on either side, is not what we want at all." See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md), [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

**A16 · How to get thrown out of Buffett's office: propose a spinoff.** *Buffett:* Berkshire won't spin off subsidiaries to capture a higher multiple — "we can't wait to throw them out of the office." A wonderful business like See's throws off capital that Berkshire redeploys tax-efficiently within a consolidated return, an advantage a shareholder can't replicate; plus the buy-to-keep promise. *Munger:* Wall Street sells spinoffs "so they can get fees… It isn't really doing that much for anybody." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**A17 · The "too hard" pile — student loans.** *Buffett:* "I don't know that much about it." He was approached on the Sallie Mae deal that fell through, told the caller he didn't understand it well — "it turned out to be a good thing I didn't." A clean small illustration of the "too tough" pile. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ A18–A19 · Goldman and GE: attractive terms, not just attractive businesses.** *Buffett:* the crisis deals were "the terms, primarily" — chaotic markets where "very few were either willing, or… able to commit major sums on short notice," so "we were the low bid, in effect" and got a very good coupon. He likes the businesses and managers too, but "I'm not sure there was any second possibility." On earnings management: "a very substantial percentage of American industry over the last 15 years… has managed earnings." *Munger:* despite criticism of investment banking, "Berkshire itself has had marvelous services from all of its investment bankers." See [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md), [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md).

**★ A20 · Optimism on America's standard of living.** *Buffett:* "over time, people will live better and better in this country. We have a system that works. It unleashes human potential." The 19th century had a civil war and six panics, the 20th two world wars and the Depression, yet "a 7-for-1 improvement in living" — progress comes "in fits and starts." Human potential was long wasted (slavery, denying women the vote) and still is. "The opportunities will win in the end." See [american-tailwind](../concepts/american-tailwind.md).

**A22 · Swiss Re — comfortable without control.** *Buffett:* Berkshire has a 20% quota share of Swiss Re's P&C, ~3% of the common, a 12% coupon security (callable after two years at 120%, then convertible), and the adverse-loss cover — senior to Swiss Re's ~20B francs of equity, "I would not regard it as a junior security." Swiss Re's problems came from financial products and the asset side, "not… through their insurance underwriting," which "has been fine over the years." Reinsurance develops "a large amount of float per dollar of premium." *Munger:* "that's a terrible problem. We wish we had more of it."

**★ A23 · Irrational CEO compensation and the captured board.** *Buffett:* boards have "relatively little effect" on pay because the CEO shapes the comp committee — "people are not looking for Dobermans. They're looking for Cocker Spaniels… wagging their tails." Capital-intensive subsidiaries need a capital-cost element (Berkshire has "dozens and dozens" of tailored plans; Pete Kiewit figured out a rational one without consultants). 100-page proxy statements are themselves a red flag. "The shareholders have the honor and the executives have the system." *Munger:* "the corporations of America would be managed better if the directors weren't paid at all" — liberal director pay breeds reciprocation, and a director with much to lose can't be independent. See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md), [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

**★ A24 · Worst-case scenario for the insurance business.** *Buffett:* a mega-catastrophe would cost Berkshire ~3–4% of the industry loss (down from the old 4–5%) — perhaps $3–4B on a $100B event, survivable. The true worst case is political: runaway inflation making auto-insurance and utility bills so hated that the public demands nationalization — "that would be a huge asset that would disappear." *Munger:* "the very worst case is some catastrophe where we lose quite a few billions… Even that, I don't think, significantly impairs the basic business." See [super-cat-insurance](../concepts/super-cat-insurance.md).

**A25 · No preset goal for international earnings.** *Buffett:* Berkshire pursues opportunity wherever it appears ("we don't know whether the phone call will come from Israel or from Indiana") and already has large look-through foreign earnings via Coca-Cola (>80% abroad), P&G, Kraft and ISCAR — no target percentage. *Munger:* "I am an agnostic about these things. There's plenty wrong and plenty right on both sides of the Atlantic." See [look-through-earnings](../concepts/look-through-earnings.md).

**A26 · "Nobody gets any joy" from layoffs.** *Buffett:* capitalism "is creative destruction" — GEICO is adding ~1,000 jobs while brick, Shaw, Johns Manville, Benjamin Moore and Acme Brick cut during the housing collapse, and the textile business ended in everyone being laid off. Some contractions are permanent (Buffalo News), some cyclical. *Munger:* the strongest businesses can use a "shared-hardship model" (ISCAR) instead of layoffs — "It's hard for an empty sack to stand upright" (Franklin); "in a world where you sometimes have to amputate a limb to stay alive, you can't expect that every business can stay exactly as it is."

**A27 · Fight egregious pay with embarrassment, not legislation.** *Buffett:* the Clinton-era $1M pay-deductibility cap was "the most counterproductive piece of legislation that Congress has ever come up with" — shareholders paid the tax, and the workarounds ratcheted pay up. The real remedy: three or four of the largest investors publicly calling out the egregious cases, because "the way you get big shots to change their behavior is to embarrass them." *Munger:* skeptical even of that — big investor groups contain "many an investment manager making $20 million a year for insignificant contributions… a man in a glass house," and public pension funds have their own agendas; "sometimes, the cure is worse than the disease." See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md), [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

**★ A28 · We don't hire potentially great managers — we buy proven ones.** *Buffett:* managers usually come with the business, already "knocking the ball out of the park"; the real question is whether they'll keep the same passion after the sale. The hardest part, with no retirement age, is recognizing when a manager loses ability with age — "we've been slow every time," waiting an extra year or more on a beloved Wesco manager with Alzheimer's. You can't install passion, "but we do recognize it when it's there" (Tony Nicely handing over the policyholder count). *Munger:* "If we really love the guy, we're really slow." See [buffett-management-criteria](../synthesis/buffett-management-criteria.md).

**★ A29 · Don't try to time the market.** *Buffett:* the October 2008 op-ed "did not predict what stocks were going to do… But I do know when you're starting to get a lot for your money." The hamburger test: cheaper is better for a lifelong net buyer, and "the fact that I paid X yesterday doesn't bother me." "Picking bottoms is basically not our game. Pricing is our game… our idea is not to tiptoe into anything." *Munger:* 1973–74 was "my time and my only time… Unfortunately, I had practically no money available" — which "is why those times occur"; don't wait for a repeat. He also flags the corporate-bond window that briefly offered "perfectly safe bonds that yielded 9 percent or more." See [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md), [investment-categories](../concepts/investment-categories.md).

**★ A30 · "Decent" utility returns — and moats filling with sand.** *Buffett:* regulated utilities earn a jurisdiction-allowed return on equity — "there's no way we get rich… but there's no way we get poor, either." The businesses that scare him are capital-intensive ones outside utilities, "where you just pump in more money" without a guaranteed range of return. The best businesses need little capital yet earn well, protected by "some moat… other than the capital required as entry" — and those do best in inflation. *Munger:* "a lot of moats have been filling up with sand lately… the daily newspaper, the network television station, all these castles with their lovely moats. The moats are filling up." See [moat](../concepts/moat.md), [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md), [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md).
