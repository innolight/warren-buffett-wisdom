# Berkshire Hathaway 2015 Annual Letter

The 2015 letter is not a landmark year in the series — it does not introduce a new framework or represent a structural turning point. Its value lies in making existing frameworks more vivid: the direct-distribution moat receives its clearest articulation across any letter; the GAAP distortion in float accounting is deepened; and the BNSF social compact, after a year of documented service failures, is vindicated by record results. The letter also marks the arrival of Precision Castparts ([[precision-castparts]]) — Berkshire's largest-ever cash acquisition — as the would-be "Powerhouse Six."

## Key themes

### 1. The direct-distribution moat: why incumbents structurally cannot respond

The most valuable section of the 2015 letter is the "Productivity and Prosperity" essay, which contains the clearest statement in the series of why GEICO's cost advantage is not merely a competitive edge but a structural trap for incumbents.

Buffett traces a century of auto insurance distribution through three stages: independent agency cartel (~40¢ per premium dollar in expenses), captive agency (State Farm, Allstate; ~25%), and direct-to-consumer (GEICO; 14.7% by 2015). Each disruption stripped a distribution cost layer and returned part of the savings to customers. At each stage, incumbents understood exactly what had happened — and could not respond, because their agent network was not just a cost to be eliminated but the source of their customer relationships, their renewal base, and their organizational identity.

The insight is that the structural moat here is not GEICO's efficiency — it is the incumbents' dependency. The agent network is the barrier on both sides. This is the foundation for the concept of [[direct-distribution-moat]].

The compounding dynamic that follows deserves equal emphasis. GEICO's cost advantage enables advertising investment that would be economically irrational for an agent-based competitor to match. Lower costs fund lower prices, which fund share growth, which funds scale, which further reduces per-unit costs — a loop that ran for decades without incumbents finding an answer.

### 2. Float: the GAAP distortion has two sides

The 2015 letter deepens the argument that Berkshire's insurance economics are systematically misrepresented in book value — not just one direction, but two.

**The liability is overstated.** Float is booked in full as a GAAP liability, as if it would be paid out tomorrow and could not be replenished. But float is a revolving fund: claims paid are continuously replaced by new premiums. Owing a dollar that will never leave the premises because new business perpetually supplies a substitute is not the same as owing a dollar that goes out tomorrow. The difference is not captured in GAAP.

**The offsetting asset is also wrong.** A $15.5B insurance goodwill asset sits on Berkshire's books — the capitalized cost of acquiring the float-generating franchise. That goodwill figure was already on the books in 2000. Since then, float has roughly tripled. The economic value of the franchise that generates the float has grown enormously; the accounting asset has not moved. Both distortions push the same direction: intrinsic value substantially exceeds book value, and the gap is structural and widening.

After thirteen consecutive years of underwriting profit, with cumulative underwriting gains exceeding $26 billion over that period, Buffett's verdict: "Without a doubt, Berkshire's largest unrecorded wealth lies in its insurance business. We've spent 48 years building this multi-faceted operation, and it can't be replicated." See [[float]] and [[intrinsic-value-vs-book-value]].

### 3. Capital flexibility: both/and as structural advantage

Most capital allocators face an either/or constraint: they can buy whole businesses at negotiated control premiums, or they can buy minority stakes at public-market prices. Berkshire can do both, and the 2015 letter frames this explicitly as a structural competitive advantage rather than a stylistic preference.

The implication is an expanded opportunity set. When attractive businesses exist only as listed securities without a control event on offer, Berkshire buys the minority stake and captures look-through earnings at public-market prices. When private businesses come available at negotiated prices, Berkshire can compete with the flexibility of a strategic buyer. The "Big Four" equity stakes — American Express, Coca-Cola, IBM, Wells Fargo — represent the minority pathway; BNSF, GEICO, and now Precision Castparts represent the control pathway. Both are running simultaneously, each drawing from the same capital pool.

Buffett's framing: "our appetite for either operating businesses or passive investments doubles our chances of finding sensible uses for Berkshire's endless gusher of cash." See [[capital-allocation]] and [[passive-ownership-advantage]].

### 4. Intrinsic vs. book value: the controlled-company accounting asymmetry

The 2015 letter opens with the clearest explanation yet of *why* Berkshire's book value progressively understates intrinsic value — and why this gap will structurally widen over time, not converge.

In Berkshire's early years, most assets were marketable securities marked to market. Book value and intrinsic value tracked each other closely. As Berkshire shifted to outright ownership of operating businesses, the accounting rules changed: losers are written down; winners are never written up. The carrying value of a business that has compounded in value for thirty years reflects acquisition cost, not current economic value. Every durable franchise Berkshire has owned and improved — GEICO, See's, BNSF — is carried at a fraction of what a buyer would rationally pay today.

This is not a flaw in GAAP accounting (which is correct for its purpose of avoiding unverified write-ups); it is a predictable consequence of applying accounting rules designed for financial assets to long-duration operating businesses. The investor who reads Berkshire's book value as the relevant measure of wealth is reading the wrong number. See [[intrinsic-value-vs-book-value]].

### 5. The social compact confirmed: BNSF after adversity

The 2014 letter documented BNSF's service failures and Berkshire's commitment to spend roughly $5.8 billion on plant and equipment in 2015 — at the time, more capital than any American railroad had spent in a single year. The 2015 letter reports the result: service "dramatically improved," record pre-tax earnings, and a strengthened market position.

The confirmation matters because the social compact is a thesis, not just a philosophy. The thesis is that regulated essential infrastructure operators who make genuine long-term commitments to service quality and capital investment earn both regulatory goodwill and customer loyalty that protects their franchise through downturns. When BNSF underperformed in 2014, the response was not defensive repositioning or retrenchment — it was the largest capital commitment in railroad history, executed while the earnings were disappointing. The 2015 results validate the thesis: massive capital investment in service capacity, rather than managing to near-term earnings, is the correct response for an essential-infrastructure franchise. See [[bnsf]].

### 6. Underwriting discipline as corporate culture

Buffett uses the 2015 letter to articulate what thirteen consecutive years of underwriting profit means institutionally, not just financially. The four disciplines of sound insurance — understand all exposures, assess likelihood conservatively, set profitable premiums, walk away if the premium cannot be obtained — are not rules. They are cultural commitments that most insurers claim and few actually maintain when competitive pressure to write volume rises.

> "Many insurers pass the first three tests and flunk the fourth. That old line, 'The other guy is doing it, so we must as well,' spells trouble in any business, but in none more so than insurance. All insurers give that message lip service. At Berkshire it is a religion, Old Testament style."

The sustained streak is evidence that culture — not process, not rules, not incentive design alone — is the operative variable. General Re failed this test in the years after acquisition despite possessing strong underwriting talent; the failure was cultural, not technical. GEICO's success is partly cost structure and partly Tony Nicely's eighteen-year reinforcement of the same discipline. See [[underwriting-discipline]] and [[general-re]].
