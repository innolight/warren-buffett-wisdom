# Berkshire Hathaway 1984 Shareholder Letter

Net worth gain $152.6M, +13.6% — "mediocre" by 20-year-compound (22.1%) standards. Buffett's "academic voice" giving way to "reportorial voice" on the drag of a growing capital base. Four landmark essays: **repurchases at sub-intrinsic value** as canonical capital allocation; the **dividend-policy framework** (restricted vs. unrestricted earnings; $1-of-market-value per $1-retained test); **bonds-as-a-business** (the WPPSS section, businesslike bond valuation); and the **rented-suit / loss-reserving** essay. Concentration policy stated explicitly. Capital Cities / ABC deal announced as a subsequent event.

## Key themes

- **Repurchases at sub-intrinsic value — canonical capital allocation.** Two benefits: (1) Arithmetic — repurchases below intrinsic value enlarge per-share value immediately ("they often find it easy to get $2 of present value for $1"). (2) Signaling — "by making repurchases when a company's market value is well below its business value, management clearly demonstrates that it is given to actions that enhance the wealth of shareholders." The market correctly assigns a higher multiple to assets in pro-shareholder hands. Buffett's three largest holdings (GEICO, Washington Post, General Foods) all engaged in significant repurchases at favorable prices. Greenmail explicitly distinguished from genuine repurchases. See [[share-repurchases]].
- **Dividend policy framework.** Distinguish *restricted* earnings (those that must be retained to maintain unit volume and competitive position in capital-intensive businesses) from *unrestricted* earnings (those that can be retained or distributed). The core test for retention: "for every dollar retained by the corporation, at least one dollar of market value will be created for owners." The "10% perpetual bond" analogy explains the math. Internal subsidiary-to-parent retention decisions are usually rigorous; parent-to-shareholder decisions usually aren't. "If earnings have been unwisely retained, it is likely that managers, too, have been unwisely retained." See [[dividend-policy]].
- **Bonds as a business — WPPSS.** Berkshire bought $139M (cost) of Washington Public Power Supply System Projects 1, 2, 3 bonds (Oct '83–Jun '84), contractually earning $22.7M after tax — 16.3% on unleveraged capital. "We extend this business-valuation approach even to bond purchases." A bond yielding 16% is a "business" earning 16% on book; what would you pay for that vs. an operating business with comparable economics? The 1946 illustration: AAA tax-exempts at <1% — "a business that earned about 1% on 'book value' (and that, moreover, could never earn a dime more than 1% on book)." Ben Graham invoked: "Investment is most intelligent when it is most businesslike." Bonds have a hard ceiling (face value) and a small but nonzero zero-risk; Buffett requires bond purchases to "clear a special hurdle." See [[bonds-as-a-business]].
- **Concentration policy — explicit.** "We remain unconventional in the degree to which we concentrate the investments of our insurance companies, including those in WPPSS bonds. This concentration makes sense only because our insurance business is conducted from a position of exceptional financial strength." Billy Rose: "If you have a harem of forty women, you never get to know any of them very well." See [[passive-ownership-advantage]].
- **Loss-reserving uncertainty — the rented suit.** "The financial statements of a property/casualty insurer provide, at best, only a first rough draft of earnings and financial condition." Four loss components: paid, reported-but-unsettled, IBNR (incurred but not reported), and revisions of prior estimates. Five-year table of Berkshire's own under-reserving: 1980 reported $6.7M underwriting profit → corrected $14.9M profit (positive surprise); but 1981–83 progressively under-reserved by tens of millions. Industry pathology: "insolvent insurers don't run out of cash until long after they have run out of net worth"; the "walking dead" then write at any price. "The corpse is supposed to file the death certificate."
- **Dominant-newspaper economics decoupled from quality.** "Once dominant, the newspaper itself, not the marketplace, determines just how good or how bad the paper will be. Good or bad, it will prosper." Even a poor newspaper retains essential "bulletin board" value to most readers; ad revenue follows household attention. The News' 50.9% news-hole ratio is a management choice imposed against market indifference.
- **GEICO market value flat while intrinsic value rises.** "We would far rather have the business value of GEICO increase by X during the year, while market value decreases, than have the intrinsic value increase by only ½X with market value soaring." Cost advantage in GEICO's core business is described as "rare … and it's almost non-existent in the field of financial services."
- **Inflation risk and runaway risk.** Buffett: "an all-bond portfolio carries a small but unacceptable 'wipe out' risk" from potential runaway inflation. The fiscal-deficit framing is direct.
- **Cap Cities / ABC subsequent event.** March 18, 1985: 3M shares of Cap Cities at $172.50 each, contingent on the ABC acquisition closing — "very late in 1985 at the earliest."

## Notable quotes

> When companies with outstanding businesses and comfortable financial positions find their shares selling far below intrinsic value in the marketplace, no alternative action can benefit shareholders as surely as repurchases.

> A manager who consistently turns his back on repurchases, when these clearly are in the interests of owners, reveals more than he knows of his motivations.

> Unrestricted earnings should be retained only when there is a reasonable prospect — backed preferably by historical evidence or, when appropriate, by a thoughtful analysis of the future — that for every dollar retained by the corporation, at least one dollar of market value will be created for owners.

> If earnings have been unwisely retained, it is likely that managers, too, have been unwisely retained.

> We extend this business-valuation approach even to bond purchases such as WPPSS.

> Investment is most intelligent when it is most businesslike. — Ben Graham (quoted)

> Most managers have very little incentive to make the intelligent-but-with-some-chance-of-looking-like-an-idiot decision… Failing conventionally is the route to go; as a group, lemmings may have a rotten image, but no individual lemming has ever received bad press.

> If you have a harem of forty women, you never get to know any of them very well. — Billy Rose (quoted)

> In insurance, you can be broke but flush.
