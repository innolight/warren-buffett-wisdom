# Berkshire Hathaway 2013 Annual Letter

Letter dated February 28, 2014. Book value gained $34.2 billion (+18.2% per share), though Berkshire underperformed the S&P 500 — which posted a strong year — consistent with Buffett's long-standing expectation that Berkshire falls short in years when the market is up strongly. Two major acquisitions closed: NV Energy (acquired by MidAmerican for $5.6B) and H.J. Heinz (with 3G Capital; $8B preferred + $4.25B common). The Powerhouse Five — BNSF, MidAmerican, Iscar, Lubrizol, and Marmon — earned a combined record $10.8B pre-tax. Insurance logged its 11th consecutive underwriting profit; float reached $77.2B. The letter's most durable section is an extended investment-philosophy essay built around two personal real estate investments (a Nebraska farm in 1986, an NYU-adjacent property in 1993), culminating in a clear prescription for non-professional investors and Buffett's will directive: 90% S&P 500 index fund, 10% short-term government bonds.

## Key themes

- **11 consecutive underwriting-profit years** totaling $22B pre-tax; float at $77.2B — the revolving-fund framing most explicitly developed here
- **GEICO passes Allstate** to become the #2 U.S. auto insurer; economic goodwill "approaching $20 billion"
- **BNSF** records $22B revenues, $3.79B net earnings, and $4B in capex — more than any railroad in a single year; carried 15% of all U.S. inter-city freight by ton-miles, position strengthened in 2013
- **MidAmerican** acquires NV Energy for $5.6B; now accounts for 7% of U.S. wind generation capacity; "retained more earnings than any other American electric utility"
- **General Re** declared "now a gem" under Tad Montross; the full rehabilitation arc complete
- **Farm and real estate analogies** as the most vivid illustration of productive-asset investing: focus on future productivity not price; ignore macro entirely; liquidity is a benefit that investors often invert into a curse
- **90/10 will directive** — Buffett's own estate instruction: 10% short-term government bonds, 90% low-cost S&P 500 index fund (suggested Vanguard)
- **EBITDA critique** at its sharpest: "When Wall Streeters tout EBITDA as a valuation guide, button your wallet"
- **Ben Graham tribute** — personal account of buying *The Intelligent Investor* in 1949; chapters 8 and 20 as the enduring guide; the GEICO connection through Graham
- **Energy Future Holdings** — $873M pre-tax bond loss confessed; "Next time I'll call Charlie"
- **Berkshire Hathaway Specialty Insurance (BHSI)** — new commercial insurance operation launched June 2013, led by Peter Eastwood; early acceptance by major brokers and Fortune 500 risk managers
- **Public pension risk** — "gigantic financial tapeworm that was born when promises were made that conflicted with a willingness to fund them"; references a 1975 Buffett memo to Katharine Graham reproduced in the letter's appendix (memo not yet separately ingested)

## Notable quotes

> Simply put, insurance is the sale of promises. The "customer" pays money now; the insurer promises to pay money in the future if certain events occur.

> Daily, we pay old claims — some $17 billion to more than five million claimants in 2013 — and that reduces float. Just as surely, we each day write new business and thereby generate new claims that add to float. If our revolving float is both costless and long-enduring, which I believe it will be, the true value of this liability is dramatically less than the accounting liability.

> Games are won by players who focus on the playing field — not by those whose eyes are glued to the scoreboard. If you can enjoy Saturdays and Sundays without looking at stock prices, give it a try on weekdays.

> A "flash crash" or some other extreme market fluctuation can't hurt an investor any more than an erratic and mouthy neighbor can hurt my farm investment. Indeed, tumbling markets can be helpful to the true investor if he has cash available when prices get far out of line with values. A climate of fear is your friend when investing; a euphoric world is your enemy.

> Forming macro opinions or listening to the macro or market predictions of others is a waste of time. Indeed, it is dangerous because it may blur your vision of the facts that are truly important.

> When Wall Streeters tout EBITDA as a valuation guide, button your wallet.

> Investment is most intelligent when it is most businesslike. — Ben Graham, *The Intelligent Investor*

> It can be remembered that soon after we purchased General Re, the company was beset by problems that caused commentators — and me as well, briefly — to believe I had made a huge mistake. That day is long gone. General Re is now a gem.
