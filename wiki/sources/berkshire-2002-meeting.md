# Berkshire Hathaway 2002 Annual Meeting

The 2002 meeting is the pivotal post-9/11, post-bubble annual gathering — and its single most consequential moment is Munger's live announcement that Berkshire is winding down the General Re derivatives book: "derivative accounting in America is a sewer is an insult to sewage." The year's hard insurance market, the reopening of terrorism aggregation as a category, and the explicit reversal of the derivatives position together make this the meeting that seeds the 2002 letter's "financial weapons of mass destruction." Around that center, Buffett and Munger deliver what may be their sharpest two-sentence definition of investing vs. speculation; an unusually pointed critique of EBITDA as a fraud indicator; the Larson-Juhl deal as a case study in acquisition discipline; a demolition of Black-Scholes for long-life options; and Munger's verdict on stock options for already-wealthy senior executives: "demented" and "immoral." Per the wiki's two-layer convention, a short distilled top of the durable themes precedes a **comprehensive Q&A digest** that captures every value-bearing exchange.

## How to read the Q&A digest

- **★** marks a timeless, load-bearing exchange worth citing in the future; unmarked entries still carry investor value but are secondary or period-specific.
- Answers are attributed separately to **Buffett** and **Munger** — a Munger line appears only when he added a distinct lesson (he answered "Nothing to add" to most). This keeps the wisdom fairly credited to both.
- **M#/A#** map to the numbered items in the raw morning/afternoon transcript for traceability.

## Key themes

### The decisive cost: getting out of derivatives is hell

The morning's most important exchange (M30) is Munger pivoting a question about feedback mechanisms into a live announcement: Berkshire is running off the General Re Financial Products book, sustaining an $88M Q1 loss plus severance to do it. The reason is structural — not any single trade gone wrong, but a system where "you give a whole bunch of traders the ability to create income by putting little numbers down on a piece of paper that nobody can really check, and it will get out of control." Every contract that pays upfront and runs 15–40 years is a bet on the preparer's continued honesty and ability. The feedback mechanism Berkshire chose was to reverse the decision even when it was "very unpleasant" — because nobody else was doing it, and Munger recognized: "It's a little like hell. It's easy to get into, and it's hard — very hard — to get out of." This completes the arc from the 2001 meeting's "potential dynamite" (A14) and directly seeds the 2002 letter. See [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md).

### Insurance post-9/11: aggregation is the risk, cost is the discipline

9/11 forced the insurance world to price what it had always implicitly excluded: man-made catastrophes that aggregate. Berkshire's framework for terrorism insurance — write non-NCB (nuclear, chemical, biological) cover where damage cannot aggregate beyond defined limits, avoid NCB where a few coordinated acts could destroy the industry — is the applied version of the rule stated in 1994 and 1995: price to exposure, not experience, and refuse unpriced aggregation. Crucially, Buffett re-emphasizes the cost/size hierarchy: Q1 float grew $1.8B with a small underwriting profit, but "the most important thing is the cost of the float and not the size of the float." A terrible float cost in 2001 (12.8%) made $37B of float useless; free float at any size is transformative. See [float](../concepts/float.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md), [super-cat-insurance](../concepts/super-cat-insurance.md).

### Investing vs. speculation: the asset's cash vs. what someone will pay

The 2002 meeting contains Buffett's cleanest single-exchange statement of the distinction: "All investment is laying out some money now to get more money back in the future. One is from what the asset itself will produce. That's investment. One is from what somebody else will pay you for it later on, irrespective of what the asset produces — I call that speculation." (A12). The bubble transferred enormous wealth via speculation; the victims learned the difference only after the fact. The corollary is that you never need a price quotation — See's Candy is worth the present value of its future cash, and no broker needs to be consulted. See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md).

### EBITDA and creative accounting: fraud's preferred language

Asked how to detect fraud, Buffett delivers a deceptively simple filter: "If we take all the people in the world that talk about EBITDA and all the people in the world who haven't talked about EBITDA, there are more frauds in the first group, percentage-wise, by a substantial margin. Very substantial." Depreciation is "the worst kind of expense" because the cash goes out first; EBITDA ignores it, ignores interest, and ignores taxes — three things that "are real." Munger goes further: "Creative accounting is an absolute curse to a civilization," invoking double-entry bookkeeping (a north Italian monk's invention) as one of humanity's great tools, now being perverted. Enron's collapse may produce a corrective; Arthur Andersen's failure is capitalism's error-correction mechanism at work, unfair to the innocent lower ranks but necessary. See [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md), [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md).

### Acquisition discipline: fat pitches and the no-brainer as the standard

The Larson-Juhl story (M21) is the 2002 meeting's anchor on acquisitions: Craig Ponzio calls on a Monday, explains the custom picture-frame distribution business in 20 minutes, and Buffett shakes hands by 10:30 Wednesday morning without visiting headquarters. Compare that with a company Buffett had just reviewed: 10 acquisitions in five years, all preceded by full due diligence and investment-banker books, 10/10 came in at one-quarter of projected earnings. Munger's explanation: "We wait for the no-brainers. We're not trying to do the difficult things." The Berkshire reputation — "our checks clear," ownership is permanent, employees aren't abandoned — means the right sellers self-select. The result is an asymmetry: Berkshire's quick handshakes outperform the market's elaborate processes because the sellers who choose Berkshire are not the ones trying to get the last dollar. See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

### Stock options: demented, immoral, and dishonestly valued

Munger delivers the 2002 meeting's sharpest governance verdict: giving large stock-option grants to a CEO who already has hundreds of millions in company stock and has given decades of loyal service is "demented … and I also think it's immoral." The analogy is Mayo Clinic surgeons or Cravath partners — by that stage you should be thinking about the right example for the institution, not another hundred million for yourself. The accounting fight is equally clear: options are both an expense (to the company) and a dilution (to shareholders); Black-Scholes, while appropriate for short-term liquid options, is "an insane way" to value a 10-year option on a company you know well, and companies use it precisely because it produces the lowest cost number. The sole carve-out: venture-capital options for genuinely cash-constrained early companies. See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md), [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md), [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

### Circle of competence: doubt is the answer

Asked how a young person should define their circle, Buffett gives the most compact formulation across the meetings: "If you have doubts about something being in your circle of competence, it isn't." Competence is self-announcing — a person who is truly competent "almost automatically has a feeling of where the edge of competence is." The corollary on investment: a small circle is fine ("it's big enough"), and when Ponzio called about picture frames, Buffett knew instantly it was within the circle without having spent a second thinking about it previously. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

## Q&A digest — Morning session

**M14 · ★ When to sell: reassessing competitive advantage.** *Buffett:* today's sales happen when the assessment of long-term competitive advantage changes: "we probably had one view of the long-term competitive advantage of the company at the time we bought it, and we may have modified that." McDonald's and Disney both have fine futures, but "we probably don't think that their competitive advantage is as strong as we might have thought." Newspapers are the canonical example — impregnable in 1970, "not the same" in 2002. The same logic applies to network TV since 1965. This is not a sell signal from price; it is a reassessment of the franchise. *Munger:* "Nothing to add." See [moat](../concepts/moat.md), [franchise-vs-business](../concepts/franchise-vs-business.md).

**M15 · ★ Cost of float, not its size, is what matters.** *Buffett:* Q1 float grew $1.8B — "not many companies in the world had a gain in float even close to that" — while achieving a small underwriting profit, so the cost was essentially zero. But "I think it's suicide, from a business standpoint, to tell a bunch of insurance managers to go out and grow a lot." The hierarchy is explicit: "the important thing is the cost of the float and not the size of the float." Float at 12.8% cost in 2001 "did us no good at all — that float cost us a lot of money"; free float at $37B is transformative. *Munger:* growing float cheaply at great scale is "almost impossible. But we intend to do it anyway." See [float](../concepts/float.md), [cost-of-float](../concepts/cost-of-float.md).

**M16 · Asbestos: a cancer on the corporate world, unlikely to be cured.** *Buffett:* Berkshire's retroactive contracts cap all asbestos exposure, so "it's much more dependent on the speed of payments than the amount of payment." The bigger concern is acquisitions — asbestos litigation "will try to drag in" any deep pocket. Ironically, asbestos also creates buying opportunities (Johns Manville, Fruit of the Loom) — companies cleansed of liability through bankruptcy can become attractive targets. *Munger:* "I would be surprised if there were a constructive solution. I think we'll have more of the mess we have now." See [national-indemnity](../entities/national-indemnity.md), [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**M17 · Index funds: broad, low-cost, and dollar-averaged over time.** *Buffett:* for the ordinary investor — broad S&P 500 index, low costs (recommends John Bogle's books), invested in roughly equal amounts over 20–30 years rather than as a lump sum. "The very nature of index funds is that you are saying, I think America's business is going to do well over a long period of time, but I don't know enough to pick the winners and I don't know enough to pick the winning times." No single metric (P/E, P/B, P/S) reliably times entry. *Munger:* notes that sustained high equity valuations (as in Japan) could make even a broad index return negative in real terms for 13 years — "I think I've lived long enough to think that may happen." See [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md), [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md).

**M18 · ★ 9/11 and aggregation: the underwriter's central discipline.** *Buffett:* pre-9/11, terrorism risk was "thrown in for nothing" — excluded for war (seen firsthand in WWII England) but not for deranged actors. The lesson is aggregation: Berkshire can sell non-NCB terrorism cover freely ("we can have tens of billions … throughout a greater New York area"), but NCB risk aggregates catastrophically — two or three coordinated acts could "destroy the insurance industry." Workers' compensation is the hidden aggregation — the WTC loss was "the largest workers' compensation loss in history by a huge margin" because victims happened to be working. The applicable rule: "you have to be realistic about what you can understand and what you can't understand, and therefore, what you can insure and what you can't insure." *Munger:* "To the extent that September 11th has caused us to be less weak, foolish, and sloppy … it's a plus." See [super-cat-insurance](../concepts/super-cat-insurance.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md), [noah-rule](../concepts/noah-rule.md).

**M19 · ★ Banks: not homogeneous; valuation is about the specific cash.** *Buffett:* Bank of Granite (John Forlines) earned 2% on assets without risk for decades; other banks were "run right into the ground." "There are more banks than bankers" — the industry produces both excellent and disastrous outcomes. Berkshire owns M&T and Wells Fargo because they are "somewhat different than other businesses." No relative P/E ratio unlocks anything: "What you're trying to do is look at all the cash a business will produce between now and judgment day, and discount it back at a rate that's appropriate." *Munger:* "I would argue that Warren and I have failed to properly diagnose banking — we underestimated the good results because we were so afraid of what non-bankers might do." See [lending-discipline](../concepts/lending-discipline.md), [wells-fargo](../entities/wells-fargo.md).

**M20 · ★ EBITDA is a fraud signal; depreciation is the worst expense.** *Buffett:* "The number of times we're going to buy into a company where people are talking about EBITDA is going to be about zero." Among EBITDA users vs. non-users: "There are more frauds in the first group, percentage-wise, by a substantial margin. Very substantial." Depreciation is the reverse of float — you pay the cash first and get the deduction later ("the worst kind of expense"), so excluding it flatters numbers. Companies like Walmart, GE, and Microsoft have apparently never used the term. *Munger:* "Or both" [self-deceived and deceiving others]. "Maxwell of England — his nickname was the 'bouncing Czech.' Three weeks before he went under, Salomon was aggressively seeking more business from him … Imagine extending credit to a guy whose nickname is the 'bouncing Czech.'" See [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md), [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md).

**M21 · ★ Larson-Juhl: the fat pitch and the no-brainer standard.** *Buffett:* Craig Ponzio called on a Monday about a custom picture-frame company; Buffett had never heard of it. In 20 minutes on the phone Ponzio explained the economics (18,000 framers in the country, 85% next-day delivery, service-intensive relationship business), named the price, and named the capital employed. Buffett offered a Wednesday meeting; they shook hands by 10:30 after 90 minutes. No visits to headquarters yet. Contrast: a major company Buffett reviewed had made 10 acquisitions with full due diligence in five years; in aggregate the 10 earned one-quarter of projected 2001 earnings. *Munger:* "We wait for the no-brainers. We're not trying to do the difficult things. … [Corporate managers] are getting — they were buying what was getting sold to them, fulfilling some myths that the management had about itself." The Berkshire reputation (checks clear, people aren't abandoned) means right-fit sellers self-select. "Somebody once defined hell, in a legal system, as a place with endless due process and no justice. And we're getting close. And similarly, in the corporate world, if you have endless due diligence and no horse sense, you've just described a corporate hell, at least for the people who own the business." *Supersession flag:* Berkshire later sold Albecca/Larson-Juhl, making this one of the few Berkshire deals that was exited. See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**M23 · Fruit of the Loom: turnaround only with the right management.** *Buffett:* bought Fruit of the Loom (40–45% of men's/boys' market, "very low-cost producer of a very basic product") through bankruptcy because the financial and operating problems were created by prior management, not by the business. The condition: John Holland must be available to run it. "If we had to take on the management that was there for a few years, we wouldn't have bought it for a dollar." Parallel: GEICO in the mid-1970s — "a marvelous company that got mismanaged in a big way for a while." The lesson: "a classic case on turnarounds" is distinguishing between a bad business and a good business with bad management. See [fruit-of-the-loom](../entities/fruit-of-the-loom.md), [geico](../entities/geico.md), [turnarounds-seldom-turn](../concepts/turnarounds-seldom-turn.md).

**M24 · Book recommendations.** *Munger:* "Ice Age" (history of glaciation, best scientific explanation book he has read; forthcoming in the US); "How the Scots Helped Create the Modern World." *Buffett:* Bob Miles's book on Berkshire managers.

**M25 · What to look for in friends and partners.** *Buffett:* "Look at the qualities you admire in other people. There isn't one that's physically or mentally impossible for you to have." Habits repelling people at 20 become permanent by 60; they are choices. The Ben Franklin model — list the virtues you admire, then set out to acquire them. *Munger:* "Warren and I both know some very successful businessmen who have not one true friend on earth. And rightly so." See [warren-buffett](../people/warren-buffett.md).

**M26 · ★ Investing doesn't require a high IQ — temperament is the whole game.** *Buffett:* "A great IQ is not needed … you do not have to be terrifically smart to do well as an investor, at all." The Rosetta Stone is Graham: "think about a stock as a part of a business." Once that reframes the question, no advanced math is needed — only the ability to detach from the crowd, define the circle of competence accurately, and insulate against popular opinion. "The idea of listening to lots of people tell you things is just a waste of time." He learned nothing useful from 11–19 (Garfield Drew, Edwards and Magee, Gerald Loeb); reading Graham at 19 changed everything. *Munger:* "If you have a passionate interest in knowing why things are happening — that cast of mind, kept over long periods, gradually improves your ability to cope with reality. And if you don't have that cast of mind, I think you're destined, probably, for failure." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [margin-of-safety](../concepts/margin-of-safety.md).

**M27 · Coca-Cola: market share stable despite marquee-account losses.** *Buffett:* "inevitables" framing was specifically Coke in soft drinks and Gillette in blades/razors, not their whole corporate portfolios. Coke holds ~13% of all liquids Americans drink; its share is higher now than five years ago. Marquee accounts (NFL, Olympics) require judgment — overpaying for a Fuji-at-LA-Olympics-equivalent is a mistake — but not panicked defense of every contract. Pepsi's gains are Mountain Dew, not cola. *Munger:* "I've got nothing to add." See [coca-cola](../entities/coca-cola.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**M28 · ★ Building a moat: time varies enormously by industry.** *Buffett:* Microsoft built its operating-system moat "relatively quickly" in an exploding industry; See's Candy "no way" in shorter than decades (one shop at a time, boxed chocolates bought once or twice a year, embedding yourself in California minds took a generation). NetJets in 1986 was "total infancy," yet Rich Santulli has already built a "sustainable competitive advantage." Coca-Cola took 60 years — from Jacobs Pharmacy in 1886 through Eisenhower's WWII bottling plants — before "a huge impetus." Walt Disney built animated film dominance fairly quickly via Snow White. *Munger:* "Ask Arthur Andersen" — very good names can be lost fast; Wrigley's is harder to lose than Andersen's. "The great trouble with the investment process is that [sustainable advantages] are so damned obvious that the stocks sell at very high prices." See [moat](../concepts/moat.md), [sees-candies](../entities/sees-candies.md), [netjets](../entities/netjets.md).

**M29 · Coke and Gillette are still "inevitables"; acquisitions are a different matter.** *Buffett:* Gillette now at 71% of worldwide blade/razor market by value (up from when he wrote about it). Both have gained share since being called "inevitables." "But it does not allow making 15 or 18 percent a year" from a huge base. Gillette's Duracell acquisition "has not done nearly as well as either the management or the investment bankers thought." *Munger:* "When you've got a wonderful business and you issue shares in it to buy another business, I'd say at least two times out of three, it's a terrible idea." GEICO's ventures into Resolute Insurance and two others were all abandoned — "why would you have an absolutely wonderful business and start one … where you bring nothing to the party?" See [gillette](../entities/gillette.md), [coca-cola](../entities/coca-cola.md), [stock-as-acquisition-currency](../concepts/stock-as-acquisition-currency.md).

**M30 · ★ Confirmation bias and the derivatives reversal: "hell to get out of."** *Buffett:* "The human being is best at interpreting all new information so that their prior conclusions remain intact. That is a talent everyone seems to have mastered." Feedback mechanisms: a non-subservient partner (Munger) who doesn't accept an argument just because Buffett states it; the honest annual report as self-reporting; the absence of a sycophant staff that reverse-engineers conclusions. "Most staffs, if they know you want to buy a company, you're going to get a recommendation." *Munger:* pivots immediately to the live example — Berkshire is "the only big corporation in America that is running off a derivative book." Originally Berkshire allowed GRS to continue; reversing that decision is "very unpleasant," yet they're doing it. "Derivative accounting in America is a sewer is an insult to sewage." In Q1 alone: $88M loss (including ~$30M severance) just to begin exiting; the longest contract runs 40 years; the person who put it on was paid that week. "It's a little like hell. It's easy to get into, and it's hard — very hard — to get out of." *Buffett:* "We didn't want to be in the business when we got in it, and we are now in the process of getting out." *Supersession flag:* GRS continued running off at cumulative loss through the mid-2000s, validating this caution — and this exchange is the direct oral source for the 2002 letter's "financial weapons of mass destruction." See [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md), [general-re](../entities/general-re.md), [institutional-imperative](../concepts/institutional-imperative.md).

## Q&A digest — Afternoon session

**A1 · Blue Chip Stamps: the float business in self-parody.** *Buffett:* the slide shown in error reveals Blue Chip went from $120M revenue when Berkshire took it over to $46,000/year. "We have had one rather incredible performance." The business was similar to frequent flyer miles — float-generating stamps you licked and pasted — but the "transformation from the lick-it stamp to something the public will accept" never happened. "We've still got $47,000 of revenue from the entire state of California. We're building a base." See [blue-chip-stamps](../entities/blue-chip-stamps.md), [float](../concepts/float.md).

**A3 · Pharmaceuticals: basket approach was the right call, still is.** *Buffett:* Berkshire made a mistake in 1993 by not taking a basket approach to pharma: "We actually did buy a tiny, tiny bit — that's worse than buying none almost." The industry "as an aggregate … earns very high returns on capital." Hard to pick winners (Bristol-Myers stumbled, Merck fell back), but "that's no reason not to have a basket approach to the industry." At some valuation level it would be actionable, and "we could put quite a bit of money" there. *Munger:* "Failed to get it right the last time. We'll probably fail to get it right the next time." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [mistakes-of-omission](../concepts/mistakes-of-omission.md).

**A4 · Coca-Cola bottlers: a decent but not wonderful business.** *Buffett:* bottling is capital-intensive (5–6% of revenues in capex "just to stay in place"), competitive on any given weekend at Walmart or a big supermarket, and subject to high leverage from acquisitions. The syrup business is more attractive — "I like the syrup business better." But bottlers riding a billion-serving trademark are not a bad business. Economics don't change whether bottler stakes are consolidated or not. See [coca-cola](../entities/coca-cola.md), [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md).

**A5 · Accounting as starting point — pension assumptions reveal character.** *Buffett:* accounting is "a good starting point for us always, but" — one company using a 12% pension return assumption vs. another using 6% should not be read identically. "We might even think the one that's using six is likely to do better because we might think they're more realistic about the world." Reality beats reported figures; Berkshire always applies its own economic model to any set of numbers. *Munger:* ideally you wouldn't have "capitalization structures designed partly for appearance's sake." See [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md), [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md).

**A6 · New goodwill rules now "making sense."** *Buffett:* Berkshire's prescribed approach — no amortization of goodwill, purchase accounting rather than pooling — is now what FASB has implemented. The prior amortization system "made no difference to us whatsoever — we just looked at the underlying economics," while deterring some competitors from making acquisitions. The change may slightly increase competition for acquisitions, since buyers previously averse to a goodwill charge will now be indifferent. See [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md), [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**A8/A9 · ★ Gen Re's "cultural drift" and "demented and immoral" options.** *Buffett:* General Re's underwriting problems came not from comp plans but from "cultural drift" — a gradual slide when competitors were also drifting, competitive pressure making it tough to walk away from marginal business. "It could be difficult for many people to not succumb and do the same things … it's a competitive world." Joe Brandon took over in September 2001 and the discipline is back. On options: General Re options made more money "by a significant margin … than had been made probably by all other entities combined" — during a period when Gen Re contributed to a decrease in Berkshire's value. Illustrates how fixed-price options reward results divorced from actual performance. *Munger:* "If you have a corporation where a man has risen to be CEO, and he now has hundreds of millions of dollars in the stock of the company … and his directors vote him a great stock option annually to preserve his loyalty … I think it's demented." (Applause) "And I also think it's immoral." The Mayo Clinic surgeons and Cravath partners don't need options in their sixties: "by that time, you ought to have settled loyalties, and you ought to be thinking more about the right example for the company." A logically constructed plan could exist for a CEO responsible for all Berkshire capital, but would require a cost-of-capital hurdle built in: "a fixed-price option over 10 years would accrue dramatic value … for putting the money in a savings account or in government bonds." See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md), [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md), [general-re](../entities/general-re.md).

**A9 · ★ Options: should be expensed; no "double-counting."** *Buffett:* if a company grants options on 9M shares at $100 against 1M existing shares, it has given away 90% of the upside — "that's been a huge cost to the shareholders." The GAAP diluted EPS calculation doesn't capture it unless the stock is above the strike. There is no double-counting: the option is an expense and a dilution and "both factors should be taken into account." Berkshire would accept stock options (at fair value) in lieu of cash for insurance premiums — "we've already done it." *Munger:* John Doerr arguing that expensing options would be double-counting is "an insane argument … I think he's taking a public position that, were it offered to me as part of my employment, I would rather make my living playing a piano in a whorehouse." See [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md), [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).

**A10 · Float size doesn't constrain investment decisions.** *Buffett:* $37B of float doesn't force Berkshire into conventional fixed-income — as long as net worth remains enormous and earning power is high, they can "make decisions as to how assets should be deployed in terms of … where we see the best returns and virtually no risk." Sometimes that's equities at extreme cheapness; sometimes it's bonds as "a default option." *Munger:* "Our constraint doesn't come from structure, it comes from a lack of enthusiasm for stocks generally." See [float](../concepts/float.md), [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md).

**A11 · Gold is not a valuation tool.** *Buffett:* "The price of gold does not enter into my thinking in any way, shape, or form, in terms of how I value a business today, a year ago, 10 years ago, or tomorrow." Correlations between gold and equity prices may exist but "in the end, you can find something that correlates with something else" — the AFC/NFC Super Bowl stock-market correlation is in the same category. All investment reduces to: what will the asset produce in cash over its life? *Munger:* "Warren is right when he says interest rates are very important in determining the value of stocks, generally. And I think he's also right when he says gold is very unimportant." See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md).

**A12 · ★ Investing vs. speculation: the clearest definition.** *Buffett:* "All investment is laying out some money now to get more money back in the future. There are two ways of looking at the getting the money back. One is from what the asset itself will produce — that's investment. One is from what somebody else will pay you for it later on, irrespective of what the asset produces — and I call that speculation." The bubble was vast wealth transfer via speculation; the asset-based investor "doesn't care whether something's quoted because we don't buy it with the idea of selling it to somebody." See's Candy's success has been the cash it produced, not a broker's daily quote. Applies to farms, oil fields, apartment buildings, and REITs identically: "every asset class … what is this thing likely to produce over time? And that's what it's worth." *Munger:* "What makes common stock prices so hard to predict is that a general liquid market creates, from time to time, a Ponzi scheme … it can build to perfectly ridiculous levels, and the levels can last for considerable periods." See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md).

**A13 · Shorting is hard even when you're right.** *Buffett:* "Charlie and I have probably agreed on at least a hundred companies that we felt were frauds … if we had acted on shorting those over the years, we might be broke now, but we were right on probably just about a hundred out of a hundred." The Ponzi-scheme phase of a market can extend far beyond any fundamental overvaluation — you can't predict how high or when it ends. When you're short, you need more and more money as the stock rises; when you're long, a declining price requires no additional capital. *Munger:* "It just isn't worth it to have that much irritation in your life. It isn't that hard to make money somewhere else with less irritation." See [arbitrage](../concepts/arbitrage.md), [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md).

**A14 · ★ Options are an expense — "can you sell it" is not an adequate moral test.** *Buffett:* expensing options doesn't "double count" — the dilution (fewer shares per earnings dollar) and the compensation expense are two separate real costs. The "we can't value options" defense is contradicted by companies dealing in billions of dollars of options simultaneously. The real driver: "the opposition, at bottom, to the expensing of options comes from people who know they're not going to get as many options if they're expensed." Option repricing below the stock price after executives declare it "ridiculously low" in merger discussions is another variant of the same. *Munger:* "The whole thing is disgusting." (see A27) See [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md), [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md), [institutional-imperative](../concepts/institutional-imperative.md).

**A15 · Post-bubble fallout is hard to predict.** *Munger:* Japan — 13 years of negative returns while interest rates fell to virtually zero, with good engineering culture and without a depression — was "so novel that the models of the past totally failed to predict it." A Keynesian democracy with high culture can have this outcome; assuming it's "totally inconceivable in America" is wrong. *Buffett:* "Post-bubble periods … can produce fallout that not everyone will be terribly good at predicting." See [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md).

**A16 · ★ Ben Graham invented the long/short model in 1924 — 30 years before A.W. Jones.** *Buffett:* "In 1924, Ben Graham set up the Benjamin Graham Fund, which was designed exactly along [long/short] lines and even used paired securities." The Federal Reserve's LTCM report credited A.W. Jones with being the father of the hedge fund; Graham preceded him by 30 years and paid a percentage of profits — "all of the attributes of today's hedge funds." Graham's experience: a high percentage of paired bets worked, "but the one time out of four … that he was wrong lost a lot more money than the average of the three he was right on." Long/short logic works in theory but the asymmetric math — you can buy the whole company long, you can never short the whole company — creates fatal risk. *Munger:* Graham was "30 years ahead of A.W. Jones." See [ben-graham](../people/ben-graham.md), [concentration-vs-diversification](../concepts/concentration-vs-diversification.md).

**A17 · ★ "Creative accounting is an absolute curse to a civilization."** *Munger:* double-entry bookkeeping was one of humanity's great inventions ("a north Italian development, spread by a monk") — a tool for keeping economic affairs under control. "Anything that sort of undoes the monk's work by turning this great system into a tool for fraud and folly does enormous damage to the country." Enron may produce some corrective fallout, but "we'll always get Enron-type behavior, it may be moderated some." On Arthur Andersen: the professional partnership model requires extraordinary risk control — "it's crazy, in the kind of world we inhabit, to operate in any other way." Capitalism without failure "is like religion without hell." See [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md), [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md).

**A18 · ★ Circle of competence: doubt is the answer.** *Buffett:* "If you have doubts about something being into your circle of competence, it isn't." Competence is self-announcing — you can understand Coke, McDonald's, Walmart, and a custom picture-frame business at first description; but "if you get to something and you don't — you're not sure whether you understand it or not, you don't." A small circle is fine: "My circle of competence is pretty small, but it's big enough." Even understanding every individual transaction of a large finance company doesn't mean the enterprise as a whole is within the circle, if the funding dynamics are unclear. *Munger:* "If you have competence, you almost automatically have a feeling of where the edge of competence is. Because after all, it wouldn't be much of a competence if you didn't know its boundary." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**A19 · Population and carrying capacity — SKIPPED.** Social/demographic policy question following morning's shareholder proposal. Off-domain.

**A20 · Expect "satisfactory" results, not a repeat of 1982–1999.** *Buffett:* "The only thing I can tell you is it'll be less than it's been in the last 20 years … I think it'll be satisfactory, compared to most alternatives." Returns of 6–7% on equities are not terrible in a world of relatively low inflation; those with expectations built by 1982–1999 "are going to be very disappointed." If Berkshire keeps obtaining float at low cost and sees occasional market opportunities in bonds or equities, "we can have a return that we won't be ashamed of." *Munger:* "Dampen the expectations way down … that's maturity and good sense." See [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md), [float](../concepts/float.md).

**A21 · Insurance discipline is geography-independent: what Jack Ringwalt proved.** *Buffett:* it makes no difference whether you write insurance from London, Washington, Omaha, or Germany — "it really depends on … the discipline to look at thousands of different things and select from them a group to do." Jack Ringwalt, who founded National Indemnity in 1941, "never looked at an actuarial book in his life … but he had enough sense to stick with what he understood … and make sure he got paid appropriately for the risk." He "beat the pants off people that had been around for a hundred years in Hartford, who had vast agency organizations and huge amounts of capital and actuaries." The Faraday syndicate at Lloyd's: Berkshire's takedown of capacity rose from ~30% to "well into the mid-90s percent." *Munger:* "If you combine a vast exposure with a vast decline rate, you have an opportunity to make quite a few good decisions." See [national-indemnity](../entities/national-indemnity.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**A22 · The genie story: your most important asset is yourself.** *Buffett:* imagines a genie appearing to a 16-year-old and offering any car in the world — but it's the only car they'll ever get. The rational response: read the manual, garage it, change the oil twice as often, fix every nick that day. "And then I would suggest … they are going to get exactly one mind and one body, and that's the mind and body they're going to have at age 40 and 50 and 60." The analogy is to human capital: "A person graduating from college, I will be glad to pay them $50,000 for 10 percent of all their earnings for the rest of their lives" — which implies $500,000 of value with no net worth at all. The most important asset vastly exceeds any financial asset they own, and should be treated accordingly. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**A23 · Deep pockets and the tort system: avoid aggregation of liability.** *Buffett:* Berkshire "would be absolutely crazy" to provide airport guards — not because it couldn't be done responsibly, but because "people would go after us because we would have deep pockets" for any security breach, regardless of merit. The tort system "selects" who provides certain services and products: a judgment-proof basement operator can do the same job with no liability; Berkshire would bear the risk of even alleged failures. *Munger:* "I think there are whole areas of activity where, for the already rich, the tort system makes participation foolish." Applies also to workers' compensation in California. See [berkshire-hathaway](../entities/berkshire-hathaway.md).

**A24 · ★ Finova: margin of safety absorbed everything 9/11 broke.** *Buffett:* Berkshire and Leucadia put up $5.6B to reorganize Finova, the failed finance company. Post-9/11, the aircraft portfolio (many older planes to weaker lessees) and resort-related receivables were "appreciably less" valuable. "We bought our position at 67 cents on the dollar, and we've already gotten 70 cents on the dollar, plus these bonds, plus we get the override on the Berkadia loan. We got all our money back, and then some." Will likely make "some hundreds of millions in aggregate over time … but a lot went wrong." *Munger:* "It's an interesting example of Ben Graham's margin of safety principle. A whole lot has gone wrong that we didn't predict, and yet we're coming out fine." See [margin-of-safety](../concepts/margin-of-safety.md), [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md).

**A25 · Real estate: usually accurately priced, worse for C corps.** *Buffett:* real estate "is not a commodity, but it tends to be more accurately priced — particularly developed real estate — most of the time." The exception: the RTC period, when a forced seller didn't know what they owned — "a lot of people in this room made a lot of money off of that." Otherwise, REITs know approximately what a Class A office building will produce. *Munger:* Berkshire also has a structural disadvantage — a C corp layer of tax between real estate income and the people who own it, making real estate "a very lousy investment" for Subchapter C entities. "We have no special competence in the field means that we spend almost no time thinking about anything in real estate." See [berkshire-hathaway](../entities/berkshire-hathaway.md).

**A26 · ★ Black-Scholes is wrong for long-term, business-specific options.** *Buffett:* Black-Scholes works when you know only past price history and dividends, and when the option is short-term. "But if it's a long-term option and you think you know something, it's an insane way to value the option." Berkshire received on June 3 a $60M payout from a put written when the S&P was at ~1,400; in exchange they gave an upside call above ~2,000. At the time, option models said these were equivalent (a cashless transaction); Buffett judged differently. Companies use Black-Scholes in compensation footnotes because it produces the lowest number — and then shorten the assumed option term to make it even lower. *Munger:* "It's a Mad Hatter's tea party, and the only thing that's consistent in it is that the whole thing is disgusting." See [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md), [model-risk](../concepts/model-risk.md).

**A27 · Investment-banking culture has deteriorated — "can you sell it" is not enough.** *Munger:* describes placing a $6M bond issue with Omaha/Lincoln underwriters 40 years ago: "they cared terribly whether their customers … were going to get their money back … they fussed over every clause in the indenture." Now: "anything that can be sold at a profit will be sold at a profit … that is not an adequate test for investment banking." Certain clients should "never be accepted at all." The First Normandy episode (a man with a fabricated record who went public for a day before the SEC pulled it): "you'd think [extending credit to him] if you wrote it as satire, people would say it was too extreme to be funny." See [institutional-imperative](../concepts/institutional-imperative.md), [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

**A28 · ★ Dexter Shoe: "a dumb decision" paid for in stock.** *Buffett:* Berkshire "lost a very significant amount of money on Dexter, thanks to a dumb decision — and maybe several dumb decisions." The domestic shoe business went offshore: nearly 1.2B pairs used in the US, and only ~5% now made domestically. "I was very wrong in paying what I did, and paying it in the manner I did, which was stock, in the case of Dexter, for a domestic shoe manufacturer." The Dexter brand is now part of H.H. Brown selling mostly foreign-produced product; the shoe business will be "substantial and reasonably profitable" but not in the way originally envisioned. *Munger:* "That shows … that no matter how hard you work at having systems for avoiding error … you still make mistakes. And I think I can confidently promise it won't be our last mistake." *Buffett reflects on the human cost:* "We take a little hit financially and we make it up by some trading strategy in government bonds that requires no effort … and when you think of the consequences to the people that have spent a lifetime learning one trade, and who live in those areas, through no fault of their own … we're the lucky ones." *Supersession flag:* this is the explicit post-mortem on a position praised in [berkshire-1994-meeting](berkshire-1994-meeting.md) (M32) and flagged as a supersession candidate there; the 2002 meeting closes the loop — the managers were superb but the economics (domestic labor cost 10× offshore) could not be rescued by good management. See [dexter-shoe](../entities/dexter-shoe.md), [commodity-business-economics](../concepts/commodity-business-economics.md), [mistakes-of-omission](../concepts/mistakes-of-omission.md).

**A30 · Simplicity enables low audit costs.** *Munger:* "One of the reasons our audit costs are so low is we have this passion for keeping everything simple. We don't want to be difficult to audit." See's Candy goes to all-cash every December, "as if it were a farm where the crop came in and was sold in December — an idiot could audit the See's Candy company without getting into trouble." Berkshire prefers activities that are simple and produce cash. On Enron: "auditors have been unduly compliant to client wishes over the last few decades … they started suggesting what I would consider quite dubious accounting … to make their figures look better later on." See [sees-candies](../entities/sees-candies.md), [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md).

**A31 · Arthur Andersen: "capitalism without failure is like religion without hell."** *Munger:* Andersen's failure was "very unfair and totally undeserved" for the lower-level employees. But "when it gets this bad, and the lack of adequate control mechanisms throughout the system … I think capitalism should just accept this kind of unfairness … and let the firms go down." Professional partnerships require strict cultures of risk control: "it's crazy, in the kind of world we inhabit, to operate in any other way." *Buffett:* Enron "will have a distinct beneficial effect on auditing, and it was needed." See [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md), [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

**A32 · No formula for predicting great investors.** *Buffett:* can't give a formula for picking a great investor, just as he can't give a formula for picking a spouse — "there are too many variables." But given a few hours with a dozen bright MBAs, he and Munger could pick someone who would be in the upper quartile. The Salomon crisis Saturday-morning pick (in 3 hours) proved this: he knew he had the right person even without being certain. *Munger:* "When multiple factors are causing success, great strength in one will compensate for weakness in another." See [warren-buffett](../people/warren-buffett.md).

**A33 · See's won't sell through Costco — distribution integrity is non-negotiable.** *Buffett:* "We don't want people discounting our candy, any more than Rolex wants somebody discounting their watches … We are already getting a bargain at our retail prices." See's has not found a way to succeed with its own stores outside the West despite 30 years of trying — one pound per capita of boxed chocolates per year is a small market, and Americans don't buy it for themselves. Costco is "a wonderful operation" built on special prices; See's distribution integrity is the product's integrity — "never the twain shall meet." *Supersession note:* See's expanded in subsequent years but the West-only strength pattern largely persisted. See [sees-candies](../entities/sees-candies.md), [direct-distribution-moat](../concepts/direct-distribution-moat.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**A36 · Kirby vacuum: consumer protection already in place before ABC News.** *Buffett:* the ABC News piece on Kirby sales practices to the elderly did not mention Berkshire's 10-year policy (unique in the industry): anyone over 65 who buys a Kirby and is unhappy can return it for a full refund, any time within 11 months and 29 days. The featured complainant had actually used the product for eight months and received a full refund. "I do not regard it as a great moment in ABC journalism." See [berkshire-hathaway](../entities/berkshire-hathaway.md).
