# Berkshire Hathaway 2001 Annual Meeting

Held in early May 2001 — with the dot-com bust well underway but **before 9/11** — this is the post-bubble meeting. Buffett and Munger spend much of it dissecting how a mania works ("a chance for people to monetize the hopes of others"), why promotion beats performance in a bull market, and why a whole investing class extrapolating the past is "massively stupid." Around that, they restate the load-bearing system in unusually blunt form: risk is permanent loss and inadequate return, *never* volatility; "cost of capital" is opportunity cost, not a CAPM number; growth is just a term in the value equation; and float — generated cheaply — is the engine, while the moat is the whole game and is being contested by scale retailers. It is also a meeting heavy with quiet warnings that events would soon validate: rising unease about financial institutions (Freddie/Fannie sold), and a derivatives book at General Re Securities that "neither Charlie nor I know fully" — the seed of the later "financial weapons of mass destruction." Per the wiki's two-layer convention, a distilled thematic top precedes a **comprehensive Q&A digest** that captures every value-bearing exchange.

## How to read the Q&A digest

- **★** marks a timeless, load-bearing exchange worth citing in the future; unmarked entries still carry investor value but are secondary or period-specific.
- Answers are attributed separately to **Buffett** and **Munger** — a Munger line appears only when he added a distinct lesson (he answered "Nothing to add" to most). This keeps the wisdom fairly credited to both.
- **M#/A#** map to the numbered items in the raw morning/afternoon transcript for traceability.

## Key themes

### After the bubble: promotion beats performance, and the cure is lower expectations

The 2001 meeting is a post-mortem on the mania. The internet, Buffett says, "was a chance for people to monetize the hopes of others … a huge trap for the public" — much wealth was *promoted* into existence, very little *produced* (M15). The deeper lesson is structural: "the biggest money made … in Wall Street in recent years, has not been made by great performance, but it's been made by great promotion" (M30), and the machine self-perpetuates because nobody has a financial interest in telling you to expect less. Friends who "ought to be mowing lawns" can raise $125M hedge funds because "the form doesn't create talent" (M28, M30). The antidote is arithmetic and humility: corporate profits stay ~6% of GDP, so stocks deliver maybe 6–7% over the next 15–20 years, and "anybody that expects to make 15 percent per year … is living in a dream world" (M18). Munger is blunter — extrapolating the recent past is "not slightly stupid, massively stupid," and "there's more felicity to be gained … from reducing expectations than in any other way" (M33). See [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md), [institutional-imperative](../concepts/institutional-imperative.md).

### Risk is permanent loss, not volatility — and "cost of capital" is opportunity cost, not CAPM

Asked to measure risk, Buffett is categorical: "we regard volatility as a measure of risk to be nuts." See's Candy loses money in two of four quarters yet "is one of the least risky businesses I know"; you can find smooth businesses that are terrible and volatile ones that are wonderful (A20). Real risk is "the risk of permanent capital loss" plus "an inadequate return on the kind of capital we put in." The same demolition is aimed at the cost-of-capital priesthood: CAPM "concluded that the companies that made the most money had the highest costs of capital," which Munger calls "inanity" — "What is Berkshire's cost of capital? We have this damn capital. It just keeps multiplying and multiplying. What is its cost?" The honest substitute is opportunity cost: "at any given time when we consider an investment, we have to compare it to the best alternative investment we have at that time" (A22). Buffett's gloss: in practice "the cost of capital is about a quarter percent below the return promised by any deal that the CEO wants to do." See [risk-vs-volatility](../concepts/risk-vs-volatility.md), [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md).

### The mistakes that matter are the blown opportunities inside your circle

The most-cited exchange is Munger on mistakes of omission: "what really costs, in comparison with what easily might have been, are the blown opportunities … it's an awesome amount of money." His Belridge Oil story — declining 1,500 more shares because he'd "have to sell something" — "has cost me $200 million" (M7). Buffett sharpens the definition: an error is *only* failing to act on "something we understand … within our circle of competence" (missing a software fortune is not an error); and the worst sin is the half-measure — "we do an eyedropper's worth of it, when we could do it very big," what Munger calls thumbsucking. Conventional accounting never records these, "but they're in our scorebook." See [mistakes-of-omission](../concepts/mistakes-of-omission.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

### Growth is not a category — it's a term in the value equation

"There is no such thing as growth stocks or value stocks, the way Wall Street generally portrays them" (M13). Growth helps value *only* "when … by adding capital now, you add more cash availability later on, at a rate … considerably higher than the current rate of interest." FlightSafety pouring $200M into simulators is growth, "but whether that's good or bad depends on what we earn on that incremental … investment over time"; the airline business has grown "ever since Orville took off" and "growth has been a curse," while See's grows "with relatively little incremental investment." The reflex to "move from growth stocks to value stocks … just doesn't make any sense." See [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md), [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md).

### The moat is the whole game — and it is being contested

Buffett ties the entire enterprise to the moat: the instruction to every manager is "to try and not only protect, but enlarge, the moat. And if you enlarge the moat, everything else follows" (A13). But 2001 produces a rare, on-the-record disagreement — Buffett thinks moats are "as sustainable … as the moats that I saw 30 years ago"; Munger answers "the old moats … are getting filled in. And the new moats are harder to predict … I would say it's getting harder." The clearest live example is the erosion of brand moats by scale retailers: Kellogg and Campbell lost ground partly by pushing pricing too far and partly because "the buying power got more concentrated" — Costco even forced Hanes into a co-branded Hanes-Kirkland pantyhose, which "wouldn't have happened 20 years ago" (A24). See [moat](../concepts/moat.md), [franchise-vs-business](../concepts/franchise-vs-business.md).

### Float is the engine — generate it, but worship low cost, not size

Munger reduces the whole machine to a hedgehog's one big thing: "If you generate float at 3 percent per annum and buy businesses that earn 13 percent per annum with the proceeds of the float … that's a pretty good position to be in" (M10). The discipline is that *cost* dominates *growth*: "Anybody can generate float … There's an unlimited market for dumb insurance policies," pleasant on day one and bleeding cash forever after (M20). Berkshire's reported cost of float ran 6% in 2000 (inflated by deliberate "pain today, gain tomorrow" deals) and was trending under 3% — "but a mega-catastrophe could change all of that" (M6, M10). See [float](../concepts/float.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

### In financial institutions you find out too late; in derivatives you may not find out at all

Berkshire sold Freddie Mac and Fannie Mae not over regulation but because "the risk profile had changed somewhat" — and the governing rule is that "you spot troubles in financial institutions late. It's just the nature of the beast" (A12). Munger: "Financial institutions tend to make us nervous when they're trying to do well." The derivatives book at General Re Securities is the same fear one level deeper: 17,000 open tickets that "neither Charlie nor I know fully"; the danger is paying people "a lot of money upfront" to "make deals where you won't know the outcome for 15 or 20 years," and an accounting convention that "front-ends way too much income" — "like going into the taxi cab business with a 30-year depreciation rate" (A14). Both warnings would be vindicated within a decade. See [lending-discipline](../concepts/lending-discipline.md), [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md).

## Q&A digest — Morning session

**M2 · First preference is buying whole operating businesses.** *Buffett:* Berkshire bought eight businesses in the last 16–18 months; "our first preference has always … been to be buying outstanding operating businesses," with marketable securities a secondary channel less friendly to making money lately. Hopes to add ~40 more businesses without issuing "any appreciable number of more shares." See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**M3 · A sector bet needs better underlying economics than tech offered.** *Munger:* "the future of the pharmaceutical industry was easier to predict than … high-technology"; in pharma "almost everybody did well," in tech "there are many permanent casualties." *Buffett:* nothing obvious said the tech sector in aggregate was undervalued, "whereas we should have had enough sense to recognize that the pharmaceutical industry, as a group, was undervalued" — pharma has a far better record of high returns on equity. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**M5 · State insurance regulation hasn't impeded us; PUHCA has.** *Buffett:* nothing in state insurance statutes has slowed an acquisition, but the 1935 Public Utility Holding Company Act limits Berkshire to 5% of a utility — "we might well … have bought an entire utility business if … that statute weren't present." *Munger:* nothing to add. See [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md).

**M6 · "Pain today, gain tomorrow" deals distort one year, then pay for decades.** *Buffett:* deals booked at a big current loss in exchange for many years of float ("good losses") added ~1.5 points to the 2000 cost of float; absent them it was ~4.5%, and Q1 2001 ran just under 3% annualized. The point of disclosing them is so holders "can make an adjusted cost to float." *Munger:* "almost all good businesses have occasions where they're making today look a little worse … to help tomorrow … very much the friends of the shareholders." See [cost-of-float](../concepts/cost-of-float.md), [float](../concepts/float.md).

**★ M7 · The biggest mistakes are the blown opportunities.** *Munger:* the most extreme mistakes in Berkshire's history "are mistakes of omission … they show up in opportunity costs"; his Belridge Oil refusal "has cost me $200 million" because he "had to go to a slight inconvenience and sell something." *Buffett:* errors count only within "our circle of competence"; the worst is doing "an eyedropper's worth … when we could do it very big" — thumbsucking. See [mistakes-of-omission](../concepts/mistakes-of-omission.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**M8 · Price product-liability tail risk pessimistically.** *Buffett:* not worried about Coke/See's/Dairy Queen sugar liability (he's "been living on" those products for 70 years), but product liability generally "is a fertile field for the plaintiff's bar"; Berkshire has passed up businesses over it and builds in "a margin of safety" by assuming the trend accelerates. (The surrounding tort-reform/junk-science politics is dropped as off-domain.) See [margin-of-safety](../concepts/margin-of-safety.md).

**M9 · "We don't have cash around just to have cash."** *Buffett:* Berkshire never targets a cash percentage; the Wall Street asset-allocation prescriptions ("60 percent stocks and 30 …") are "total nonsense." Cash piles up only when they can't find things to buy, or after a sale; "we'll never employ it just to employ it," and "we don't like lots of leverage, and we never will." *Munger:* can't add anything. See [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md), [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md).

**★ M10 · The hedgehog: float at 3, businesses at 13.** *Munger:* "to some extent we're like the hedgehog that knows one big thing. If you generate float at 3 percent per annum and buy businesses that earn 13 percent … with the proceeds of the float … that's a pretty good position to be in." *Buffett:* what they actually look for in any business, regardless of cost structure, is "an enduring competitive advantage" plus top-notch people. See [float](../concepts/float.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**★ M11 · A commodity-cost business cannot carry above-market labor.** *Buffett:* in airlines the figure that matters is cost per available seat mile; USAir's ~12¢ vs. Southwest's <8¢ meant "you're going to get killed, eventually" — "the guy with the lower cost comes in and kills you." Tells the Buffalo News story: a 1981 strike by a <2% union threatened to make the paper "valueless"; he told them, "if you're smart enough to figure out … the point you can push us to and still come back … you're smarter than I am." *Munger:* airline unions are "brutally tough" in a commodity-style business; "no individual airline can take a long shutdown." See [commodity-business-economics](../concepts/commodity-business-economics.md), [buffalo-evening-news](../entities/buffalo-evening-news.md).

**★ M12 · Berkshire's advantages — and its one big disadvantage.** *Buffett:* "our checks clear," deals get done fast with no financing contingency (they "bought Johns Manville because the other group had financing difficulties"), sellers keep running their businesses, and the ownership is "more stable than any company our size." "And we are under no pressure to do anything dumb … it's not because anybody's making us do it." The biggest disadvantage is size. *Munger:* "This is not a hog heaven period for Berkshire. The investment game is getting more and more competitive." See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md).

**★ M13 · There is no such thing as growth stocks or value stocks.** *Buffett:* growth "is part of the value equation," positive only when added capital throws off cash "at a rate … considerably higher than the current rate of interest"; airline growth was "a curse," See's growth is cheap. "Anybody that tells you, 'You ought to have your money in growth stocks or value stocks,' really does not understand investing." *Munger:* Berkshire is "especially partial to laying out large sums … where we won't have to be smart again." See [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md), [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md).

**M14 · Invest in yourself — but passive accumulation alone is "a failed life."** *Buffett:* a bright young person is "a $500,000 asset just standing there"; "the best investment you can make, at an early age, is in yourself." *Munger:* a word of caution — "if all you succeed in doing in your life is to get early rich from passive holding of little bits of paper … it's a failed life. Life is more than being shrewd at passive wealth accumulation." See [warren-buffett](../people/warren-buffett.md).

**★ M15 · The internet let people "monetize the hopes of others."** *Buffett:* the net now looks like "less of a competitive threat" to retailers (dot-com jewelers and furniture sellers with hundred-million valuations "have vanished"), while remaining "a huge opportunity" for GEICO and See's. But "very few have been turned into wealth by actually producing cash results"; it was "a huge trap for the public." *Munger:* the credit-and-delivery grocery business "barely supported one family for a hundred years," yet someone turned it into "a great internet idea … that can only be described as mania" (Webvan). See [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md).

**M16 · "We don't have a master plan."** *Buffett:* no strategy sessions, no staff, no reports — "we try to look at what comes in … for things we understand, where … a durable, competitive advantage … and where the price is sensible." Two decades out, "we will own a lot more businesses," with insurance still the biggest. "We have no more master plan now than we had back in 1965 when we bought the textile mill." *Munger:* "almost a sure thing" there's more value behind each share in 20 years, and "an absolutely sure thing that the annual percentage rate of progress will go way down." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**★ M18 · Reset expectations: stocks ~6–7%, and pension assumptions are a scandal-in-waiting.** *Buffett:* corporate profits stay ~4–6% of GDP, so business value grows roughly with GDP; stocks are "a perfectly decent way to make 6 or 7 percent a year over the next 15 or 20 years," but expecting 15% is "a dream world." Perversely, pension funds used ~6% assumptions when prospects were good and now use "9 percent or better" when prospects are poor — because lowering it "will change the charge to earnings substantially." "Big-bath" inventory write-offs let managers dump future costs into one year. *Munger:* "pension fund accounting is drifting into scandal"; it's like projecting that "the longer it's been without an earthquake the less likely an earthquake is." See [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md), [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md).

**M19 · Executive Jet won't be "mature" for decades.** *Buffett:* tens of thousands of potential customers, only ~700 jets produced a year, so growth runs for "a long, long time"; a mature net margin "if you're talking 5 percent after-tax … is probably a reasonable figure," but that's "pure speculation." See [netjets](../entities/netjets.md).

**★ M20 · "There's an unlimited market for dumb insurance policies."** *Buffett:* float should grow ~$2.5B (~10%) in 2001, but Berkshire is already ~10% of all U.S. property-casualty float, so 10% can't persist for 25 years; "the even bigger objective is to keep it low-cost." Any manager could generate $5B of float "in a minute … and we would be paying the price for decades to come." *Munger:* agrees long term it won't happen; only "higher-cost float" could change the math, which they avoid. See [float](../concepts/float.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**M21 · Why GEICO stays in the U.S. — the opportunity cost is managerial talent.** *Buffett:* extending GEICO's low-cost model abroad has been "remarkably hard"; the binding constraint isn't capital ("Financial cost bothers us not at all") but "human cost" — he'd rather have Tony Nicely's team gaining U.S. share than "starting in a project in Europe or Asia." Tried to export See's "50 different ways" with "very limited success." See [geico](../entities/geico.md).

**M23 · The real catastrophe risk is aggregation in primary writers, not capped reinsurance.** *Buffett:* Berkshire writes "huge limits" but always caps them; retroactive policies assume asbestos liabilities "will be paid in full" but are capped. The frightening exposures are the unhedged aggregations — a homeowner writer with 15% of Long Island or Florida, or "Freddie Mac or Fannie Mae … guaranteeing mortgages" in quake/flood zones (the New Madrid fault "caused church bells to ring in Boston"). "In insurance, the unthinkable always happens." (Folds M34: USG/asbestos is "one-tenth of one percent of Berkshire.") See [super-cat-insurance](../concepts/super-cat-insurance.md), [noah-rule](../concepts/noah-rule.md).

**M24 · State Farm and the mutual-vs-stock structure.** *Buffett:* State Farm is a low-cost mutual that "can subsidize … current auto policy holders with … profits derived from … the past" and faces no return-on-capital demand — "that's always true when a stock company competes with a mutual"; GEICO is still lower-cost. *Munger:* State Farm is "a thoroughly admirable company." See [geico](../entities/geico.md).

**M25/M27 · Service moats don't face dumb-competitor risk the way commodity businesses do — but legacy cultures create cost impossibilities a brand name can't overcome.** *Buffett:* on the dumb-competitor question in service businesses — "I don't worry about the dumbest competitor in a business that's service. The customer will figure that out over time." On [netjets](../entities/netjets.md): with 265 planes, four-hour notice access to 5,500 airports, and European coverage — "nobody's going to catch us, in my view, in fractional ownership." *Munger:* on United Airlines' announced fractional jet venture: "A senior United Airlines pilot now makes about $300,000 a year plus fancy fringes, including pension. And what he does is work a very limited hour — number of hours a month. And about half of that he spends sleeping in a comfortable bunk on long ocean flights. That is not a culture that will work well in fractional jet ownership." The transferable lesson: service businesses built on high personal-interaction moats don't face the same asymmetric commodity-price death spiral as airlines (where the low-cost entrant simply kills you on unit economics), but legacy cultures that embed structurally high costs into labor contracts create impossibilities that brand recognition cannot rescue — the cost is baked in before the competition arrives. See [netjets](../entities/netjets.md), [commodity-business-economics](../concepts/commodity-business-economics.md). (M25/M27)

**★ M26 · The "trick" of underwriting is finding variables that correlate.** *Buffett:* good auto underwriting means sorting drivers by accident propensity using a manageable set of predictors; GEICO's ~5M policyholders let it find correlations like "people with a good credit history are better drivers … We don't care too much why … What we really need to know is that the two factors correlate" — while screening out spurious correlations. Fast, fair claim settlement matters because the un-injured "start feeling worse … as they talk to more and more lawyers." (Folds A19: GEICO can't beat a USAA preferred risk because it scores by aggregate criteria, not individual observation.) See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md), [geico](../entities/geico.md).

**★ M28 · "The form doesn't create talent."** *Buffett:* renaming something a "hedge fund" or "asset allocation firm" "doesn't make anybody any smarter"; promoters extrapolate a few past successes to attract money now. Recounts the Graham-Newman origin — its sister partnership "Newman and Graham" ran "hedge-fund style," which is the model he copied in 1956. Bets that aggregate hedge-fund returns "will not hit 10 percent" over the next 15 years. *Munger:* the industry has grown enormously; "you get these waves of fashion that go to great extremes." See [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md), [institutional-imperative](../concepts/institutional-imperative.md).

**M29 · Small sums have a far bigger universe.** *Buffett:* from 1950 his returns "averaged about 50 percent a year … 37 points better than the Dow," working with "a tiny, tiny … amount of money," going through "20,000 pages in the Moody's … manuals … twice." As capital grows "the universe of possible ideas … shrinking dramatically." With a small sum "you will find some things that promise very large returns compared to what we will be able to deliver." *Munger:* a brilliant man with little money "should work in very obscure stocks … but it's a long slog." See [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md).

**M30 · Promotion, not performance, is Wall Street's biggest money maker.** *Buffett:* "the monetization of hope and greed … is a way to make a huge amount of money"; friends whose record says "they ought to be mowing lawns" can still raise a $125M hedge fund. "The biggest money made … has not been made by great performance, but … by great promotion." *Munger:* "the current scene is obscene. There's too much mania … too much misleading sales material … too much on the television emphasizing speculation." See [institutional-imperative](../concepts/institutional-imperative.md).

**M31 · Expensing stock options: powerful forces want false accounting.** *Munger:* the options accounting is "corrupt … because you like a certain outcome better than another," but fighting FASB is "like splitting your lance against stone." *Buffett:* when FASB tried to act, "the pressure … American business brought on … Congress" was incredible — he likens it to an 1890s Indiana bill to set pi at three because the true value was "too difficult." The only fix is institutional investors banding together, but many "are getting paid extraordinary sums" and "focus very much on matters of form and not substance." See [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md), [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

**★ M32 · No company should predict 15% annual growth.** *Buffett:* predicting 15% is "a mistake" that "leads people to stretch on accounting" and change trade practices (Gillette's Duracell "has not turned out the way … hoped"; the bankers' presentations "would look pretty silly now"). The math is brutal: 15% would take Berkshire to "a trillion-six in 20 years," and a no-cash $500B business would need to deliver "75 billion this year … 86 and a quarter billion next year." *Munger:* "stocks sell like Rembrandts. They don't sell based on the value … They sell based on the fact that Rembrandts have gone up in value in the past" — imagine filling every pension fund with Rembrandts. (Folds M33: 15% for Berkshire is "so close to zero, it's not worth calculating"; Munger — extrapolation is "massively stupid," and "moderate expectations will do fine.") See [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md), [institutional-imperative](../concepts/institutional-imperative.md).

## Q&A digest — Afternoon session

**A1 · Float has been "a huge asset," roughly a quarter of book-value gain.** *Buffett:* concedes the questioner's estimate that ~5–6 points of book-value growth came from float "is probably maybe on the high side," but float is "largely tantamount to equity because we've had so much equity." Added US Liability, an excess-and-surplus carrier, with an underwriting profit and added float — "the best of all worlds." See [float](../concepts/float.md).

**★ A2 · High U.S. returns on equity are part genuine, part financial engineering.** *Buffett:* not solely accounting — American business benefits from "more of a meritocracy" — but options accounting and "big-bath" write-offs flatter the numbers. *Munger:* take General Electric — "a deliberate increase in financial leverage … a deliberate increase in repurchase of stock … even when they're paying huge multiples of book value … and … writing off everything in sight." "Think of how high we could drive our return on equity … We could run Berkshire with no equity. And then people could say … 'these guys have finally learned how to manage the damn thing.'" Part shrewd management, part "gamesmanship." See [return-on-equity-yardstick](../concepts/return-on-equity-yardstick.md), [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md).

**A3 · Save early, stay out of debt, keep accumulating knowledge.** *Buffett:* "getting a stake early is very useful, and getting knowledge early is very useful"; the beauty of investing is "everything is cumulative … The stuff I learned when I was 20 is useful today." Stay away from credit cards. *Munger:* before "your feelings totally take over, you should look carefully at both parents and all four grandparents." (Folds A9, see below.) See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**A4 · GEICO retention is mostly mix, not decay.** *Buffett:* the apparent drop in the retention ratio reflects a shift toward standard/non-standard and newer policies (both higher-lapse), not a real fall — preferred business "five or more years" has "a very, very high retention ratio." The mix is now swinging back toward preferred. See [geico](../entities/geico.md).

**A5 · "True synergy" between General Re and Berkshire's reinsurance.** *Buffett:* Gen Re/Cologne bring distribution and a knowledge base National Indemnity could never accumulate; Berkshire lets them "retain much bigger portions" they previously laid off via retrocession, and can answer "in an hour" what others "wouldn't know what to do with in a month" — Lloyd's-like capacity. "There's really true synergy." *Supersession flag:* this upbeat read predates the reckoning — General Re's mispriced terrorism aggregation surfaced in the post-9/11 [berkshire-2001-letter](berkshire-2001-letter.md) (the "[noah-rule](../concepts/noah-rule.md)" — knowing the odds doesn't help if you're not paid for the exposure), and Gen Re ran underwriting losses for years after. See [general-re](../entities/general-re.md), [super-cat-insurance](../concepts/super-cat-insurance.md).

**A7 · Concrete, cement, and aggregates are understandable businesses.** *Buffett:* fundamental businesses with periodic regional overcapacity; "at a price … for low-cost capacity and advantageously located raw materials … we would do it." Munger has "a fair amount of familiarity with it." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ A8 · Business schools are "pathetic" at teaching investing — because investing is valuing a business.** *Buffett:* "what you really want a course on investing to be is how to value a business … if you don't know how to value a business, you don't know how to value a stock," yet that "just isn't taught," because the teachers "don't know themselves … so they teach something that says, 'Nobody knows anything,' which is the efficient market theory." CEOs who "feel helpless … on the question of asset allocation" then hire investment bankers who "get 20X if they do it and X if they don't." *Munger:* schools do fine on accounting but "miss one enormous opportunity" — learning to invest "you'll become a much better business manager." (Folds A31: thinking right beats years of practice — "it's way more important what you've thought about for two years than what you've practiced for 10 years … basically they've got their head screwed on right in the first place.") See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**A9 · "It's crazy to get in debt."** *Buffett:* on 18% credit-card debt — "the idea of trying to borrow money at 18 percent … thinking you're going to get ahead in life, it isn't going to work … Charlie and I can't make money with 18 percent money. I mean, we're looking around for float because we don't want to pay 5 percent." Once in debt, "it is hell to get out." See [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md).

**A10 · A chronic trade deficit is "selling off a tiny bit of the farm."** *Buffett:* a net trade deficit means a country "consuming more than it's producing," trading away assets "to live a little bit better"; sustainable for a rich country with a good record, "but by and large, it's not a good policy … to run large trade deficits year after year." *Munger:* fine if you're importing power plants like the early U.S., not "radios and television sets." (Seed of the 2003 "Squanderville" essay.) See [american-tailwind](../concepts/american-tailwind.md).

**A11 · Satisfied sellers are a "recruiting force."** *Buffett:* Berkshire won't do auctions ("if that's the way they look at their business, we may get more unpleasant surprises"); instead "a reasonable percentage of our acquisitions come … because we've made another acquisition in the past where the seller was happy" — like NetJets, where ~70% of business comes from existing owners, and Frank Rooney's referral brought Buffett in. *Munger:* "the best business getter that Berkshire Hathaway has is the business practice that's already on our desk … a very old-fashioned idea." See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**★ A12 · Berkshire sold Freddie and Fannie because trouble shows up late in financials.** *Buffett:* sold "because … the risk profile had changed somewhat," explicitly *not* over regulation. The governing rule: "There's so much about a financial institution that you don't know by looking at just figures" — "you spot troubles in financial institutions late … a financial institution can go beyond the point … of solvency even while they still have plenty of money around." *Munger:* "Financial institutions tend to make us nervous when they're trying to do well." (Validated by the GSEs' 2008 conservatorship.) See [lending-discipline](../concepts/lending-discipline.md), [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md).

**★ A13 · Moats may be getting harder to find — and the job is to widen them.** *Buffett:* doesn't think moat quantity/sustainability "has changed that dramatically in 30 or 40 years" (though Sears and GM "thought there were … very wide moats"). *Munger (rare open disagreement):* "the old moats … are getting filled in. And the new moats are harder to predict … I would say it's getting harder." Either way, "we do tell [managers] to try and not only protect, but enlarge, the moat. And if you enlarge the moat, everything else follows." See [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**★ A14 · Derivatives are "potential dynamite."** *Buffett:* General Re Securities has "probably … 17,000 outstanding tickets," and "neither Charlie nor I know fully … what goes on" — so they rely on Mark Byrne being "smart and trustworthy." The structural danger is paying people "a lot of money upfront" to "make deals where you won't know the outcome for 15 or 20 years"; "people occasionally crack under that" (as at Salomon, and at Edison in California). Recommends *When Genius Failed*. *Munger:* "the accounting profession sold out … It front-ends way too much income … like going into the taxi cab business with a 30-year depreciation rate … another case where the accounting profession has failed the wider civilization." *Supersession flag:* GRS would be wound down over the following years at a large cumulative loss, and this answer is the direct seed of the 2002 letter's "financial weapons of mass destruction" ([berkshire-2002-letter](berkshire-2002-letter.md)). See [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md).

**A15 · Lou Simpson runs GEICO's equity book autonomously.** *Buffett:* Lou is "smart, and careful, and high-grade, and experienced," manages ~$2B independently — "He doesn't know what I'm doing. I don't know what he's doing"; press reports of "Buffett buying X" are sometimes Simpson. (Folds A17: the Gap stake "is totally, 100 percent a Lou Simpson … investment" — Buffett has "never" read Gap's annual report; Lou works a smaller-cap universe, "$100 million positions sometimes.") See [lou-simpson](../people/lou-simpson.md).

**★ A16 · "Confidence is a real coward" — leveraged finance has no honeymoon.** *Buffett:* Finova (the old Greyhound leasing company, ~$14B assets) hit funding difficulties and "they compound on you very quickly … Confidence is a real coward. I mean, it runs when it sees trouble." Berkshire bought $1.428B face of debt at distressed prices and, jointly with Leucadia in "Berkadia," put forward a reorganization plan — aiming, as at Salomon, to model "a very clean, simple, prompt way of cleaning up a corporate mess." *Munger:* hopes for "more cures of bankruptcy following this model." See [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md).

**★ A20 · "We regard volatility as a measure of risk to be nuts."** *Buffett:* risk is "the risk of permanent capital loss" and "an inadequate return," not volatility — See's "will lose money in two quarters of the four … It's one of the least risky businesses I know," while a do-nothing business has perfectly smooth (and worthless) results. "If somebody starts talking to you about beta … zip up your pocketbook." *Munger:* "how can a professoriate that is so smart come up with such silly ideas and spread them all over the country?" See [risk-vs-volatility](../concepts/risk-vs-volatility.md).

**★ A22 · "Cost of capital" is opportunity cost, not CAPM.** *Munger:* theorists "went bonkers," concluding that "if you earned a hundred percent on capital … your cost of capital was a hundred percent … the companies that made the most money had the highest costs of capital" — "inanity." "What is Berkshire's cost of capital? We have this damn capital. It just keeps multiplying." The real test is opportunity cost: compare to "the best alternative investment we have at that time." *Buffett:* in practice "the cost of capital is about a quarter percent below the return promised by any deal that the CEO wants to do." His three capital questions: pay out or retain (can a retained dollar create more than a dollar?); if paying out, buyback or dividend; if retaining, deploy at the lowest business risk. See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md).

**A23 · You know a "big idea" when you see it — with a prepared mind.** *Buffett:* maybe 25 big ideas each over a career, "one every year or two"; he kept the Moody's Xeroxes from 50 years ago "because it was so obvious," knew GEICO was big after four hours with Lorimer Davidson in 1951, knew Western Insurance Securities was big. "My idea of a truly big idea is … I call Charlie and he only says no, rather than, 'That's the worst idea I've ever heard.'" *Munger:* "opportunity comes to the prepared mind … I don't think you can teach people in two minutes how to have a prepared mind." See [concentration-vs-diversification](../concepts/concentration-vs-diversification.md), [lorimer-davidson](../people/lorimer-davidson.md).

**★ A24 · Scale retailers are attacking brand-name moats.** *Buffett:* Kellogg and Campbell lost ground partly by "pushing their pricing too far" (cereal) and shifting lifestyles (soup); Coke's per-capita has risen "virtually every year" for 110 years with almost no real price inflation since 1930. But "there will be a battle, always, between brands and retailers, because the retailer would like his name to be the brand." *Munger:* "the buying power got more concentrated and tougher … add the Walmarts and the Costcos" — Costco even got "Hanes … to allow a co-branded … Hanes-dash-Kirkland" pantyhose, which "wouldn't have happened 20 years ago." See [moat](../concepts/moat.md), [franchise-vs-business](../concepts/franchise-vs-business.md).

**★ A25 · Short selling is tempting but "very painful."** *Buffett:* you see "way more stocks that are dramatically overvalued … than … dramatically undervalued," yet shorting faces unlimited losses, and overvalued promoters can "bootstrap value into the business" by issuing stock — "you can run out of money before the promoter runs out of ideas." Even when "the batting average is very high" eventually, "it was a whole lot easier to make money on the long side." Tells the Northern Pacific Corner (170 to 1,000 in a day; the Newark brewer who "jumped into a vat of hot beer and died" over a margin call). *Munger:* it "just isn't worth it to have that much irritation in your life. It isn't that hard to make money somewhere else with less irritation." See [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md), [arbitrage](../concepts/arbitrage.md).

**★ A26 · The "loyalty effect" — Berkshire won't trade Coke for Pepsi.** *Buffett:* even if convinced another consumer company would grow cash faster, "I don't even think about doing what you're suggesting"; controlled businesses "just aren't for sale … a fancy price will not tempt us" (exceptions: permanent cash loss or intractable labor). "I wouldn't dream of trading around businesses so that my estate was 105 percent of some very large number instead of 100 percent." Board seats also forfeit "an enormous amount of investment mobility." *Munger:* "the loyalty effect is a plus in our life," though "not nearly as important" in public stocks as in private companies. See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md), [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**★ A27 · Succession: assets that run themselves, plus a named allocator.** *Buffett:* asks each manager every two years, in writing, "If you die tonight, what will I wish you had told me tomorrow morning?" He and Munger "know who will succeed me in … two jobs, one marketable securities and one business operations"; the culture is "so strong … it'd be very hard to change it," and his stock ownership can prevent any change. The 1991 Salomon stint — "nine months and four days" away — proved "everything went on just as before." *Munger:* "the main defense … is to have assets that will do well, more or less, automatically. And we have a lot of those" — though successors won't be "as good as Warren at … allocating the money." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**A28 · Won't forecast commodities.** *Munger:* "the short answer is we don't want to talk about silver." *Buffett:* won't forecast oil or any price; "the equilibrium price … is whatever it's selling for today," but it'll be different next year and "we can't tell you what it'll be." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**A29 · A power system needs a margin of safety in capacity.** *Munger:* "the most important thing with a power system is to have a surplus of capacity … that margin of safety is enormously important," like building a bridge for far more than the maximum load. *Buffett:* California's deregulation forced utilities to sell generation "at multiples of book value" to deregulated generators who now "have an interest in having too little supply" — so the old regulated system, which paid a fair return for keeping ~15–20% spare capacity, "made more societal sense." (Folds A35: PUHCA repeal odds are higher now because "politicians do not like to face major brownouts" and the industry needs capital.) See [margin-of-safety](../concepts/margin-of-safety.md), [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md).

**★ A30 · A great business rarely fixes bad management — better to leave.** *Buffett:* persuading "decent, intelligent people … doing unintelligent things … to change their course of action has been poor." *Munger:* "Worse than poor." *Buffett:* if owners of a good business "keep doing dumb things with your money, you'll probably do better to get out and get in with people who've got a good business and … will do sensible things," because "they didn't rise to become the CEO … to have some shareholder tell them their most recent idea is dumb." See [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md), [turnarounds-seldom-turn](../concepts/turnarounds-seldom-turn.md).

**A32 · Berkshire won't issue valuation guidance.** *Buffett:* "It would be a mistake … a big mistake … to ever recommend buying or selling the stock"; the goal is for the stock to "track its intrinsic value" so each holder's experience matches the business's progress. The only implicit signal is a repurchase, which would never be done above intrinsic value. The biggest judgment in valuing Berkshire is "what do we do with the money, as it comes in?" *Munger:* "averaged out, it's worked pretty well." See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md), [intrinsic-value-vs-book-value](../concepts/intrinsic-value-vs-book-value.md).

**A33 · Graham's "cigar butt" net-nets are gone — and the culture changed.** *Buffett:* sub-working-capital stocks are "almost impossible to find now"; if they reappeared "you'd probably find wonderful businesses selling a lot cheaper, too," and "our inclination would be to go with a cheap, wonderful business." Praises Walter Schloss's "absolutely sensational" 46-year record in "cigar butt companies … one free puff and that's about it." *Munger:* "the whole culture has changed" — when a business stops working, "somebody else owns a lot of the working capital" (French workers, Marks & Spencer); "There're a lot of reasons why the investment idiosyncrasies of one era don't translate … into another." See [cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses](../concepts/cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses.md), [walter-schloss](../people/walter-schloss.md).

**A34 · Reading recommendations.** *Munger:* Matt Ridley's *Genome* ("a perfectly amazing book") and Herb Simon's *Models of My Life*. *Buffett:* Katharine Graham's *Personal History* — "an honest story." Both note Janet Lowe's biography of Munger. See [kay-graham](../people/kay-graham.md).

## Connections to other sources

- The risk-as-permanent-loss and anti-beta argument (A20) is the same demolition Buffett delivered in [berkshire-1994-meeting](berkshire-1994-meeting.md) (M50), now compressed to "zip up your pocketbook" with See's seasonality as the proof case.
- The cost-of-capital answer (A22) extends the "money costs money" capital-charge mechanics of [berkshire-1995-meeting](berkshire-1995-meeting.md) (M7) — there the discipline taught managers, here it is turned on the academics' CAPM.
- The moat discussion (A13, A24) is the contested sequel to the moat-and-castle metaphor of [berkshire-1995-meeting](berkshire-1995-meeting.md) (M25): Munger's "old moats getting filled in" and the Costco/Walmart brand erosion show the castle under siege, validating his 1995 warning that "most moats aren't worth a damn."
- "No such thing as growth stocks or value stocks" (M13) is the spoken companion to the growth-as-value argument running through the [berkshire-2000-letter](berkshire-2000-letter.md), with the same See's-vs-airlines contrast.
- The derivatives warning (A14) and the Freddie/Fannie exit (A12) are the live, pre-crisis seeds later formalized in the [berkshire-2002-letter](berkshire-2002-letter.md) ("financial weapons of mass destruction") and vindicated by 2008 — supersession in reverse, where the meeting's caution proved prescient.
- The General Re "true synergy" optimism (A5) is contradicted within months by the [berkshire-2001-letter](berkshire-2001-letter.md)'s post-9/11 "Noah rule" reckoning on mispriced terrorism aggregation — a clean example of a live answer overtaken by events.
- The reduce-your-expectations theme (M18, M32, M33) develops the equity-return math of Buffett's 1999 Fortune article and the [berkshire-1999-letter](berkshire-1999-letter.md), here sharpened by Munger's Rembrandt analogy and the pension-fund critique.
