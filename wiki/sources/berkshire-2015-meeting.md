# Berkshire Hathaway 2015 Annual Meeting

Held on May 2, 2015, at the CenturyLink Center in Omaha — the Golden Anniversary meeting marking 50 years since Buffett took control of Berkshire — the 2015 meeting stands out for its unusual concentration of adversarial material. Three live controversies framed the day: the Seattle Times and BuzzFeed investigation alleging predatory lending at Clayton Homes; shareholder anger at Berkshire's deepening partnership with 3G Capital's cost-cutting machine; and the loss of American Express's 16-year co-brand agreement with Costco, affecting one in ten AmEx cards in circulation. Buffett's extended, data-dense defense of Clayton's mortgage model — the longest single answer in this transcript — produced the meeting's clearest articulation of the aligned-interest lending principle. Beyond the controversies, the 50th-anniversary lens unlocked unusually candid retrospective content: Buffett's confession that the entire Berkshire insurance empire traces to three strokes of luck he could not replicate; a Munger meditation on rationality as a moral duty rather than an investment technique; and a frank Teledyne post-mortem showing how clever incentives became too strong and culture went off the rails. This page keeps the wiki's two-layer convention: a distilled thematic top, then a comprehensive ★-marked Q&A digest as the home for detail.

## How to read the Q&A digest

## Key themes

### Skin in the game vs. originate-and-sell: what aligned mortgage lending looks like

The predatory-lending allegations against Clayton Homes gave Buffett the occasion to articulate the structural principle that was, in his telling, absent from nearly every other part of the 2008 housing collapse: the mortgage originator and the mortgage holder were the same party. Clayton retained roughly $12 billion of mortgages on 300,000 homes, absorbing 100% of any loss when a loan defaults — "a net loss to us. It is a net loss to the customer." He compared this to the reform discussions about requiring originators to retain even 3% of the mortgage. Clayton was keeping 100% in roughly half its cases. The transfer: in any advisory or lending relationship, search for whether the party giving advice bears the same consequences as the party taking it. Where they diverge — the financial advisor paid on AUM, the mortgage banker paid on origination volume, the investment banker paid on deal close — expect drift toward self-interest. See [lending-discipline](../concepts/lending-discipline.md). *(M9)*

### Three lucky breaks and the meta-lesson: keep yourself open to good accidents

Asked whether he could recreate Berkshire's insurance success from scratch, Buffett catalogued three "extraordinary pieces of luck" without which the entire edifice collapses: a 20-year-old showing up unannounced on a Saturday and getting four hours with Lorimer Davidson, who explained insurance in a way no business school could match; Jack Ringwalt's once-a-year, five-minute selling mood coinciding exactly with Buffett's readiness in 1967 — "we not only couldn't have done it a day later, we couldn't have done it an hour later"; and Ajit Jain walking into Buffett's office in the mid-1980s having never worked in insurance. The meta-lesson is not fatalism but positioning: "if you keep yourself open to having good accidents happen, and kind of get past the bad accidents, you know, some good things will happen." Munger's gloss at the meeting: Buffett wasn't merely lucky to visit GEICO at 20 — not every 20-year-old goes to Washington and knocks on doors of empty buildings because they're curious. The luck required a prepared mind that kept showing up. See [float](../concepts/float.md), [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md). *(M15)*

### Hidden incentives: how decent people misbehave without financial gain

The Teledyne discussion surfaced Munger's sharpest warning about incentive design: Singleton's performance culture was so intense that three different Defense Department divisions ended up in scandals — not because Singleton was corrupt, but because the incentives pushed people past the ethical line without any financial motive. Buffett then produced a more intimate example from National Indemnity in the late 1960s: Jack Ringwalt would half-jokingly berate his claims manager every time a claim came in ("how could you do this to me?") — the man couldn't bear it, so he started hiding claims in a drawer. Zero financial incentive; pure ego-protection. The policy implication is direct: never publish earnings forecasts, because if you tell managers you need to hit X, you create pressure to fudge reserves, load up channels at quarter-end, or otherwise distort numbers — "not to profit anybody financially, but merely because they don't want to make the CEO look bad." See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md). *(M31)*

### Brands as the best inflation hedge: one-time outlays that compound in nominal terms

The classic inflation question ("which Berkshire businesses do best?") produced the meeting's clearest statement of the brand-as-inflation-hedge principle. The best businesses during inflation are "the businesses that you buy once and then you don't have to keep making capital investments subsequently." A brand impression is the ultimate example: Gillette spent $100,000 for all radio rights to the 1939 World Series, buying impressions on millions of minds in 1939 dollars — and harvested them selling razors in 1960 and 1970 and 1980 dollars. See's Candies built its brand decades ago; the inflation raises its replacement cost without requiring See's to spend another dollar. The anti-pattern is a capital-intensive business like a railroad or utility, which must keep reinvesting at inflated costs — "just keeps eating up more and more money, and your depreciation charges are inadequate and you're kidding yourself as to your real economic profits." Munger's coda: "We don't want inflation because it's good for See's Candy" — if it ever goes completely out of control, nobody wins. See [economic-goodwill](../concepts/economic-goodwill.md), [bonds-and-inflation](../concepts/bonds-and-inflation.md). *(A2)*

### Fees kill returns: the 7-year verdict on the hedge fund bet

At the seven-year mark of the decade-long bet (made in 2008), the Vanguard S&P 500 fund had returned a cumulative 63.5%; the basket of five fund-of-funds hedge funds had returned 19%. Buffett's diagnosis: "the hedge funds haven't done bad. It's the investors in the hedge funds have paid a very big price." The fund managers, if handling $1 billion at 2-and-20, collected $20 million per year just for showing up, regardless of performance. This is the most data-rich single snapshot in the meeting corpus of the fee drag argument — and it compounds: the 44-point gap after seven years is not volatility but structural extraction. See [investment-friction](../concepts/investment-friction.md), [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md). *(M41)*

### Rationality as moral duty: Munger's deepest self-description

Asked "what matters to you most?" at the 50th anniversary meeting, Munger gave his most explicit statement of personal philosophy. Rationality was not a technique for amassing wealth but a moral principle — "rationality is a moral duty." His grandfather (Judge Munger, whose name he bears) treated rationality as a foundational virtue. The corollary that follows: "I think if you have some easily removable ignorance and keep it, it's dishonorable. I don't think it's just a mistake or a lack of diligence. I think it's dishonorable to stay stupider than you have to be." Berkshire, in Munger's description, is "a temple of rationality — what's really admired around Berkshire is somebody who sees it the way it is." This frame resolves why the culture is so hard to replicate: most organizations reward people who make the boss look good; Berkshire rewards people who accurately perceive reality. See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md). *(A34)*

### Market valuation in context: interest rates are the denominator

Presented with the market-cap-to-GNP ratio at 125% (back to roughly its 1999 level) and corporate profits as a percentage of GDP at 10.5% (well above the historical 4–6.5% range), Buffett declined to issue a verdict without the context of interest rates. "If we get back to what are normal interest rates, stocks at these prices will look pretty high. If we continue with these kinds of interest rates, stocks will look very cheap." The profit-to-GDP figure was a concern for "other segments of society" (i.e., workers) rather than for investors, since it showed American business prospering at record levels. The meta-lesson: any absolute valuation signal requires an interest-rate input — valuations are comparisons between a current price and a stream of future cash at a discount rate. Strip out the rate and the signal is undetermined. See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [bonds-and-inflation](../concepts/bonds-and-inflation.md). *(M20)*

## Q&A digest — Morning session

**★ M9 · Clayton Homes: defending the aligned-interest mortgage model.** *Buffett:* responds to a shareholder distressed by the Seattle Times predatory-lending investigation. The core defense: Clayton retains roughly $12 billion of mortgages on 300,000 homes, bearing losses on any default just as the homeowner does — "we have no interest in selling anybody a house, and having that mortgage default, because it is a net loss to us. It is a net loss to the customer." He points to a factual error in the article — the journalist conflated gross profit margin (20%) with net margin (3%), the same error as claiming Macy's 40% gross margin is its profit. 91 state examinations in three years; largest single fine of $5,500. Default rate of ~3% annually attributable primarily to job loss, death, and divorce. *Munger:* "we can't make lending to poor people who buy houses 100 percent successful for everybody. We wouldn't be running the business right if the foreclosure rate was zero." *Supersession/context note:* subsequent regulatory scrutiny and reporting lent partial support to concerns the 2015 investigation raised — including alleged pricing disparities for minority borrowers — so Buffett's aligned-interest defense should not be read as the last word on Clayton's lending practices. See [clayton-homes](../entities/clayton-homes.md), [lending-discipline](../concepts/lending-discipline.md).

**M10 · 3G Capital: efficiency is not cruelty.** *Buffett:* declines to endorse running businesses with more people than needed — "I hope our Berkshire companies are not being run with more people than they need, either." Notes that U.S. railroads went from 1.6 million employees in 1947 to under 200,000 today, carrying more freight in safer conditions. *Munger:* the alternative "is what eventually happened in Russia — they pretend to pay us and we pretend to work." The principle: right-sizing is not anti-social; chronic overstaffing produces bad economics for everyone.

**M11 · Van Tuyl auto dealerships: a local business with no scale advantage.** *Buffett:* acquired Van Tuyl Group (now Berkshire Hathaway Automotive) because running dealerships well is a genuinely good local business, not because of national economies of scale. "I don't think you widen profit margins, particularly, by having a thousand dealers versus having a hundred." Won't pursue the finance business — Wells Fargo's cost of funds (12 basis points) is structurally lower than Berkshire's. Will buy more dealerships based on local quality, not roll-up arithmetic. *Munger:* Van Tuyl's meritocracy (the right people get power and ownership) is like Kiewit — "kissing cousins."

**M12 · No universal formula for predicting earnings ten years out.** *Munger:* "We don't have a one-size-fits-all system for buying businesses. They're all different, every industry is different, and we also keep learning." *Buffett:* most mental work in evaluating a business is filtering — "the filters are there, and there are a lot of things that, if we see it in a business…will stop us from going on to the next layer." The skill is not a checklist but the accumulation of judgment about which things matter in a specific industry.

**M13 · Munger supports IBM stake.** *Munger:* did not try to talk Buffett out of IBM. Notes IBM's successful adaptation from punched-card dominance to electronic computing — "it's very rare that when a technological change comes along that people adapt as successfully as IBM did that time." Considers it a credible company bought at a reasonable price. *Buffett:* "When we bought it, it was a two-to-nothing vote." *Supersession flag:* Buffett sold virtually all of Berkshire's IBM shares in 2017, telling CNBC he had reassessed his views on the competitive position. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ M14 · Don't talk up your investments — buyers want the stock cheap.** *Buffett:* points out that Berkshire and all four of its largest holdings are buying back stock, which means they benefit from the stock being cheap, not dear. "If we talked our book, from our standpoint, we would say pessimistic things about all four of the biggest holdings we have, because all four of them are repurchasing their shares." *Munger:* "Warren, if people weren't so often wrong, we wouldn't be so rich." The broader lesson: the entire Wall Street assumption that an investor should want their holdings to go up as soon as possible is wrong for any long-term buyer. See [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md), [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**★ M15 · Three extraordinary pieces of luck — and how to position for good accidents.** *Buffett:* the Berkshire insurance success traces to three specific strokes of luck: Lorimer Davidson spending four hours explaining insurance to a 20-year-old stranger on a Saturday; Jack Ringwalt's rare five-minute selling mood coinciding with Buffett's availability in 1967 ("we couldn't have done it a day later, we couldn't have done it an hour later"); and Ajit Jain walking into the office in the mid-1980s having never worked in insurance. The trifecta cannot be replicated. But the meta-lesson: "if you keep yourself open to having good accidents happen, and kind of get past the bad accidents, you know, some good things will happen." *Munger:* "Warren says he was lucky to go to GEICO, but not every 20-year-old was going down to Washington, D.C., and knocking on the doors of empty buildings to try and find something out that he was curious about." See [lorimer-davidson](../people/lorimer-davidson.md), [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md), [float](../concepts/float.md).

**M16 · Berkshire culture runs deep — self-reinforcing and institutionalized.** *Buffett:* illustrates the reach of Berkshire's culture with Mrs. Louis in Germany, who wanted to sell her life's work to Berkshire specifically: "that would not have been the case 30 or 40 years ago." Culture must come from the top, be consistent, be lived, be rewarded when followed and punished when not. "People who join us believe in it; people who shun us don't believe in it, so we — it's self-reinforcing." When Charlie and I are gone, the culture being institutional (not personality-based) will make it clearer that it will persist. *Munger:* "It's what we kept learning that made it work, and I don't think that will ever stop." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**M17 · Coca-Cola moat and health concerns about sugar.** *Buffett:* 1.9 billion eight-ounce Coca-Cola product servings consumed in the world daily; expects more Coca-Cola cases consumed in 20 years than today. Notes Fortune ran an article in the late 1930s saying Coca-Cola growth was over, and 1988 buyers were also unexcited. "I sit here as somebody who, for the last 30 years, one-quarter of all the calories I've consumed come from Coca-Cola." *Munger:* "Sugar is an enormously helpful substance. It prevents premature softening of the arteries." The broader point: durable consumer preferences in staples are remarkably sticky; new health anxieties recur across decades without dislodging the underlying habit. See [coca-cola](../entities/coca-cola.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**M18 · Van Tuyl follow-up: why auto dealers are a good local business.** *Buffett:* confirms no interest in the finance component (Wells Fargo has structural funding-cost advantage). The value is in locally run, locally trusted operations: "we will hope that they're regarded by the people who buy cars as a local business, not some part of some giant operation." Multiples moved up after the Van Tuyl acquisition, so subsequent deals require price discipline. Prefers buying at 10–12× a bad year's earnings over 8× a good year's — "because if we're going to be in the car business forever, we're going to have some good years and some bad years."

**★ M20 · Market valuation indicators: interest rates are the critical context.** *Buffett:* the market-cap-to-GNP ratio at 125% (back to approximately its 1999 level) and corporate profits at 10.5% of GDP (above the historical 4–6.5% range) are real signals — but both must be read against the interest-rate environment. "If we get back to what are normal interest rates, stocks at these prices will look pretty high. If we continue with these kinds of interest rates, stocks will look very cheap." High corporate profits as a share of GDP are a concern for workers, not for investors — they show American business prospering. And Berkshire itself has never made an acquisition or turned one down based on macro factors: "we know we don't know what that's going to look like… but it doesn't really make any difference if we're buying a business to hold for a hundred years." *Munger:* "Since we failed to predict what happened, and what exists now, why would anybody ask us what our prediction is in the future?" Adds: "We think any company that has an economist, you know, certainly, has one employee too many." See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [bonds-and-inflation](../concepts/bonds-and-inflation.md).

**M21/M23 · BNSF crude-by-rail safety and Marmon tank cars.** *Buffett:* new DOT rules on crude-by-rail safety (released two days prior to the meeting, 300 pages) cover thicker tanks, better fire protection, electronically controlled brakes, speed limits in urban areas. Berkshire's interests are mixed: the railroad wants safer transport; Marmon's Union Tank Car business will benefit from retrofitting demand. Bakken crude turns out to be closer to condensate — more volatile than traditional crude, hence more dangerous. BNSF leads the industry in safety. Berkshire's reinsurance division offered $5–6 billion of coverage to the four major railroads; railroads declined the price. *Munger:* "you'd be out of your mind to own these big companies and not run them with big attention to safety." See [bnsf](../entities/bnsf.md), [super-cat-insurance](../concepts/super-cat-insurance.md).

**M24 · Consolidating insurance float at National Indemnity.** *Buffett:* the series of intercompany cessions (50% of GEICO's book up to National Indemnity; similar moves with MedPro and GUARD) have no strategic mastermind — "just got around to it." The motivation: National Indemnity holds Berkshire's vast surplus capital, and managing many small insurance pockets is less efficient than concentrating at the parent. "Our general approach is just to keep every place loaded with more capital than anybody could possibly conceive of us needing." See [national-indemnity](../entities/national-indemnity.md), [float](../concepts/float.md).

**M25 · Dollar as world reserve currency in 50 years.** *Buffett:* would bet a lot of money on the dollar remaining the world's reserve currency 50 years from now. *Munger:* "I'm probably more nervous than a lot of people about printing a lot of money and spending it. There are times when you have to do it, I'm sure. But I'm happier when we print money and use it improving infrastructure than I am when we just spread it around with a helicopter."

**M26 · Using the Berkshire Hathaway name for subsidiaries.** *Buffett:* authorized Berkshire Hathaway HomeServices (the real-estate franchise replacing the Prudential franchise) and Berkshire Hathaway Automotive Group after Van Tuyl. The condition: if the name gets abused, it gets yanked. A useful discipline — bad behavior reaches headquarters faster when the brand is at stake. *Munger:* "We'd be crazy to try and sell Berkshire Hathaway peanut brittle instead of See's." See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**M27 · MidAmerican wind power: 58% of Iowa generation — structural advantage from consolidated tax return.** *Buffett + Greg Abel:* MidAmerican (now Berkshire Hathaway Energy) announced its tenth Iowa wind project, bringing installed capacity to over 4,000 megawatts; by end of 2016, approximately 58% of the energy delivered to Iowa customers will come from wind. $18 billion committed to renewable assets across utilities. Additional projects in Nebraska (400 MW, the first there) and Nevada (retiring 76% of NV Energy's coal by 2019). Key structural advantage: BHE's tax credits (1.9¢/kWh for 10 years) are usable because it sits inside Berkshire's consolidated tax return — "there's really no one situated as well as MidAmerican Energy is…to really put its foot to the floor in terms of developing wind and solar." *Munger:* "It's not a threat, it's a huge benefit to humanity, and I think it will be a huge benefit to Berkshire." See [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md), [greg-abel](../people/greg-abel.md).

**★ M28 · Dexter Shoe: the double mistake — bad business bought with stock.** *Buffett:* the most memorable failure was Dexter Shoe — "I looked at a shoe business in Dexter, Maine, and decided to pay 400-or-so million dollars for something that was destined to go to zero in a few years, and I didn't figure that out." Compounded by paying in stock: "I guess that stock would be worth, I don't know, maybe 6 or 7 billion now. It makes me feel better when the stock goes down because the stupidity gets reduced." The generalized rule: "almost any time we've issued shares, it's been a mistake." *Munger:* "Of course. We don't do it much anymore." See [dexter-shoe](../entities/dexter-shoe.md), [stock-as-acquisition-currency](../concepts/stock-as-acquisition-currency.md).

**★ M29 · Surprised by low inflation despite unprecedented monetary expansion.** *Buffett:* candidly wrong on the 2008-era inflation prediction ("I've been very wrong"). Could not have predicted six years of near-zero rates, negative rates in Europe, a $4 trillion Fed balance sheet, and no inflation. The saving grace: "we're always prepared for anything… We will be very willing to act if economic turbulence of any kind occurs, and we'll be prepared and most people won't be." Cash position at $60+ billion. *Munger:* "We've made very little progress in life by trying to outguess these macroeconomic factors. We basically have abdicated. We're just swimming all the time, and we let the tide take care of itself." See [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md), [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

**M30 · Deferred taxes are not a hidden form of equity.** *Buffett:* total deferred tax liability is roughly $60 billion; the $37 billion from accelerated depreciation on fixed assets is not "hidden float." In the utility business, regulatory commissions factor the tax deferral into allowed returns — it helps customers, not shareholders. The railroad's surface transportation board handles it similarly. A meaningful scenario: if the 35% corporate rate were reduced, the deferred liability would shrink as a book entry — but that change wouldn't affect real economics. *Munger:* "it would be a book entry. It wouldn't mean much." See [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md), [float](../concepts/float.md).

**★ M31 · Teledyne's lesson: hidden incentives and ego-driven misbehavior.** *Munger:* Singleton was a genius (800 on every test; chess near grandmaster level) but his performance culture was so intense that three Defense Department divisions ended in scandal — not from greed but from culture overriding ethics. Buffett then offers the National Indemnity case: Jack Ringwalt's affectionate berating of his claims manager whenever a loss came in caused the man, without any financial incentive, to hide claims in a drawer. "And that caused us to not only misreport fairly minor figures, but it also caused us to misinform our reinsurers." The policy application: "If you tell your managers you never want to disappoint Wall Street, and you want to report X per share, you may find that they start fudging figures to protect your predictions." Berkshire never issues earnings guidance for exactly this reason. *Munger:* "at the end, he had three different Defense Departments that got into scandals. He wasn't doing anything wrong. He wasn't trying to hurt the Defense Department on purpose. But the incentives got so strong." See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md), [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

**M32 · Berkshire is not a SIFI — and Dodd-Frank weakened the Fed's crisis tools.** *Buffett:* Berkshire doesn't remotely qualify as a systemically important financial institution (SIFI) under the 85%-financial-revenues test (Berkshire is ~20%); has not been approached by regulators. The more important point: Dodd-Frank weakened the Fed's and Treasury's power to do "whatever it takes" during a panic — the very power that stopped a run on $3.5 trillion of money market funds in September 2008. "When you have a panic, you have to have someone, somewhere, who can say and be correctly believed, that he or she will do whatever it takes. I think Dodd-Frank weakens that, and I think that's a terrible thing to weaken." *Munger:* derivatives trading in banks "is a lot like running a bucket shop in the '20s or a gambling parlor in the current era… mostly nonsense." See [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md).

**M33 · Experimenting with online workers' comp insurance.** *Buffett:* launched BHDWC.com as a direct online workers' comp product — "we believe in experimenting at Berkshire." Progressive showed channel conflict between direct writing and agents is manageable. GEICO's distribution evolution (direct mail → TV → phone → cable → Internet → mobile) illustrates the principle: "what wins 20 or 30 or 50 years from now" is whatever saves people money and gives good service most efficiently. See [geico](../entities/geico.md), [direct-distribution-moat](../concepts/direct-distribution-moat.md).

**M35 · Berkshire vs. 3G: the 25-person home office is the proof.** *Buffett:* the premise that Berkshire tolerates excess headcount is mostly wrong — GEICO with 33,000 employees is run as efficiently as 3G would run it; the home office has 25 people when it could have 500. Where Berkshire does tolerate some excess, it reflects trust in a manager Buffett thinks highly of — "I basically tolerate it where I've got a manager that I think well of." The newspapers have had to cut back as revenue has shrunk; the textile business cut back systematically before final closure. "I think better of the 3G way of operation than I do of our operators where they really have excess people in it. We've got very few of those, but we do have a few." *Munger:* "Charlie's office in Los Angeles that has two, counting him." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md).

**★ M36 · Great brands survive retailer pressure — nourish them or lose them.** *Buffett:* when Sam Walton sent a first six-pack of Sam's Cola 20 years prior, it looked threatening. Walmart has enormous power, and so does Coca-Cola. Heinz Ketchup holds 60% U.S. brand share and higher in many foreign markets. Gillette has 70% by dollar value of the global razor blade market after 100 years. "A strong brand is really potent stuff." But the brand has to "stand for the promise that's in people's mind about them." You can never coast — "you can't afford to be complacent about a brand." The pattern: brands don't die from head-on attack by retailers; they die from internal neglect or from the brand-holder prioritizing short-term volume over the brand's integrity promise. See [moat](../concepts/moat.md), [sees-candies](../entities/sees-candies.md), [gillette](../entities/gillette.md), [coca-cola](../entities/coca-cola.md).

**★ M37 · Value investing principles transfer across all markets and borders.** *Buffett:* asked about value investing in China (where the stock market had doubled in six months): "I certainly think investment principles do not stop at borders." In any market, apply the same three principles from Graham: think of stocks as small pieces of businesses; treat market fluctuations as opportunities rather than threats; focus attention on businesses where competitive advantage is intelligible and the 5–10-year picture is reasonable to assess. *Munger:* "How can there be anything else that makes any sense except value investing? Who in the hell doesn't want value when you buy something?" And: "It never gets that popular though. People are looking for an easier way. And that's a mistake. It looks easier, but, in fact, it's harder." *Buffett:* markets with more speculation create better opportunities for the disciplined investor — "if you're a young investor and you can sort of stand back and value stocks as businesses and invest when things are very cheap, no matter what anybody is saying on television… it is really not a very tough intellectual game. It's an easy game, if you can control your emotions." See [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md), [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

**M39 · CEO-CIO succession: operational experience cannot be bypassed.** *Buffett:* a chief investment officer running Berkshire is "very unlikely" without significant operating experience alongside the investment track record. "I've said that I'm a better investment manager because I've been an operating manager, and I'm a better operating manager because I've been the investment manager… Operations — I've learned a lot through operations by being in operations." *Munger:* the owned and controlled businesses grow more important each year relative to the marketable securities portfolio — "it would be crazy not to go with the tide to some extent." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**★ M40 · Weschler and Combs: trustworthiness is more important than the brains.** *Buffett:* both Todd Combs and Ted Weschler were selected not primarily on track record but on character. "We have seen dozens and dozens of investment managers with great records over the years… there are plenty of investment managers on Wall Street with great records that don't fit [our character] bill." Both are expanding beyond securities: Combs worked on Charter Brokerage and a Phillips acquisition; Weschler went to Germany twice to close the Louis deal. *Munger:* "that trustworthiness is more important than the brains. It's not that they don't have the brains, but we wouldn't hire anybody, no matter how able, if we didn't trust them." The anti-pattern from Salomon: brilliant people who self-destructed making money they didn't need. See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md), [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**★ M41 · Hedge fund bet update: S&P 63.5% vs. hedge funds 19% after seven years.** *Buffett:* at the seven-year mark of the ten-year bet, the Vanguard S&P 500 fund had compounded to a cumulative 63.5% return; the basket of five fund-of-funds hedge funds stood at 19%. "The hedge funds haven't done bad. It's the investors in the hedge funds have paid a very big price." Fund managers handling $1 billion at 2-and-20 collect $20 million a year just for showing up. The original zero-coupon bonds (each party bought ~$350,000 worth, to mature to $500,000) were converted to Berkshire stock a few years prior — so the winner will receive "appreciably more than a million dollars." See [investment-friction](../concepts/investment-friction.md), [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md).

## Q&A digest — Afternoon session

**★ A2 · Which Berkshire businesses do best in high inflation — and why.** *Buffett:* the best businesses in inflation are those where you "buy once and then don't have to keep making capital investments subsequently." A brand built 50 years ago — See's, Gillette's 1939 World Series radio impressions ($100,000 in 1939 dollars, yielding sales in 1960–1980 dollars) — is an asset whose replacement cost inflates without requiring another outlay. Real estate bought decades ago has the same quality. The worst businesses in inflation: capital-intensive ones (utilities, railroads) where depreciation charges become inadequate and the real earnings are overstated. "Any business with heavy capital investment tends to be a poor business to be in in inflation and often it's a poor business to be in generally." *Munger:* "We don't want inflation because it's good for See's Candy… If it weren't for the Weimar inflation, we might never have had Adolf Hitler." See [economic-goodwill](../concepts/economic-goodwill.md), [bonds-and-inflation](../concepts/bonds-and-inflation.md).

**A3 · Commercial insurance organic growth vs. acquisition.** *Buffett:* almost certain Berkshire will not acquire a large commercial insurer — they already have the ideal operation in Berkshire Hathaway Specialty Insurance, with Ajit overseeing it and more capital than any commercial insurance company in the world. Building it at book value avoids paying a nondeductible acquisition premium and avoids inheriting culture problems. "We've actually made money while we're in the building stage." *Munger:* "I certainly agree with you." See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md), [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**★ A4 · Reputation is built slowly and is the reputation you deserve.** *Munger:* very few people have an overnight great reputation (Lindbergh). Most must earn one slowly over time, and opportunities compound on top of it: "I see, all the time, opportunities come to people where it's the credibility they've gotten in the past that causes them to have the new opportunity." *Buffett:* cites Gianni Agnelli: "When you get old, you'll have the reputation you deserve. For a while you can fool people, but when you get to be my age, whatever reputation you have, it's probably the one you deserve." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**★ A5 · Climate change and one-year insurance pricing: "I do not want them thinking about global warming."** *Buffett:* global warming may be real and important, but it should not change an underwriter's one-year price on a one-year policy. "Would you change the rate for tomorrow on insurance because of global warming? I doubt it very much." A 50-year wind-storm policy in Florida would be a different story. The annual pricing system means Berkshire can respond to any change in risk evidence in the next renewal cycle. The practical instruction to underwriters: "I want them thinking about whether there's a moral risk involved and who owns the property. I mean, that can be very significant" — illustrated by the fictional Marvin the Torch: "I don't burn buildings; I create vacant lots." *Munger:* "A lot of people get very invested. It's like a crazy ideology. It's not that global warming isn't happening. It's just that you can get so excited about it you make all kinds of crazy extrapolations that aren't necessarily correct." See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**A6 · Oil & gas: poor track record, but Berkshire Hathaway Energy is different.** *Buffett:* ConocoPhillips (bought near the 2008 oil price peak, later written down) and ExxonMobil (bought 2013, sold by the time oil fell 2014) were not distinguished investments. "We have not distinguished ourselves, at all, in the oil and gas field." But BHE is fundamentally different — it owns regulated utilities and renewable generation, not commodity extraction. Will continue to back Greg Abel in buying utility properties. *Munger:* ExxonMobil's dividend, given interest rates, made it "not a bad cash substitute." See [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md).

**A9 · Subsidiaries and short-term vs. long-term management.** *Buffett:* does not see evidence that formerly public companies (converted to Berkshire subsidiaries) are more short-term oriented than those that were always private. The real distinction is consistent tone from the top: "We believe in sort of hammering the same message out there over and over again." Monthly financials on virtually every business are read carefully — "I don't think they're unimportant, but we don't live by them." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**A11 · Why GEICO can't always quote the lowest rate — and why that's fine.** *Buffett:* GEICO wins the business about 40% of the time when someone calls for a quote; competitors win 60%. Different underwriting weightings produce different rates for different risk profiles. With only 11% of the private-passenger auto market, there is enormous room to grow — even if some people get better rates elsewhere. "I don't think any company, of size, will be the lowest more often than GEICO." *Munger:* older people not deteriorating as fast as their cohort "may be paying an unfair price for the insurance, but it's a good tradeoff." See [geico](../entities/geico.md), [direct-distribution-moat](../concepts/direct-distribution-moat.md).

**★ A12 · Reinsurance facades: alternative capital is degrading the market.** *Buffett:* the reinsurance market has structurally worsened and "it's unlikely to be as good as it was." Two forces: (1) pension funds buying reinsurance as a "noncorrelated asset class" without understanding it; (2) hedge funds using reinsurance as a "beard" — domicile offshore in Bermuda, run a hedge fund, use a reinsurance entity as the nominal business. "When you get a whole lot of people that are bringing money in and they sort of need your facade of reinsurance to cover up what their real motivations are, you're likely to get less attractive prices in reinsurance." Berkshire's response: write only at prices where the odds favor an underwriting profit; let volume fall dramatically if needed — "our standby costs aren't huge." One niche still belongs to Berkshire: of the eight reinsurance contracts written globally with premiums of $1 billion or more, Berkshire has written all eight. *Munger:* "We're playing the game for the long pull and other people just pretend to be doing so." See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md), [float](../concepts/float.md).

**A14 · NetJets: not a mistake, despite the pilot contract dispute.** *Buffett:* NetJets is a "very decent business" — the aircraft-order overhang is not a capital risk because planes are sold to fractional owners, not held on Berkshire's balance sheet. Berkshire has had only three strikes in 50 years (across hundreds of unions), all brief. Average pilot pay of $145,000 per year; seven days on/seven days off; training exceeds competitor standards. "We have no anti-union agenda whatsoever." *Munger:* has never met a NetJets pilot who wasn't "a wonderful fellow and a very skilled, able, and dutiful, reliable person." See [netjets](../entities/netjets.md).

**A15 · Duracell-P&G deal: tax exchange, declining business, still a good outcome.** *Buffett:* the exchange of Berkshire's low-basis P&G shares for Duracell was structured as a tax-efficient exchange (analogous to a real-estate 1031 exchange) — neither Berkshire nor P&G would have done the deal without those tax benefits. Duracell is a declining business (alkaline batteries losing relevance to lithium and rechargeable) but "it will be around for a very, very, very long time on a worldwide basis," and Duracell's brand position is strong. "I'm looking forward to getting the deal complete." See [gillette](../entities/gillette.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**A16 · Why Buffett favors individual over corporate philanthropy.** *Buffett:* "I am much more of a believer in individual philanthropy than corporate philanthropy… I really feel that I've got everything I need, but I do also feel that I'm working for the shareholders and they should determine their own philanthropic activities — it's their money." Won't write checks to his alma mater from company funds. Describes his own surplus: "I have never given a penny to any organization that has cost me anything in my life… Everything I've given has been ungodly surplus." *Munger:* "My taste for giving away somebody else's money is also quite restrained." The principle: CEOs who use corporate funds for personal philanthropic preferences are spending shareholder money on their own preferences. See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**A18 · Household formation: probably cyclical, not structurally lower.** *Buffett:* the subdued post-crisis household formation rate (which benefits Berkshire through ACME, Johns Manville, Benjamin Moore, MiTek, Shaw, BNSF) is more likely cyclical than a permanent shift toward European-style multi-generational living. Jobs becoming more plentiful should eventually convert young adults out of parents' homes. His earlier timeline was wrong. "Your guess would be as good as mine." *Munger:* "I think I speak for a lot of members of the audience when I say I have some grandchildren that I wish would marry somebody suitable promptly."

**★ A21 · GEICO-Van Tuyl synergies are "a very dumb idea."** *Munger:* flatly rejects the suggestion to put a GEICO gecko on every Van Tuyl salesperson's desk. "It's a very dumb idea, and we're not going to do it." *Buffett:* GEICO is a low-cost model; adding dealership distribution channels would add cost, and historically, selling auto insurance through dealerships "hasn't been particularly effective." The broader principle: paper synergies touted by investment bankers almost never survive contact with operational reality. Two separate businesses with separate cost structures do better apart. See [geico](../entities/geico.md), [investment-friction](../concepts/investment-friction.md).

**★ A23 · Defending against activism: performance is the best defense.** *Buffett:* the best defense is performance. But notes that activist money has poured in because it's been easy to raise — now activists are "reaching" on the kinds of companies they target and the claims they make. The logic on buybacks: "you repurchase them if you're taking care of the needs of the business and your stock is selling for less than it's intrinsically worth. That — I don't see how anything could be more simple." Activists often push companies to buy back stock that is overpriced (the empirical pattern: buybacks collapse when stocks are cheap and surge when stocks are dear). Berkshire would love to buy its own stock "by the bushel basket at 120 percent of book, because we know it's worth a lot more than that." *Munger:* "I don't think it's a great age, this age of activism. It's hard for me to think of any activists I want to marry into the family." See [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md), [intrinsic-value-vs-book-value](../concepts/intrinsic-value-vs-book-value.md).

**★ A24 · American Express: a history of adaptation, and the Costco loss.** *Buffett:* AmEx began moving trunks and valuables; pivoted to traveler's checks as railroads displaced express; pivoted again to the Travel and Entertainment card when Diner's Club emerged. Crucially, when Diner's Club priced its card at $3, AmEx priced at $5 and thereby created a better image: "People that pulled out their American Express card at a dinner table, they looked like J.P. Morgan." Under CEO Ken Chenault, AmEx has been "very nimble and very smart" in anticipating more recent threats. The Costco loss (one in ten cards, confirmed in 2015) is significant, but "I probably shouldn't get into the specifics of Costco. I've got a Costco director sitting next to me." Happy to own 15% of the company; would be even happier if the stock goes lower so AmEx's $4–5 billion annual buyback retires more shares. *Munger:* "I like it a little better when they had a little less competition, but that's life." *Supersession note:* AmEx eventually partnered with Citibank as the Costco co-brand replacement; the company's earnings did recover, validating Buffett's thesis. See [american-express](../entities/american-express.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**A26 · Debt level and float permanence: no plans to deleverage.** *Buffett:* Berkshire holds $60+ billion in cash; "would rather be sitting with 20 billion and made a great $40 billion acquisition." The float is "essentially very close to permanent" — it can decline a couple percent in a year or increase a few percent, but there is no material drain in sight. Euro bonds could be issued at roughly 1%, which 50 years ago would have seemed impossible and would have changed their capital structure decisions entirely. Not going to deleverage; might take on a little more debt for a really large deal. *Munger:* "We'd love to have something come along where we actually felt a little capital constrained. We haven't felt capital constrained for a long time." See [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md), [float](../concepts/float.md).

**A30 · The college earnings premium: a "ridiculous" and "fraudulent" statistic.** *Munger:* the standard argument that college-educated people earn more is confounded by selection — "maybe they do better because they were better to start with before they ever went to college, and they never tell you that." *Buffett:* "I think that's one of the silliest statistics they publish… you're talking about two different universes… to attribute the entire difference to the one variable." Munger adds that during the Great Recession, every successful university behaved exactly like 3G — laid off lots of people and worked better after. "I don't think there's a college in America that wants to go back to its old habits." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**A32 · Early investing process: just kept reading and went with instincts.** *Buffett:* no elaborate planning; looked for businesses where the 5–10–15-year picture seemed reasonable to assess; stayed away from things requiring too many decimal places of precision. In those days, capital-constrained (had to sell something to buy something) — "always an interesting challenge, when you're measuring something you hold against something that has come along." *Munger:* "We made some of our luck by being curious and seeking wisdom… and there's nothing that produces wisdom more thoroughly than really getting your own nose whacked hard when you make a mistake." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ A34 · Munger: rationality as a moral duty.** *Munger:* asked what matters most, gives the meeting's most philosophical answer. His grandfather Judge Munger, his father, and he all treated rationality as a foundational virtue — not merely a technique. "Rationality was just totally worshiped by Judge Munger." The moral corollary: "I think if you have some easily removable ignorance and keep it, it's dishonorable. I don't think it's just a mistake or a lack of diligence. I think it's dishonorable to stay stupider than you have to be." Berkshire is "a temple of rationality — what's really admired around Berkshire is somebody who sees it the way it is." He connects this to Confucius and notes that generosity follows naturally: "we're a social animal, and we're tied to other people." *Buffett:* what matters most to him is that Berkshire does well — not the stock price but the underlying enterprise building every year. "The only thing that would keep me up at night" is actually damaging Berkshire's long-term value. See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**A36 · Investing as a game — and Munger's caveat about "merely" buying stocks.** *Buffett:* early success combined three elements: "a great teacher, exceptional focus, and the right sort of emotional qualities." Investing is "not like Henry Singleton's questions… it's actually a pretty easy game." The focus was total: went through every manual, loved every minute. *Munger:* "I don't like being too much an example to people who want to get rich by being shrewd and buying and passively holding securities. I don't think that's enough of a life… If you wrest a fortune from life by being shrewder than other people and buying little pieces of paper, I don't think that's an adequate contribution in exchange for what you're taking." Running Berkshire — building something real — is the higher calling. See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**★ A37 · Dow Jones: a dominant position squandered through lack of imagination.** *Buffett:* Dow Jones in the 1960s and '70s "owned the field" — the news ticker, the Journal, a trusted name in every brokerage firm — at the onset of the greatest expansion in financial information demand in history. They bought a chain of small newspapers, missed the financial data terminal opportunity, and let Bloomberg take the institutional financial-data market. "They couldn't have been in a better place. They couldn't have started with a stronger position. They had a very good balance sheet. And they just let the world pass them by." The structural failure: a family controlled by a lawyer drawing dividends, with "nobody there with any imagination as to what could be done in the financial field." The lesson: a dominant competitive position is necessary but not sufficient; it requires managers with imagination to extend it into adjacent opportunities before others do. *Munger:* "They did end up with 6 or $7 billion, so they may have blown their opportunities, but they didn't destroy their fortune." See [moat](../concepts/moat.md), [media-economics](../concepts/media-economics.md).

## Connections to other sources

- The Clayton Homes defense (M9) is the first extended discussion of lending discipline in the meeting corpus, and the clearest articulation of why originate-and-hold creates different incentives than originate-and-sell. It connects to the 2008 and 2009 meetings where the housing crisis post-mortem focused on securitization abstraction rather than on specific company defenses.
- The three lucky breaks in insurance (M15) is the retrospective companion to the insurance history Buffett has told across multiple letters — here compressed into a 50th-anniversary confession of irreplicability. It connects to the 2013 meeting's "institutional franchise" argument (M25 in [berkshire-2013-meeting](berkshire-2013-meeting.md)), which argues the franchise outlasts the individual; this meeting shows how fragile the founding was.
- The Teledyne incentive post-mortem (M31) extends and sharpens the 2013 meeting's Teledyne discussion ([berkshire-2013-meeting](berkshire-2013-meeting.md) M34), where Munger criticized Singleton's stock-issuance phase and praised his buybacks. The 2015 version adds the DoD scandal dimension and the Ringwalt claims-hider as a zero-financial-incentive example — making the ego-protection mechanism explicit.
- The market valuation discussion (M20) is the 2015 update of the interest-rate-as-gravity metaphor from [berkshire-2013-meeting](berkshire-2013-meeting.md) M21. In 2013, the metaphor was about asset prices rising when rates are low; in 2015, Buffett extends it to say whether the current 125% market-cap-to-GNP ratio is alarming depends entirely on whether rates normalize — an explicitly conditional claim rather than a verdict.
- The hedge fund bet update (M41) is the year-7 data point in a series that runs from [berkshire-2012-meeting](berkshire-2012-meeting.md) onward; the 44-point cumulative gap (63.5% vs. 19%) by 2015 provides the empirical spine for the passive-investing and investment-friction concepts.
- The brand-as-inflation-hedge theme (A2) is the meeting-floor companion to the economic goodwill concept developed in Berkshire letters across the 1980s — here Buffett restates it through the Gillette 1939 World Series example and connects it explicitly to the question of which businesses to own going into an inflationary period, a question the letters answered at the level of principle.
- The Dow Jones case study (A37) mirrors the Dexter Shoe lesson (M28): in both cases, what looked like a strong position was destroyed not by an external enemy but by a combination of structural failure (wrong managers, wrong incentives) and failure to see what was coming. Together they make the point that a moat requires imagination to maintain, not just a dominant starting position.
