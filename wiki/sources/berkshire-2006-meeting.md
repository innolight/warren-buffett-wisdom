# Berkshire Hathaway 2006 Annual Meeting

The 2006 meeting opens with a theatrical flourish — the ISCAR acquisition signed the night before, announced to 24,000 shareholders assembled in Omaha — and sustains that energy through a session of unusually concrete investment wisdom. Three threads distinguish the year. First, ISCAR exemplifies the entire Berkshire acquisition philosophy in a single anecdote: no investment bankers on either side, an owner who refused to auction, a page-and-a-half letter that brought Buffett and the Wertheimer family together. Second, the housing and credit analysis (M17) is among the most prescient in the meeting series: Munger explicitly names the manufactured-housing credit hangover as a template for what is building in stick-built mortgages — eighteen months before the 2008 crisis arrived. Third, the investment-management profession receives its sharpest critique yet: $140 billion in annual fees for an activity that "in aggregate cannot work out for investors," with Buffett willing to make a named bet. The meeting also contains Buffett's most candid admission that he failed to sell Coca-Cola at a "silly" 50× price, a complete diagnosis of the dollar's structural decline, and a chilling replay — via Salomon 1991 — of how close a derivatives cascade came to "absolute chaos." Note: Buffett announced his Gates Foundation giving pledge in June 2006, just after this meeting; that event is not discussed here.

## How to read the Q&A digest

- **M#/A#** map to the numbered sections in the raw morning/afternoon transcript for traceability.

## Key themes

### The filter that selects for great partners: ISCAR and the no-auction principle

Eitan Wertheimer sent a page-and-a-half letter about an Israeli carbide-cutting-tool company Buffett had never heard of. "Character and talents sort of just jumped off the page at me." No investment banker on either side — a fact Buffett underlines by noting that a $4 billion deal got announced with no mention of an advisor. The explanation for why this happens is the 2006 meeting's most durable acquisition principle: "the people that pass through that filter of caring enough about their business that they don't simply put it up like a piece of meat at an auction are also the people, in our view, that make the best managers and make the best partners over time. There is something going on in their brain that says this business is so important, and the people that are here are so important… that we actually care about the home in which these businesses reside." The afternoon session makes the contrast explicit: "a strategic buyer is some guy that pays too much"; private equity runs businesses from Fund A to Fund B to Fund C, each taking a 20% slice; investment-banking projections four years out Buffett would bet against every time. See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

### Speculative dynamics: there are no clocks on the wall

The Cinderella metaphor reaches its definitive form in M15: "the punch bowl is flowing, the dance partners are getting prettier, you know at midnight it turns to pumpkins and mice — but there are no clocks on the wall." Applied to copper, oil, and Internet stocks alike: fundamentals move the price first, then "speculation takes over and speculative participation can become dominant." The quote from "Adam Smith" (Jerry Goodman): in these markets, there are no clocks on the wall. The housing analysis goes further — M17 and M19 together constitute a nearly complete diagnosis of the coming 2007-08 crisis, written eighteen months early. Manufactured housing had already lived through exactly this sequence: easy credit, securitization, a retailer selling to anyone with a pulse, and a hangover that took years to clear. Munger names the pattern in M17: "Some of the sin that was in the manufactured housing finance a few years ago has shifted into the finance of the stick-built houses. There is a lot of ridiculous credit being extended in America in the housing field, and my guess is there will be some trouble in the stick-built sector in due course." *Supersession: Munger's prediction was confirmed by the 2008–09 mortgage crisis.* See [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

### Media economics: when competition dissolves a franchise

There were once three television "highways" between Procter & Gamble and several hundred million eyeballs, "and those three highways could make a lot of money when there were only three highways. But you keep building more ways… and you decrease the value of the highways." Newspapers went from what Buffett calls "the ultimate bulletproof franchise" — where owning a monopoly paper was the safest investment imaginable — to a business whose virtuous cycle is running in reverse: fewer readers → weaker advertising argument → fewer readers. "When they take people out to the cemetery, they're taking newspaper readers. When people graduate from school, they're not gaining newspaper readers." The World Book, selling 300,000 sets a year at $600 in the mid-1980s, collapsed because the internet provided a cheaper substitute — "not that the product we have isn't worth the money; it's that people have lots of other alternatives." Munger's summary: "It will be a rare business that doesn't have a way worse future than it had a past." And the precise analogy: "If the technology had not changed, they'd still be impregnable franchises. Fortunately, carbide cutting tools appear to have no good substitute." See [media-economics](../concepts/media-economics.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

### Underwriting under genuine uncertainty: models are "silly"

Post-Katrina, the core question in catastrophe insurance is unanswerable: is the last 100 years or the last 2 years the relevant actuarial base? "We know certain atmospheric conditions have changed. We know water temperature has changed. But we do not know all the variables that go into the propensity of hurricanes to occur." Buffett is flat: "We don't believe in modelers at all. I read all this stuff about modeling. It's silly. The modelers don't know a thing, in my view." Munger adds the thermodynamics logic: warming oceans create "more high-energy uproar in weather" and anyone who writes weather-related insurance ignoring global warming is "out of our minds." The equation is non-linear: "1 percent or 2 percent changes in something can produce 100 percent of probabilistic changes in cost. It's an explosive sort of equation." The discipline throughout: price to what you think exposure is, not to what experience has been. And Berkshire's structural advantage: "We're willing to lose many billions of dollars in a given catastrophe if we think we've been paid appropriately for it." See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md), [super-cat-insurance](../concepts/super-cat-insurance.md).

### The market serves you; it does not instruct you

Graham's Chapter 8 of *The Intelligent Investor* is cited explicitly in A12 and A15: "the market is there to serve you and not to instruct you. That's of enormous importance." The investment process that follows: identify things that are important AND knowable; if something is important but unknowable, forget it; if it is knowable but unimportant, move on. Momentum, charting, trend-following — all treat the market as instructor rather than servant. "If the price is out of line where the facts and reasoning lead you, then action is called for. If it doesn't, you forget it and go play bridge that day." The emotional corollary, from A15: "You can make a lot of money on Sunday. Things are really screwed up if you're getting calls on Sunday. All you have to do is make sure you're the callee and not the caller." In 2002 junk bonds, Korean stocks at 3× earnings a few years earlier — the issue was never about IQ or information. "They were just paralyzed for one reason or another." See [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md), [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md), [margin-of-safety](../concepts/margin-of-safety.md).

### Investment friction: the self-neutralizing profession

The critique in M29 is the most systematic Buffett has given at an annual meeting. The investment profession generates "about $140 billion a year" in fees for activity that "in aggregate can only accomplish what somebody can do in ten minutes a year by themselves." He names a specific bet: a significant sum against any ten hedge funds ≥$500 million beating the S&P after fees over ten years — "gets away from the survivorship bias." The 2-and-20 structure lets managers capture upside, fold bad partnerships, reopen as new ones. The dynamic makes the profession unique: "if you're going to have a baby you're better off calling an obstetrician; most professions have value added above what the laymen can accomplish. In aggregate, the investment profession does not do that." Munger adds the comparison to a casino: "the proprietors of many private partnerships are taking a house edge that looks a lot like the rake of the croupier in Monte Carlo, except it's bigger." And on state pension fund entertainment: "I think it ought to be a crime." See [investment-friction](../concepts/investment-friction.md), [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md).

## Q&A digest — Morning session

**M4 · Q1 earnings and insurance seasonality.** *Buffett:* GEICO had excellent growth. Gen Re had a good quarter. But caution: under standard accounting, a one-year hurricane policy earns one-quarter of premium per quarter — entirely pro-rata — while the real exposure concentrates in Q3. "You can't take our insurance underwriting results in any way for a benign quarter like Q1 and extrapolate them for the year." Pro-rata accounting mismatches true risk distribution. See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**★ M5 · ISCAR acquisition.** *Buffett:* $4 billion for 80% of ISCAR (Israeli cutting-tool maker, part of the IMC Group, the world's second-largest in its category). Family retains 20%. Announced the day before the meeting; Berkshire's first acquisition based outside the United States. Originated from a page-and-a-half letter from Eitan Wertheimer. No investment banker on either side. "It's interesting. In this world, in which many businesses get auctioned off, figures get dressed up before they sell them and leveraged up and so on, we continue to hear from people periodically who consider their business as too important to auction." *Munger:* "This is a company that, from very modest beginnings, grows to be the best company in its field in the world. The average quality of the people in this company is not only extraordinary, it's off the chart." See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), [berkshire-hathaway](../entities/berkshire-hathaway.md).

**M8 · Cyclical compensation: pay for what is under management control.** *Buffett:* If you own a copper mining company, measure management by cost of production per unit, not by whether copper is $2 or $3.50 a pound. GEICO's two unit measures: unit growth, and profitability of seasoned business (new business costs money; don't charge that against the manager). "We do not want to pay for anything that is not under their control. We do not want to pay for the wrong things." No compensation consultants in 40 years; never lost a manager over comp differences. *Munger:* "In that frenzy of envy which characterizes compensation in investment banking, Warren remonstrated, softly, I thought, towards a slightly more rational result, and he was outvoted." Envy, not greed, drives comp escalation: "You can hand somebody a $2 million bonus, and they're fine until they find out the person next to them got $2.000001 million." See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).

**★ M9 · Culture trains managers — and ensures succession.** *Buffett:* "I think, actually, in reading that letter — that's part of the reason it's written — to convey, not only to our partners, but also to our managers and anybody else, what Berkshire is all about." Culture is the training: written word, this meeting, the annual letter — all consistent signals. "There aren't many mistakes in terms of people entering [the culture] or behaving inconsistently with it." Three obvious CEO candidates exist; the board knows which one right now. A new CEO will go through a "media probation-type affair for a year or two," but "they won't mess with the culture — they're too smart." *Munger:* "If Warren has kept the faith until he's 75 maintaining a certain kind of culture, do you really think he's going to blow the job of passing the faith on?" See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**M10 · Closed-end fund premiums.** *Buffett:* Paying 120% of NAV for a closed-end fund when you can buy the same assets through an open-end fund at NAV "would require convincing me very strongly that the management of the closed-end fund was better." History: "overwhelmingly, closed-end funds have gone to discounts." Overseas Securities paid a 30-40% premium for years and "baffled everybody." Eventually they come back to earth. *Munger:* Nothing to add.

**★ M11 · Corporate boards must think like owners; large shareholders are the real cure.** *Buffett:* Three board jobs: (1) get the right CEO; (2) prevent that CEO from overreaching; (3) exercise independent judgment on important acquisitions. "CEOs — even smart CEOs — are frequently motivated in acquisitions by other than rational reasons." On governance fashions (majority voting, checklists, grades): "It isn't going to do anything in terms of making American business work any better." The only cure: "If the eight or ten largest shareholder groups — the really large institutional investors — said, 'This compensation plan doesn't make any sense and we're not voting for the directors,' you'd get change. But so far, they've been unwilling to do that." Some have even "farmed out their voting." See [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md), [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).

**★ M12 · Tech remains in the "too hard" pile.** *Buffett:* On IBM, Sun, Oracle, Dell, Intel: "I know enough to know that I don't know enough to make an investment decision." Charlie's three boxes: in, out, too hard. "A lot of things end up in the too-hard pile, and it doesn't bother us at all." Tom Watson Sr.: "I'm no genius, but I'm smart in spots and I stay around those spots." The virtue of staying inside the circle: ISCAR's fundamentals "won't change. The way the people think won't change." Contrast with telecom, which changed beyond recognition in 15 years. *Munger:* A foreign correspondent observed they didn't seem smart enough to do so much better than others, and asked for the explanation. "We know the edge of our competency better than most people do. And I always say it's not a competency if you don't know the edge of it." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

*(Skipped: M7 Social Security / entitlement spending; M13 tax policy and income inequality; M14 ethanol economics — all primarily social-policy debates outside investment-wisdom scope. Munger's thermodynamics remark on ethanol is noted in underwriting themes above.)*

**★ M15 · Commodity speculation: no clocks on the wall.** *Buffett:* Every commodity cycle begins with fundamentals, then "speculation takes over and can become dominant as time goes by." The classic sequence: "what the wise man does in the beginning, the fool does in the end." The Cinderella metaphor: "the punch bowl is flowing, the dance partners are getting prettier, you know at midnight it turns to pumpkins and mice — but there are no clocks on the wall. And in the markets — copper now, Internet stocks in 1999, uranium stocks in the 1950s — there are no clocks on the wall." Silver: "I was early in that conclusion, and early in selling. Other than that, everything I did was perfect." *Munger:* "We've demonstrated how little we know about commodity prices by our very skillful operations in silver." See [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

**M16 · International investing: Brazil not off limits.** *Buffett:* Berkshire needs to deploy "hundreds and hundreds of millions of dollars at minimum" in any security — a constraint that narrows the field in small economies. PetroChina: $400M invested (now worth ~$2B) illustrates the scale problem even in China's largest company. "Brazil would not be off limits at all, but we'd have to be able to get a lot of money into a business we understood at an attractive price… We would want it to be cheaper than if it were in the United States." *Munger:* No more.

**★ M17 · Clayton homes, credit abuse, and the coming stick-built reckoning.** *Buffett:* Manufactured housing volumes are at their lowest since the mid-1960s — stuck near 130,000 units — because the industry abused credit five years ago: no-doc loans, any down payment, immediate securitization, losses borne by distant investors. Clayton far outperformed every competitor. The logic: manufactured homes should be financed on shorter terms (they're personal property when not on owned land); extending 30-year terms was "a mistake." *Munger:* "Some of the sin that was in the manufactured housing finance a few years ago has shifted into the finance of the stick-built houses. There is a lot of ridiculous credit being extended in America in the housing field, and — my guess is there will be some trouble in the stick-built sector in due course." *Buffett:* "Dumb lending always has its consequences and usually on a big scale, but you don't see it for quite a while. It's like a disease that doesn't manifest itself for a few weeks, and by the time you know you have an epidemic, you're very well into it." *Munger:* "And some of this dumb lending is being facilitated by contemptible accounting." *Supersession: Munger's forecast was confirmed by the 2008–09 mortgage crisis.* See [clayton-homes](../entities/clayton-homes.md), [lending-discipline](../concepts/lending-discipline.md).

**M18 · Russia: property rights are not a given.** *Buffett:* Berkshire inherited a Siberian oil business through Salomon. "As long as we were drilling, we were welcome. When we wanted to start taking the oil out, after our money had been used to drill the holes, they weren't quite as friendly." Met Mikhail Khodorkovsky at Sun Valley in 2003; Khodorkovsky was jailed shortly after; Yukos was taken through tax claims. "I think it's a little hard to develop a lot of confidence that the world has changed permanently there in terms of its attitude toward capital, and particularly toward outside capital." *Munger:* "If they ever do find any oil, that old man will steal it." See [salomon](../entities/salomon.md).

**★ M19 · Real estate bubble: the speculative-purchase tell.** *Buffett:* Berkshire's residential brokerage shows a slowdown everywhere, sharpest in markets with high speculative/investment purchases. The key diagnostic: "when you have investment-type holdings, speculation-type holdings, when you have the day traders of the Internet move into the day trading of condos, then you get a market that can move in a big way." Dade and Broward County example: December 2004 — under 9,000 condos listed, 2,900 sold per month. Now: 30,000 listed ($15B worth), under 2,000 sold per month. "Very likely, you can get real discontinuities in a market like that, where all of a sudden people realize the whole supply-demand situation has changed." Primary-home buyers with jobs tend to stay through a modest price decline; speculative holders won't. "I would be surprised if there aren't some significant downward adjustments from the peak, particularly in the higher-end properties." *Munger:* Omaha housing prices are "quite reasonable" — the country is not all the same. *Supersession: The subsequent 2007–11 housing decline, centered on speculative and high-end markets, matched this diagnosis.* See [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

*(Skipped: M20 meeting attendance and Furniture Mart sales — logistics/celebration.)*

**★ M21 · Excess cash is a failure — but dumb deals are worse.** *Buffett:* Normal Berkshire cash level is about $10 billion ("we don't scrape the bottom, but we don't need 40 billion"). With $37B+ at the time, "we would be much happier if we had 10 billion of cash and all the balance in things we liked very much." The priority ordering: "We don't like having excess cash around. We like even less doing dumb deals because we do them forever. If we make a dumb deal, it just sits there. We don't resell it three months later by having an IPO of it." ISCAR = $4B well-deployed. Another potential deal of "as much as $15 billion" was in view (low probability). *Munger:* Go back to the annual report of ten years ago and compare — "despite the great difficulties of deploying cash, we managed to put an awful lot of wonderful stuff into Berkshire in the last ten years." See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md).

**★ M22 · Should have sold Coca-Cola at the "silly" price.** *Buffett:* Coca-Cola earns "100 percent pretax on tangible assets" — $5-6B of tangible assets generating a similar amount pretax. Growing unit volumes every year since 1886. "It's a really wonderful business that sold at a very silly price some years back. And you can definitely fault me for not selling the stock. I always thought it was a wonderful business, but clearly, at 50 times earnings, it was a silly price on the stock." In 1997-98, the stock traded above $80 on ~$1.50 per-share earnings. By 2006, earnings had grown to ~$2.17 but the stock was far below its peak. Will own it in ten years. *Munger:* No more. *Supersession: Coke did not return to its 1998 nominal high until the early 2020s, though per-share intrinsic value compounded steadily; Berkshire's low tax cost basis made selling costly regardless.* See [coca-cola](../entities/coca-cola.md), [mistakes-of-omission](../concepts/mistakes-of-omission.md).

**★ M23 · Reinsurance: pricing under non-stationary uncertainty.** *Buffett:* Marine/Gulf Coast: ~$2.5B of premium against ~$15B of losses in two years. Prices are hardening, but "what we don't know is whether exposures are up even more." The unanswerable question: "You can take the hundred-year experience and it tells you one thing, and you can take the last couple years and it tells you something else. Which is more meaningful? We don't know." Berkshire can absorb a $250B insured loss (Katrina was ~$60B); Berkshire's share would be ~$10B — "a very large percent of the industry would be in very, very serious trouble." The asymmetric survivorship pitch to buyers: "everybody knows we can pay." On models: "I read all this stuff about modeling. The modelers don't know a thing, in my view. We get paid for making guesses. If, over a lifetime, the guesses are decent, we will know that we were doing the right thing." *Munger:* "Why shouldn't we use our capital strength to get into volatile stuff that makes other people frightened?" See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md), [super-cat-insurance](../concepts/super-cat-insurance.md), [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md).

**M24 · NetJets: candid on scale diseconomies.** *Buffett:* NetJets has the world's only true worldwide fractional-jet service and the strongest position in large aircraft. "But I'd have to tell you that I did not anticipate — I thought we would have economies of scale, to some degree, and so far you can almost argue that we've had diseconomies of scale." Expenses got "out of hand" in 2005; he was "dead wrong" on his profitability forecast. Rich Santulli is "the only person in the world I would have run that" — 16 hours a day, loves the business. "I think we will be profitable before long, but you should take my prediction there with a certain amount of skepticism until it actually happens because I've been wrong." *Munger:* "The product integrity is so extreme between FlightSafety and NetJets. That system is very obsessive about product integrity and my guess is that obsession, in due course, will be rewarded." *Supersession: NetJets turned profitable in subsequent years; by the 2010s it was a consistent profit contributor.* See [netjets](../entities/netjets.md).

**M25 · Silver: bought fundamentally, sold early.** *Buffett:* The original thesis: silver production and reclamation ran roughly 100M oz/year below consumption; relatively few pure silver mines (mostly a copper/lead/zinc by-product). "I thought silver would get tight at some point. And I was very early in that conclusion, and early in selling." Now no silver. The anti-commodity lesson: "it's not like sitting with a stock where, in most cases, earnings are piling up for you. You have to hope that a commodity moves in price, because it is not producing anything as it sits there looking at you." *Munger:* "We didn't get where we are by owning noninterest-bearing commodities. It's a good habit to trumpet your failures and be quiet about your successes."

**M26 · "We don't play big trends."** *Buffett:* Responding to a macro investing question: "we really just zero in on whether people will keep eating candy and whether we can charge a little more for it next year. We don't play big trends. Big trends — they just don't mean that much. There's too much money to be made from year to year to think about things that take decades to manifest themselves." No purchase of a business, stock, junk bond, or currency has ever been based on a macro call. *Munger:* "Not only that, we've recently failed to profit much from one of the biggest commodity booms in history. And we'll probably continue to fail in the same way." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**M28 · Share repurchase philosophy: compelling AND announced.** *Buffett:* Berkshire has the lowest turnover of any major company — a reflection of genuine owner mentality (the Walton family concentration at Walmart shows it's not just about Buffett ownership). If shares get "really cheap," Berkshire will announce intent to buy — as it did four or five years earlier (announcement itself eliminated the opportunity, which is fine: "we are not looking to make money off of buying from shareholders at a depressed price"). In the modern era, most repurchases are motivated by fashion or stock-propping, not value. "Forty years ago it was a very fertile field to look at companies aggressively buying in their shares [at genuine discounts]. I would say that motivation has been swamped." *Munger:* "It used to be that almost every company that bought in shares was buying them in at an obvious bargain price. Now a lot of share buying is designed to prop the stock price. It's more like Sam Insull." See [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md), [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**★ M29 · Investment friction: the self-neutralizing profession.** *Buffett:* The investment profession is "hard to think of another business like that, Charlie": in aggregate, the professionals accomplish only what an individual could do in ten minutes a year by themselves. Estimated $140 billion a year in fees. "If you're going to have a baby, you're better off calling an obstetrician. Most professions have value added above what the laymen can accomplish. In aggregate, the investment profession does not do that." Specific bet: "I am willing to bet a significant sum to anybody who wants to name ten partnerships that are $500 million or more of management and pit those, after fees, against the S&P over a ten-year period." 2-and-20 structures let managers capture upside, fold bad ones, open new ones. On the perverse pricing dynamic: "the main thing that's been learned is that the more you charge, at least temporarily, the more money you bring in — people have this idea that price equals value." State pension fund entertainment: *Munger:* "I think it ought to be a crime to entertain, in any way, a state pension fund official, and I think it ought to be a crime, if you are a state pension fund official, to accept the entertainment." See [investment-friction](../concepts/investment-friction.md), [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md).

## Q&A digest — Afternoon session

**A2 · Business schools: improved from a very low base.** *Buffett:* Finance teaching has improved "quite a bit over 20 years ago, but that was from a very, very low base. The orthodoxy of 20 or so years ago where the flat Earth was being embraced has improved considerably." KU, Columbia, Missouri, Stanford cited. But the distribution of student ambitions is worrying: "Right now an awful lot of the students who come to visit say private equity or hedge fund. It's hard to imagine a world where everybody is running a hedge fund — not sure what we would do for food and clothing." *Munger:* "Something like half of the business school graduates in the elite eastern schools want to go into private equity or hedge funds, and those I bump into seem to judge their progress in life as to whether they're keeping up with their age cohort at Goldman Sachs. This can't possibly end well."

**★ A3 · CEO succession: cultural continuity requires patience, not structure.** *Buffett:* The successor "will go through a media probation-type affair for a year or so." Investment bankers will test whether the new person is "softer" on auctions. But the culture will be evident "after a year, two years, maybe." The structural temptation — chairman/CEO split, Buffett handling deals while a COO runs operations — won't work: "I'm not sure what a chief operating officer would do at Berkshire except expose the fact that I wasn't doing anything." After death, "it's going to be even stronger than before, because at that point people would realize it was institutionalized and not just a person." *Munger:* "Speaking for the Munger heirs, I would rather the current method of operation continue to wring the last drop of good out of Warren." **★ A6 · Media businesses: structural decline, not a valuation opportunity.** *Buffett:* "Generally speaking, the economics of media businesses do not have a great outlook." Three television highways to many: "Very few businesses get better because of more competition." Newspapers: "circulation will not be larger in five years, and advertising pages will not be greater." Current newspaper multiples "unattractively high if you would see some decline, like 5 or 6 percent a year on earnings, occurring." The additional risk: "perception lag" — owners keep projecting old trends, may use declining cash to buy other newspapers at prices that don't make sense. (A11 folded here: "When they take people out to the cemetery, they're taking newspaper readers. When people graduate from school, they're not gaining newspaper readers. The virtuous circle — everybody reads the paper because every ad is in it; every ad is in it because everybody reads the paper — that virtuous circle is going in the other direction now.") *Munger:* "It's simplicity itself. It will be a rare business that doesn't have a way worse future than it had a past." And on past confidence: "I once thought General Motors was a bulletproof franchise. But we have a wonderful way of coping with a lot of these things — we have this 'too hard' pile." See [media-economics](../concepts/media-economics.md), [franchise-vs-business](../concepts/franchise-vs-business.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**A7 · Dollar / current account: "significant consequences" are coming.** *Buffett:* "My views are strong as ever, perhaps a bit stronger." The structural argument: Greenspan in February 2002 called the current account deficit "invariably" trouble-inducing (it was then $385B; by 2006, more than double). Greenspan never repudiated the view, "just talked about other things more." The carry cost of currency futures positions has turned negative, so Berkshire is pivoting to earning power in non-dollar currencies (ISCAR's earnings are largely non-dollar). One likely consequence: "significantly higher inflation, because as you owe more and more as a country, it gets more tempting to devalue what you owe by paying in a cheaper currency." The 1987 portfolio-insurance analogy: small mechanistic programs, individually rational, became "a doomsday machine" when aggregated. (A22 folded here: the net debtor position has gone from $4.5T cumulative deficit to only $2.5T actual debt because US foreign-asset capital gains exceeded foreign-owned US-asset gains — but Buffett notes this gap reversed in the most recent quarter.) *Munger:* "Almost surely painfully" will there be a comeuppance, though great civilizations can sustain misbehavior longer than you'd expect.

**A8 · CPI has understated inflation.** *Buffett:* The index uses an imputed rental computation rather than actual housing costs, and rentals have "lagged significantly below what housing costs really are for an American family." Core CPI excluding food and energy "strikes me as pretty core to anything." Walmart and Costco LIFO adjustments are "just peanuts" — reflecting genuine price stability in mass merchandise — but steel, jewelry, and petrochemical-based carpet (Shaw Industries) have seen significant price increases. "For a typical young family, the CPI probably underestimates the burden they have faced in terms of their own living situation." *Munger:* Sees it at Costco — almost no inflation in their composite basket, yet dramatic rises in select categories. See [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md).

**★ A9 · Acquisitions: partners not deals, no bankers, no auctions.** *Buffett:* Business Wire: a letter from Cathy after Buffett read a Wall Street Journal article. Medical Protective: suggested to Jeff Immelt knowing GE wanted to rationalize insurance assets. PacifiCorp: originated with Dave Sokol and Scottish Power. Applied Underwriters: two founders who built it from nothing, keeping 19%. "The one thing we haven't done is we haven't participated in any auctions." On investment-banking books: "the projections are just plain silly… I would love to meet the people who write those books and make them a bet on the earnings they project four years out." Private equity "Fund A to Fund B to Fund C" — each taking a 20% slice — with the same pension funds possibly on both ends. "We will not get any sensible buys from the resellers." *Munger:* "The interesting thing is the mindset. With all these new helpers, they talk about doing deals. That is not the mindset at Berkshire. We are trying to welcome partners — a totally different mindset." His banker's quip: "'How does your firm make its money?' — 'Off the top, off the bottom, off both sides, and in the middle.'" See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), [dave-sokol](../people/dave-sokol.md).

**★ A10 · Salomon 1991: derivatives near-catastrophe and the role of reputation.** *Buffett:* In August 1991, on a Sunday, Berkshire/Salomon was within 30 minutes of filing for bankruptcy — "a law firm was drawing up the papers." At 10am the Treasury had issued what amounted to a death sentence; at 2:30pm Nicholas Brady reversed it. What would the Monday have looked like? A $6-700B derivative book (large for its era), security settlements due the next day, Gorbachev being spirited away simultaneously, the Dow opening down 200 points. "If you had superimposed upon that the fact that Salomon failed in Japan starting at 7 o'clock the previous night — if you were delivering securities to them against payment the next day, you weren't going to get paid. If you were expecting securities, you weren't going to get those securities." *Munger:* "It could have been absolute chaos. Nick Brady really prevented that bankruptcy — and he knew about Berkshire Hathaway from being a family shareholder with the Chaces way back. There was an element of personal reputation in the avoidance of finding out what would have happened that next day." *Buffett:* The potential for such cascades today is "probably magnified quite a bit" from 1991 levels. See [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md), [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md), [salomon](../entities/salomon.md).

**★ A12 · Fear vs. logic: the real constraint is emotional, not informational.** *Buffett:* Ben Graham's dictum: "you're neither right nor wrong because people agree with you or disagree with you. You're right because your facts and reasoning are right." The process: identify things important AND knowable; forget important-but-unknowable; filter out knowable-but-unimportant. "All you do is try to make sure that the facts you have are correct — and that's usually pretty easy in this country." Graham Chapter 8: "the market is there to serve you and not to instruct you." In 1974, in 2002 junk bonds, in Korean stocks at 3× earnings: "it wasn't that anybody didn't know they were cheap. They were just paralyzed for one reason or another. And the lesson of following logic rather than emotion is something that — it's obvious. And some people have great trouble with it, and others have less trouble." *Munger:* "When you have a huge convulsion, like a big fire in this auditorium right now — if you can be wise when everybody else is going crazy, sure, there will still be opportunities. But that may give you long, dull stretches, if that's your strategy." See [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md), [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

**★ A13 · Starting over with a small amount of capital.** *Buffett:* First partnership formed May 4, 1956 — fifty years and two days before this meeting — with $105,000. If doing it again with $1M: "we would look around the world… probably be doing something very similarly." Small capital = small stocks with an impact on a small portfolio. Three or four years earlier he would have put "100 percent in Korea" — companies at 3× earnings with strong balance sheets and industry positions. "You do not have to have tons of good ideas in this business — you just have a good idea that's worth a ton, occasionally." *Munger:* "Once you've found the best thing, then you measure everything against that because it's your opportunity cost. That's the way small sums of money should be invested. And the trick is getting enough expertise that your opportunity cost — meaning your default option, which is still pretty good — is very high." On modern portfolio theory: "that's why modern portfolio theory is so asinine." See [concentration-vs-diversification](../concepts/concentration-vs-diversification.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**A14 · Siegel's optimistic equity thesis.** *Munger:* On Jeremy Siegel's book arguing "the tried and true triumph over the bold and new": "I think he's demented. He's comparing apples against elephants and trying to make accurate projections." *Buffett:* "He's a very nice guy, Charlie." Brief exchange; no durable investment principle added.

**★ A15 · Sunday calls: the market serves, not instructs.** *Buffett:* "You can make a lot of money on Sunday. You may not get a chance very often, but any call you get on Sunday you're probably going to make money on. Things are really screwed up if you're getting calls on Sunday." The LTCM example: off-the-run Treasuries 30 basis points wide of on-the-run. "All you have to do is make sure that you're the callee and not the caller on Sunday. You never get in a position where the other fellow can call your tune. You have to be able to play out your hand under all circumstances. But if you can play out your hand, and you've got the right facts, and you reason by yourself, and you let the market serve you and not instruct you, you can't miss." *Munger:* "Well, I would say some of you probably can miss." See [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md), [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md).

**A16 · Reading the Berkshire annual report.** *Buffett:* "We try to put everything in that report that we would want to know if the positions were reversed — if I were sitting with all of my net worth in Berkshire and had been on a desert island for a year, and the manager was reporting to me about the business." Report it in a way that is understandable; don't drown people in unimportant information. Focus on: what kind of businesses do they have, what kind of managers are running them, what are they likely to earn over time, and what resources are available to add to the collection. "The real question of what Berkshire will be worth 10 years from now will depend on the annual earnings we've developed by that time, the quality of those earnings, the possibilities going forward from those businesses, and the liquid assets we have." *Munger:* Approach it by disposing of the "no-brainer decisions" first — the non-insurance operating businesses; then evaluate the insurance operations and the deployment of surplus cash. See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md), [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**★ A17 · Float, growth limits, and the art of catastrophe pricing.** *Buffett:* Float is at $48-49B — "close to 10 percent of the float of the entire American property-casualty insurance industry." Growth from here will be "very hard at big clip." The fundamental underwriting question: earthquake experience from 100-200 years "has more validity than the windstorm or hurricane experience of the last hundred years — I don't know that for sure, but I would bet real money that way." On the thermodynamics of cat pricing: "1 or 2 percent changes in something can produce 100 percent of probabilistic changes in cost. It's an explosive sort of equation." The right discipline: "We're willing to lose many billions in a given catastrophe if we think we've been paid appropriately for it. But it is not like figuring odds on flipping coins or rolling dice — you are dealing with changing variables." *Munger:* "If the oceans get warmer — I think they are getting warmer — the weather is going to have more high-energy uproar in it. The natural reaction is to raise your prices as the risks go up. And whether you've raised them enough, and carefully controlled your risks enough — that's the art of the business." See [float](../concepts/float.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md), [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md).

**A18 · Health care: too hard pile.** *Munger:* "That has tended to go into the too-hard pile at Berkshire." Watches ethics in the field as chairman of a large central-city hospital: "there's a lot of bad ethics in health care, and also a lot of good ethics. It's a very, very complex field with a lot of change and a lot of technological differences." *Buffett:* "We have not owned much in health care. My only expertise is in diet." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**A19 · Bankruptcy: value in complexity, but crowded now.** *Buffett:* Bought Fruit of the Loom bonds without expecting to end up with the company; Enron's "Ospreys" — a complicated embedded structure — produced more than a triple on a fair-sized position. Penn Central: the judge "just said, 'This is too damn complicated — I'm going to put this all together and give you a quick, fast solution.'" Smart, because "otherwise Penn Central might still be going." The lesson: "anytime there's something big and complicated, there's certainly a good chance of mispricing." But today "so many people are looking at bankruptcies… it does not have a lot of promise right now." *Munger:* Courts have entered a "bidding contest to attract bankruptcy business" by developing a culture of overpaying lawyers, trustees, and consultants. See [fruit-of-the-loom](../entities/fruit-of-the-loom.md).

**A20 · P&G-Gillette: "dream deal" in a world of retailer concentration.** *Buffett:* Called it a dream deal at announcement. The rationale: "the struggle between the manufacturers of brands and retailers will go on and become more intensified." Walmart, Costco, and global concentration mean manufacturers need to strengthen their hand. Gillette blades are "even stronger than most P&G brands" in competitive position. "The future of both is better as a combined enterprise." No view on the pharmaceutical piece — "I don't know a thing about that. I'd help if I could, but I can't." See [gillette](../entities/gillette.md).

**★ A21 · "A strategic buyer is some guy that pays too much."** *Buffett:* When a private-equity-owned business goes to auction seeking a "strategic buyer": "A strategic buyer is some guy that pays too much — because he wants to justify it, so he says it's strategic. I have never understood being a strategic buyer. Every time somebody calls me up and says, 'We think you're the logical strategic buyer for that,' I hang up faster than Charlie would." The structural problem: a financial buyer who bought "with the idea of 'how do I get out of this thing?' in mind from day one" manages differently than an owner who planned to run it for a hundred years. "Some of these things — Fund A is selling to Fund B to Fund C — I've seen some that have changed hands three or four times. They're marking them up, and everybody's getting 20 percent of the profit every time they move it from one place to another. And probably Fund C or D is owned by the same pension funds that own Fund A." *Munger:* "In the 1930s there was a stretch with certain kinds of real estate where you could borrow more against the real estate than you could sell it for. I think that's happened in some of these private equity deals. It's weird." See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**A23 · Insurance vs. gambling: the created-risk distinction.** *Buffett:* "Gambling involves creating risks that don't need to be created." A roulette ball is a created risk; a Florida coastal home's hurricane exposure is a pre-existing risk that just needs to be transferred. "Cat coverage transfers large existing risks as opposed to creating a risk that is not required." Insurance also ideally aggregates to reduce systemic risk. "I hope you're right that the house wins in both cases." *Munger:* The croupier frame applies well to much of the investment management industry — "the proprietors of the partnerships are taking a house edge that looks a lot like the rake of the croupier in Monte Carlo, except it's bigger." See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md), [investment-friction](../concepts/investment-friction.md).

**A24 · Short selling: correct intuitions, terrible economics.** *Buffett:* No ethical objection to short-selling — would love (if legal) to lend his stock to shorts and get paid. "There's nothing evil, per se, about selling things short." But: "it's a very, very tough way to make a living. A stock that you buy at 20 can go down 20 points. A stock you sell short at 20 can go up an infinite amount." Over a lifetime he has probably had a hundred correct short ideas and "I'll bet if I'd tried to do it and make money out of it, I probably would have lost money, had no fun, and the opportunity cost would have been enormous." *Munger:* "One of the most irritating experiences in the world — to figure out something is crooked and foolish and short it at X and have it go to 3X. Now you're watching all these happy crooks splashing around in your money while you're meeting margin calls." See [concentration-vs-diversification](../concepts/concentration-vs-diversification.md).

## Connections to other sources

- The ISCAR acquisition logic — no auctions, owner-sellers, the "filter" — is the 2006 practice-ground for principles developed conceptually across all the earlier letters; it gives [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md) its most vivid real-world case.
- The manufactured-housing credit-abuse diagnosis (M17) directly parallels Munger's 2002 observations about pre-Berkshire industry lending habits in [berkshire-2002-meeting](berkshire-2002-meeting.md), but goes further by predicting the stick-built extension, making it the earliest on-the-record forecast of the 2008 mortgage crisis in this series.
- The newspaper moat-decay discussion (A6) extends the secular decline Buffett first flagged in the [berkshire-1991-letter](berkshire-1991-letter.md) (newspaper as "preprint wrapper") and sharpens the Buffett/Munger view that technology can permanently destroy what was once impregnable — the inverse of the ISCAR durability thesis.
- The investment-friction critique (M29) is the meeting-floor counterpart to the annual-letter exposition in [berkshire-2005-letter](berkshire-2005-letter.md), where the Gotrocks/Helpers parable first appeared; the meeting adds the specific bet offer and the state-pension-fund corruption point.
- The Salomon 1991 near-bankruptcy replay (A10) elaborates on the derivatives warnings in [berkshire-2002-meeting](berkshire-2002-meeting.md) and [berkshire-2003-meeting](berkshire-2003-meeting.md); the 2006 addition is the counterfactual ("absolute chaos") and the key role personal reputation played in Nick Brady's reversal.
- Buffett's dollar/current-account thesis (A7) extends his published 2003 *Fortune* article and the relevant discussion in [berkshire-2004-meeting](berkshire-2004-meeting.md); by 2006 he is pivoting from currency futures (expensive carry) to earning power in non-dollar currencies via ISCAR and other acquisitions.
