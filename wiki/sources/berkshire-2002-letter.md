# Berkshire Hathaway 2002 Annual Letter

Dated February 21, 2003. Per-share book value up 10.0% to $41,727 (38-year CAGR of 22.2% since 1965). The headline 2002 events: the canonical **"financial weapons of mass destruction" derivatives essay**; the **General Re cleanup** under Joe Brandon and Tad Montross ($1.31B reserve charge plus the WTC-exposure wind-down); the **"no exit strategy"** acquisition philosophy crystallized; cost of float returned to 1% (after 12.8% in 2001); float grew $5.7B to $41.2B; Berkshire's first sustained foray into **junk bonds** ($8.3B by yearend, a 6× increase). New acquisitions: Larson-Juhl, Fruit of the Loom, CTB, Garan, The Pampered Chef; MidAmerican Energy added Kern River and Northern Natural pipelines. The letter also contains a major **corporate governance** essay (Audit Committee's four questions; the "independence" critique; the Arthur Andersen / FASB options-expensing history).

## Key themes

- **Derivatives as "financial weapons of mass destruction."** The canonical Buffett warning. Three structural critiques: (1) mark-to-model degenerates into "mark-to-myth" with asymmetric incentive to overstate; (2) credit-downgrade collateral triggers create pile-on liquidity spirals; (3) counterparty daisy-chains correlate in crisis. LTCM 1998 as the precedent. Gen Re Securities at yearend still held 14,384 contracts with 672 counterparties despite ten months of winding down. "Like Hell, both are easy to enter and almost impossible to exit."
- **General Re cleanup.** Buffett's frank admission: "I was dead wrong" about Gen Re's pre-merger discipline. Three failures: mispriced current business, accumulation of nuclear/chemical/biological (NCB) risk that the WTC attack alone would have threatened the company's existence, and inadequate reserves. $1.31B catch-up reserve charge in 2002. Joe Brandon and Tad Montross now restoring discipline; size "simply doesn't count."
- **"No exit strategy."** Acquisition philosophy made explicit against the LBO/private-equity frame: "we have no 'exit' strategy — we buy to keep." Why Berkshire is the first (and sometimes only) choice for sellers and their managers.
- **Cost of float framework, post-WTC.** 2001 cost was 12.8% (half from WTC); 2002 back to 1%. Float $41.2B, up $5.7B. The framework re-explicated: an insurer is valuable if cost-of-float is below the cost the company would otherwise pay for funds; declining interest rates have transformed "tolerable" underwriting losses into burdens that move insurance "deeply into the lemon category."
- **The three insurance disciplines.** "(a) underwrite with unwavering discipline; (b) reserve conservatively; and (c) avoid an aggregation of exposures that would allow a supposedly 'impossible' incident to threaten their solvency." The framework against which Gen Re had silently failed.
- **Audit Committee essay.** The four questions that put auditors "on the spot" — would they have done it differently; would an investor have received plain-English understanding; is internal audit as the auditor would run it; have actions moved revenue/expense between periods. Composition matters less than the questions asked.
- **Corporate governance and "independence."** The fund-board case study: legal independence has produced "absolutely pathetic" stewardship for 60+ years. Berkshire's response: pay directors a pittance, no D&O insurance, select directors with large purchased (not granted) Berkshire stock — to make family net worth the dominant decision variable.
- **Stock options and the FASB capitulation.** Recap of 1993-94: Andersen's Professional Standards Group ruled options were an expense; partners reversed; FASB voted 7-0 to expense; Senate voted 88-9 against; SEC capitulated. "Compensation-hungry CEOs loved this outcome: Let FASB have the honor; they had the system." Extends 1992 letter on the same theme.
- **Pro-forma earnings as cultural symptom.** "Last year, on a pro-forma basis, Berkshire had lower earnings than those we actually reported." The reverse direction is the giveaway that "pro-forma" is a euphemism for "ignore the bad."
- **GEICO momentum.** Premiums grew from $2.9B (1996 acquisition) to $6.9B; Internet new business +75%; the canonical "save $1B/year for policyholders" framing under [tony-nicely](../people/tony-nicely.md). The 1981-83 commercial-umbrella misadventure: $3.05M premium → $94.1M loss, of which $90.3M was uncollectable reinsurance receivables. "So much for 'cheap' reinsurance."
- **Ajit's reinsurance franchise.** $13.4B of float, "more than all but a handful of insurers have ever built up," from a 1986 standing start with a workforce of 20. The retroactive-insurance / "deferred charges — reinsurance assumed" mechanism explicated: $3.4B asset amortized over the life of policies; $440M annual charge constitutes an intentional underwriting "loss" that hides the true profitability of capped long-tail premium.
- **MidAmerican expansion.** Berkshire's first major non-insurance utility platform. Kern River + Northern Natural acquired; MidAmerican will transport ~8% of all U.S. gas. Buffett owns 80.2% economic but limited to 9.9% voting under PUHCA (Public Utility Holding Company Act). Sokol/Abel as dealmakers and managers.
- **Junk bonds re-entered.** Commitments sextupled to $8.3B. The comparison with stocks made explicit: stocks are conservatively-financed businesses where gains have exceeded losses 100:1 over 38 years; junk requires more careful price-value calculation with expectation of occasional large losses.
- **Equity caution sustained.** "Despite three years of falling prices ... we still find very few [stocks] that even mildly interest us. That dismal fact is testimony to the insanity of valuations reached during The Great Bubble." The hurdle: 10% pre-tax / 6.5-7% after corporate tax. Short money at <1% after-tax. "Successful investing requires inactivity."
- **Cost-consciousness as culture.** The widow's seven-word obituary ("Fred Brown died, golf clubs for sale") as the model.

## The derivatives essay

Berkshire inherited General Re Securities in the 1998 Gen Re merger; Buffett "judged it to be dangerous" but failed to sell it and is now winding it down. The essay assembles several distinct mechanisms by which derivatives concentrate hidden, correlated risk:

1. **Accounting incentives to overstate.** Mark-to-market in absence of real markets becomes mark-to-model, and ultimately "mark-to-myth." Asymmetric: errors favor the bonused trader or option-compensated CEO. "Only much later did shareholders learn that the reported earnings were a sham."
2. **Pile-on collateral triggers.** Credit downgrade triggers force immediate collateral posting, draining liquidity from a company already in trouble, which can trigger further downgrades — a spiral.
3. **Counterparty daisy-chains.** Reputed diversification collapses in crisis: "an exogenous event that causes the receivable from Company A to go bad will also affect those from Companies B through Z." No central bank in derivatives the way the Fed insulates strong banks from weak ones. "When a 'chain reaction' threat exists within an industry, it pays to minimize links of any kind."
4. **Concentration in few dealers.** "Large amounts of risk, particularly credit risk, have become concentrated in the hands of relatively few derivatives dealers, who in addition trade extensively with one other."
5. **Total-return swaps make a joke of margin requirements** — Party A puts up all the capital, Party B gets the economic exposure with no capital at risk. LTCM 1998 as the canonical near-failure.

Gen Re Securities at yearend: 14,384 contracts outstanding, 672 counterparties worldwide, $6.5B of receivables after a year of liquidation. "Valuing a portfolio like that, expert auditors could easily and honestly have widely varying opinions."

The acknowledgment that derivatives can sometimes be useful — "at Berkshire, I sometimes engage in large-scale derivatives transactions in order to facilitate certain investment strategies" — is paired with the macro warning: "the macro picture is dangerous and getting more so."

## General Re cleanup

The full Gen Re admission. Buffett "had studied the operation for decades and had observed underwriting discipline that was consistent and reserving that was conservative. At merger time, I detected no slippage in Gen Re's standards. I was dead wrong."

What had gone wrong, silently, before the merger:

- Current business was being grossly mispriced.
- Accumulated NCB (nuclear/chemical/biological) aggregation that "would have been fatal had, say, terrorists detonated several large-scale nuclear bombs in an attack on the U.S."
- WTC alone "would have threatened the company's existence" had Gen Re remained independent.
- Reserves were inadequate; $1.31B catch-up charge taken in 2002 to correct prior-year underestimation.

The fix under Joe Brandon (CEO) and Tad Montross: NCB aggregation reduced to tolerable level by 2002; underwriting attitude reset to "write only properly-priced business, whatever the effect on volume"; reserving discipline rebuilt. Importantly, by yearend each of Gen Re's three largest worldwide AAA-rated competitors had been demoted by at least one rating agency, leaving Gen Re "in a class by itself in respect to financial strength."

The retroactive-insurance / "desirable" charge mechanism explicitly distinguished from the Gen Re mistakes: $440M of 2002 amortization of the $3.4B "deferred charges" asset is an intentional accounting drag from capped-loss policies, not a reserving failure.

See [float](../concepts/float.md) and [national-indemnity](../entities/national-indemnity.md).

## GEICO update

Tony Nicely's 41 years at GEICO. Premiums: $2.9B (1996 Berkshire 100% acquisition) → $6.9B (2002). Internet new business up 75%. Policyholder savings vs. industry average: ~$1B annually. 19,162 associates; 19% profit-sharing payouts.

The 1981-83 commercial-umbrella misadventure as a discipline lesson: GEICO took in $3,051,000 of premium, paid $2,979,000 of it to reinsurers, retained $72,000 of risk. Losses to date: $94.1M (130,000% of net premium retained); $90.3M of which is uncollectable from "deadbeat reinsurers" (including a fresh $19M write-off in 2002). The lesson re-stated: reinsurer credit quality is the foundation, not a checkbox.

## Ajit's reinsurance — retroactive-insurance mechanics

$13.4B of float built from a 1986 standing start with a 20-person workforce. 2002 underwriting profit: $534M, after a $428M retroactive-insurance amortization charge.

The mechanism: Berkshire assumes from a primary insurer the obligation to pay capped losses on incidents that have already occurred (e.g. workers' comp claims from decades earlier). Berkshire receives a large upfront premium that is less than expected losses; the difference is booked as an asset ("deferred charges – reinsurance assumed"). At yearend, $3.4B sits on the balance sheet as this asset. It is amortized into income as a "loss" over the expected life of the policy ($440M in 2002 total, Ajit + Gen Re). Underwriting result reads worse than the economic reality; the cash float is what matters. ~80% of Berkshire's $6.6B asbestos and environmental loss reserves arise from these capped contracts, "whose costs consequently can't skyrocket."

- **Kern River** (Southwest Wyoming → Southern California): 900 MMcf/day; $1.2B expansion will double throughput.
- **Northern Natural Gas** (16,600-mile system, Southwest → Midwest): bought from Dynegy after the Enron unwind. ## Corporate governance essay

Five interlocking arguments:

1. **Boardroom atmosphere** — not law — is why directors fail. It is socially difficult in a well-mannered boardroom to question a CEO's acquisition, replace the CEO, or push back on a compensation-committee recommendation backed by a consultant. Outside directors meeting without the CEO is therefore endorsed.

2. **"Independence" is overrated.** Buffett's 40-year experience on 19 boards / interaction with ~250 directors: most were technically independent, but most lacked at least one of business-savvy, interested, shareholder-oriented. "Too often I was silent when management made proposals that I judged to be counter to the interests of shareholders. In those cases, collegiality trumped independence."

3. **The 62-year mutual-fund case study.** Federal law has mandated independent fund directors since 1940 (originally 40%, now 50%). Their two real jobs: pick the best manager, negotiate the lowest fee. Result over six decades: "absolutely pathetic." The hypocrisy is exposed when fund company A is sold to manager B and the independents promptly bless B (formerly available cheaper). Buffett's daughter declined a fund directorship that would have boosted her income by ~50%.

4. **Big-owner action is the real lever.** "Twenty, or even fewer, of the largest institutions, acting together, could effectively reform corporate governance at a given company, simply by withholding their votes for directors who were tolerating odious behavior." Bogle, Chris Davis, Bill Miller singled out for leadership.

5. **Berkshire's response.** Forthcoming "independence" rules will force changes in Berkshire's board. Berkshire will add directors with large purchased (not granted) Berkshire stock — making family net worth the dominant decision variable. Director fees deliberately "a pittance"; no D&O insurance; Buffett family members on the board not to run the business but to "ensure ... that Berkshire's special culture will be nurtured when I'm succeeded by other CEOs."

The compensation-committee passage is especially direct: consultants are "tail-wagging puppy dogs"; directors should not serve on comp committees unless they can negotiate as they would on their own behalf; "for anything short of [truly exceptional performance], it's time for directors to shout 'Less!'"

## Audit Committee — the four questions

The reform that matters is not committee composition but the questions the committee asks the outside auditor, on the record, at least a week before each earnings release:

1. Would the auditor have prepared the financials differently in any way (material or not)? If so, disclose management's and auditor's reasoning.
2. Would the auditor, as an investor, have received in plain English the information essential to understanding the period's performance?
3. Is the company using the same internal-audit procedures the auditor would use as CEO?
4. Is the auditor aware of any accounting or operational actions that have moved revenues or expenses between reporting periods?

"Audit committees can't audit." Only the outside auditor can. The committee's job is to make the auditor afraid of misleading it. "Haste is the enemy of accuracy."

Buffett notes the SEC's recent shortening of reporting deadlines is "a mistake and should be rescinded."

## The stock-options / FASB / Andersen history

A connected sub-essay woven into the governance discussion. The narrative:

- A decades-prior Arthur Andersen audit was the "gold standard"; its Professional Standards Group (PSG) ruled in 1992 that options should be expensed.
- Andersen's "rainmaking" partners overruled the PSG to keep clients with high reported earnings.
- FASB voted 7-0 to expense options.
- The Senate voted 88-9 against expensing under industry/CEO pressure. Several senators called for FASB's demise.
- Arthur Levitt (SEC Chairman) "reluctantly" backed off; he later called it the act of his chairmanship he most regrets.
- FASB adopted an "honor system": of the S&P 500, 498 chose the non-expensing method.

"Let FASB have the honor; they had the system."

See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md) and the 1992 essay in [berkshire-1992-letter](berkshire-1992-letter.md).

## Three suggestions for investors

1. **Weak accounting is a tell.** If a company doesn't expense options, or has fanciful pension assumptions, watch out. "There is seldom just one cockroach in the kitchen."
2. **Unintelligible footnotes mean untrustworthy management.** "If you can't understand a footnote or other managerial explanation, it's usually because the CEO doesn't want you to. Enron's descriptions of certain transactions still baffle me."
3. **Be suspicious of earnings projections.** "Managers that always promise to 'make the numbers' will at some point be tempted to make up the numbers."

Also: EBITDA-trumpeting as a particularly pernicious practice. Depreciation is cash paid up front; pretending it isn't an expense is "nonsense." The ten-year-prepaid-employees thought experiment makes the point.
