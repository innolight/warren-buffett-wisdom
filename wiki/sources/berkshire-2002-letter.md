# Berkshire Hathaway 2002 Annual Letter

The 2002 letter is Buffett's most concentrated treatment of institutional integrity — what destroys it and what sustains it. Post-bubble, post-WTC, post-Enron, he delivers the canonical "financial weapons of mass destruction" derivatives essay, a frank admission of Gen Re's pre-merger failures, a 62-year indictment of governance theater, and three investor tests for detecting accounting dishonesty. Float recovered to $41.2B at 1% cost after three distressed years, proving the insurance disciplines Gen Re had silently abandoned. The permanent-capital "no exit strategy" frame crystallizes Berkshire's M&A edge.

## Key themes

### Derivatives concentrate five distinct systemic risks

Buffett does not merely warn about derivatives — he identifies five structural mechanisms that make them dangerous at a macro level: (1) **Mark-to-myth accounting.** Without real markets, mark-to-model creates an asymmetric incentive to overstate, because errors always favor the bonused trader or the option-compensated CEO. "Only much later did shareholders learn that the reported earnings were a sham." (2) **Collateral-downgrade spirals.** A credit-rating cut triggers immediate collateral calls, draining liquidity, which can trigger further downgrades and more calls — a pile-on that can topple a company for reasons unrelated to its underlying business. (3) **Counterparty daisy-chains.** Apparent diversification collapses in a crisis: "an exogenous event that causes the receivable from Company A to go bad will also affect those from Companies B through Z." No central bank exists to insulate the strong from the weak in derivatives the way the Fed does in banking. (4) **Dealer concentration.** Large credit risks accumulate in a handful of dealers who also trade extensively with each other. (5) **Total-return swaps circumvent margin rules**, allowing one party to take full economic exposure with zero capital at risk. LTCM 1998 is the near-failure case. Gen Re Securities — still holding 14,384 contracts with 672 counterparties after ten months of liquidation — is the balance-sheet illustration of how easy it is to enter and how hard to exit.

Why it transfers: any opaque balance sheet with long-duration positions marked to a model — credit default swaps, private equity marks, loan-book reserves — is susceptible to the same accounting drift and correlation collapse under stress. The mechanism is structural, not specific to 2002.

### All three insurance disciplines must hold simultaneously

For low-cost float to materialize, an insurer must: (a) price with unwavering discipline, (b) reserve conservatively, and (c) control aggregation so no "impossible" event threatens solvency. Gen Re violated all three silently before the WTC attack exposed them. Current business was grossly mispriced. NCB (nuclear/chemical/biological) aggregation had reached a level where even the WTC attack alone "would have threatened the company's existence" had Gen Re remained independent. Reserves were understated by $1.31B — caught up in 2002 at heavy cost to reported earnings. Float nonetheless recovered to $41.2B at 1% cost (from 12.8% in the WTC year) once leadership reset the culture to "write only properly-priced business, whatever the effect on volume."

The corollary: reinsurer credit quality is foundational, not optional. GEICO's 1981–83 commercial-umbrella misadventure took in $3.05M of premium, ceded $2.98M to reinsurers, and incurred $94.1M of losses — $90.3M of which is uncollectable from deadbeat reinsurers. The lesson generalizes: any business accepting long-tail liabilities (insurance, lending, guarantees) needs the same three disciplines. Optimism in pricing plus optimism in reserving plus concentrated aggregation risk equals existential exposure that surfaces only years later.

### Permanent capital is a competitive moat in M&A

"Unlike LBO operators and private equity firms, we have no 'exit' strategy — we buy to keep. That's one reason why Berkshire is usually the first — and sometimes the only — choice for sellers and their managers." Five businesses joined Berkshire in 2002 — among them The Pampered Chef, built by Doris Christopher from a $3,000 borrowed against her life insurance to $700M+ in revenue through 67,000 kitchen consultants — all sourced from owner-operators who wanted their businesses and people to remain intact.

The lesson: acquisition advantage comes not only from price but from the permanence of the offer. A seller who has built a culture and protected people will often accept a lower price from a permanent buyer than a higher one from an operator who plans to re-engineer and exit. Berkshire's capital structure — no leverage, no LPs demanding a return of capital, no fund life — is itself the competitive moat. The right structure attracts sellers who would otherwise not engage.

### Board independence fails through social pressure, not bad law

After 40 years on 19 public-company boards and interactions with roughly 250 directors, Buffett's diagnosis is social, not structural: "It's almost impossible, for example, in a boardroom populated by well-mannered people, to raise the question of whether the CEO should be replaced." Most directors were technically independent; most lacked at least one of business savvy, genuine interest, and shareholder orientation. "Too often I was silent when management made proposals that I judged to be counter to the interests of shareholders. In those cases, collegiality trumped independence."

The 62-year mutual-fund case study is the proof: federal law has mandated majority-independent fund boards since 1940. Their two real jobs are to select the best manager and negotiate the lowest fee. The record: "absolutely pathetic." The hypocrisy surfaces when Fund A is sold to Manager B and the "independents" immediately bless B — a manager who was available at lower fees all along. Berkshire's response: pay directors a pittance, no D&O insurance, and seek only directors with large purchased (not granted) Berkshire stock, so family net worth is the dominant decision variable. The structural reform that actually matters is ownership alignment, not committee composition.

### Accounting dishonesty follows detectable patterns — three investor tests

Post-bubble, Buffett delivers three diagnostic tests any investor can apply. First, **options non-expensing and fanciful pension assumptions are cockroach tells** — visible corner-cutting signals invisible corner-cutting: "There is seldom just one cockroach in the kitchen." Second, **unintelligible footnotes mean untrustworthy management** — opacity is usually intentional. Third, **consistent earnings projection is a fraud incubator** — "Managers that always promise to 'make the numbers' will at some point be tempted to make up the numbers."

The FASB/options history is the political economy backdrop: Arthur Andersen's Professional Standards Group ruled in 1992 that options were an expense; rainmaking partners overruled them to keep clients happy. FASB voted 7-0 to expense options; the Senate voted 88-9 against under industry pressure; the SEC capitulated. Result: 498 of 500 S&P companies chose non-expensing. "Let FASB have the honor; they had the system." The episode shows that accounting standards can be captured by the interests they are meant to constrain — so the investor's last line of defense is recognizing the behavioral tells. The same logic applies to pro-forma earnings: Buffett notes that Berkshire's own 2002 earnings, on a pro-forma basis, would have been *lower* than reported — the reverse direction no one ever discloses.
