# Berkshire Hathaway 2003 Annual Meeting

The 2003 meeting was dominated by three ideas that had urgency in the moment but turned out to be far more lasting: a sustained warning about derivatives as a systemic threat (catastrophically validated by 2008), a careful articulation of why float is better measured by cost than size, and a sharp critique of every accounting trick then fashionable — EBITDA, option expensing, aggressive pension assumptions. Against that backdrop, Berkshire announced two acquisitions: McLane from Walmart (a $22 billion distribution business acquired in a phone call and closed with a handshake) and Clayton Homes (manufactured housing's best operator acquired after Buffett read the founder's book). Context: post-Enron governance reform, the first Bush dividend-tax-cut debate, and Berkshire's first disclosed currency bets.

## Key themes

### 1. Derivatives as systemic time bomb — the risk concentrates, not disperses

Buffett's famous "financial weapons of mass destruction" label pre-released in Fortune was not a prediction but a warning: derivatives have "long crossed the point where they decrease risk to the system, and now they enhance risk." The mechanism is concentration. When every large company hedges its foreign-exchange exposure through a handful of counterparties, risk has merely been transferred — and those counterparties are all exposed to the same events simultaneously. LTCM in 1998 demonstrated this at small scale; Fannie and Freddie illustrated that even very sophisticated risk managers could end up depending on counterparties who themselves unravel under a six-sigma event. Munger was blunter: he would "be amazed, if I live another five or ten years, if we don't have some significant blow-up." *(M38, A6)*

> **Supersession flag:** Validated dramatically in 2007-2009. AIG wrote $440B in credit default swaps it could not pay; Lehman Brothers' derivatives book froze markets globally. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac entered U.S. government conservatorship in September 2008.

### 2. Cost-free float: the goal is cost, not size

At $42.5 billion on March 31 2003, Berkshire's float had grown from $10 million in 1967. But Buffett was explicit that growth was not the metric: "What we really want is cost-free float. I mean, that is the goal, and growth is not — not at the top of the list at all." If float has negative cost (i.e., underwriting profit), it functions like equity without diluting shares. Growth that requires accepting underpriced risk destroys value. Gen Re had spent years exemplifying the wrong approach; Joe Brandon's turnaround had made it a source of profit again. *(M13, M15)*

### 3. Stock options are lottery tickets on the wrong variable

Options pay out not because the option-holder performed but because Berkshire as a whole did. Buffett described the Gen Re options inherited in the acquisition: "They would not have been valuable if General Re had been left alone as a standalone company. They were — they profited from the fact that other parts of Berkshire did well." Option vesting over time also rewards the passage of time more than performance, and because options benefit from retained earnings but not dividends, they misalign holder and shareholder interests. Good compensation ties reward to what is actually under the manager's reasonable control. *(M18, A27)*

### 4. See's Candy taught the anti-cigar-butt lesson — and it almost didn't happen

Munger told the story with unusual specificity: "If See's Candy had asked $100,000 more, Warren and I would've walked." [Buffett interjected: "Ten-thousand more."] What saved them was listening to a third-party critic (Ira Marshall) who said they were underestimating quality. The broader lesson is not just "buy quality" but "be willing to update your framework under criticism." Buffett confirmed that the transition from cigar-butts to wonderful companies was not a sharp line — the old ideas lingered and they "occasionally moved back" — but the direction was consistently toward better businesses. *(M29, M30)*

### 5. Opportunity cost replaces cost of capital

"Charlie and I don't have the faintest idea what our cost of capital is at Berkshire, and we think the whole concept is a little crazy, frankly." What they use instead is the simplest tool in freshman economics: opportunity cost. Every decision competes against the next-best use of capital — whether another equity, a bond, a whole business, or a repurchase. CAPM's cost of equity assumes the investor can't do better; Buffett and Munger assume they can, so the relevant hurdle is their actual best alternative. Munger noted this is exactly what Mankiw's introductory economics text prescribes, yet "the rest of the world has gone off on some crazy kick where they can create a standard formula." *(A20)*

### 6. Conservative accounting protects operating decisions, not just financial integrity

At Gen Re, Buffett discovered the company had been discounting workers' comp reserves at 4.5 percent rather than a conservative rate — inherited from the mid-1980s as a quick fix for underperformance. Munger articulated the broader principle: over-optimistic accounting doesn't just mislead investors, it misleads the managers themselves into making bad operating decisions. The Berkshire countermodel: "We tend to almost reach for opportunities to make our accounting very conservative. Way more than other people. We think it protects our business decision-making." *(A15, A16)*

### 7. The capital-light paradox: great businesses can't absorb more capital

The ideal business earns high returns on capital AND can keep deploying more capital at those returns — but "those businesses are so rare." The paradox is that See's Candy, Coca-Cola, The Buffalo News, and Gillette's blades all earn extraordinary returns on their invested capital but cannot meaningfully compound at those same rates on incremental capital. Berkshire's structural advantage is the ability to harvest that cash and redeploy it wherever it earns best. Munger flagged the second failure mode: a business that "reports 12 percent on capital but there's never any cash" — it keeps reinvesting just to stay in place. *(A23)*

## Q&A digest — Morning session

**M11. McLane acquired from Walmart** — $22B distributor purchased in an afternoon; Walmart wanted to focus on its core business; former customers had avoided McLane because it was owned by a competitor. Lesson in [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md): deal made in "an hour or two" with a CFO handshake — Walmart's word is a contract. [berkshire-hathaway](../entities/berkshire-hathaway.md)

**M12. Clayton Homes acquisition** — Buffett read Jim Clayton's autobiography after UT finance students gifted it; called CEO Kevin Clayton; made the deal over two phone calls. Illustrates the Berkshire acquisition flywheel: reputation reaches people seeking a permanent, simple buyer. [clayton-homes](../entities/clayton-homes.md)

**M13. Q1 2003 earnings and float update** — $290M pretax underwriting profit; float up to $42.5B ($1.3B growth in the quarter); $16B cash earning ~0.75% after-tax. Gen Re turned the corner under Joe Brandon and Tad Montross; Ajit Jain "made so much money I don't want to even tell you about it." [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md)

**M14. NetJets: bright future, no profit this year** — Used-plane write-downs causing losses; 75% market share by value in fractional ownership; European operation losing money but integral to global service. Competitive position is durable, but current economics are painful. [netjets](../entities/netjets.md)

★ **M15. "What we really want is cost-free float"** — Growth from $10M in 1967 to $42.5B is remarkable but secondary. The goal is always to make float cheap or free. Holding managers responsible for profitable float, not for delivering more float. If cost-free, the utility to Berkshire is essentially that of equity without share issuance. [float](../concepts/float.md)

★ **M16. Black-Scholes is "a know-nothing value system"** *(Munger)* — Black-Scholes is defensible for short-term options when you know nothing about intrinsic value. For long-dated options it produces "insane" results: Costco issued options at $30 and $60 strike prices; Black-Scholes valued the $60-strike options *higher* than the $30-strike. Buffett made $120M exploiting a Black-Scholes mispricing. Any mechanical formula can be beaten by someone who knows something. [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md)

★ **M18. Options are lottery tickets on the wrong variable** — Stock options issued company-wide give individual managers a lottery ticket on Berkshire's aggregate results — entirely outside their control. A properly designed system ties reward to what the person can actually influence, includes a cost-of-capital charge, and doesn't reprice. Munger: "More than 99 percent of corporate compensation systems are more than a little crazy." [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md)

**M19. Low inflation helps equity investors; expect 5–6% real returns** — Responds to Buffett's 1977 Fortune article. Inflation is always "in remission" — latent, tending to recur with human behavior and legislative failure. Low-inflation environments produce higher real returns, but a 1982–1999 habit of expecting 14–15% is "dreaming." Expected nominal equity return of ~5–6% is honest and probably correct. [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md)

**M21. No sweeping macroeconomic projections** — Berkshire doesn't decide what to buy based on macro forecasts. "We think America will do pretty well over time." Decisions are company-by-company, cash-flow-based. Macro is noise unless it directly prices an instrument they can act on.

**M22. "Desirable GDP" — GDP counts things you wish you didn't need** — Standard GDP counts airport security guards, war materiel, excess medical bureaucracy. Per-capita GDP that excludes what Buffett calls undesirable goods would show near-zero growth for 2001–2003. Investors should evaluate business against "desirable" economic output, not headline GDP. Useful mental hygiene for interpreting macro data.

★ **M24. Credit insurers don't understand correlation** *(Q from Bill Ackman)* — Credit insurance is the "easiest money" in insurance right until it isn't. Risks appear diversified by individual credit rating but are massively correlated in stress: "When things go bad, all kinds of things correlate that no one ever dreamed correlated." GEICO wrote $70K in premiums in the early 1980s and had already paid $93M in losses. Anyone buying a spread of 10-15 basis points over a credit that could blow up in a correlated storm is not getting paid for what they're actually risking. [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md)

**M25. We look a long way into the future** — Berkshire buys businesses it plans to own forever, so the competitive position must be durable over many decades. This rules out hula-hoop companies and any industry where the winner is unknowable.

★ **M29. See's Candy: listen to criticism** *(Munger)* — "If See's Candy had asked $100,000 more, Warren and I would've walked." [Buffett: "Ten-thousand more."] Ira Marshall told them they were underestimating quality; instead of defending, they changed their minds. Munger: "Think of all the money we made from accepting that one criticism." The willingness to update on constructive challenge is a competitive advantage and a cultural cornerstone. [sees-candies](../entities/sees-candies.md)

★ **M30. From cigar butts to wonderful companies** — Graham's quantitative approach worked on small scale; the qualitative shift came from experience, Munger's influence, and lessons like See's. "It makes more sense to buy a wonderful business at a fair price, than a fair business at a wonderful price." The transition wasn't a bright red line — they occasionally moved back. Lesson: evolution of framework is gradual, not sudden. [cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses](../concepts/cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses.md)

**M31. Hard to regain a lost competitive advantage** — Almost no examples in history. Packard Motor Company went downscale in one year and never returned to prestige. Pepsi-Cola managed it by completely reinventing its marketing, which is "very, very seldom done." GEICO got off track in the early 1970s but had a sound underlying model — the exception that proves the rule. [moat](../concepts/moat.md)

★ **M32. "Fretful disposition" is the enemy of long-term performance** *(Munger)* — Focusing on stock price rather than business value "is dynamite, because it really means that you think that the stock market knows more than you do." If the Stock Exchange closed tomorrow, Buffett and Munger would keep running operations. See's Candy hasn't had a market quote since 1972; that doesn't make the value uncertain. The stock market is there to serve you, not instruct you. [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md)

**M33. Triple-A rating: a privilege, not a license to underwrite stupidly** — Munich Re and Swiss Re lost their triple-A ratings. Berkshire will not relax underwriting to chase the business that flows to the last remaining triple-A reinsurer. "It should not cause us to do anything at all stupid. It just means that people that care about security of future promises should come to us." [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md)

**M36. MidAmerican Energy will grow — with or without PUHCA repeal** — Dave Sokol and Greg Abel have contributed to Berkshire value beyond MidAmerican, volunteering time on deals that benefited Berkshire and earned nothing for themselves or their company. Energy field is enormous; MidAmerican will become much bigger. [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md)

★ **M37. Why Buffett doesn't take a performance fee** — At the partnership, he charged 25% of profits above 6%. At Berkshire: "I would pay to have this job I have." He already had enough money; double-dipping off partners he treated as true partners felt wrong. Munger: conscientious people who hate to disappoint suffer *more* under performance fees, not less. The fee created pressure; removing it created freedom. [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md)

★ **M38. Derivatives: "a mild wakeup call"** — The Fortune pre-publication was intentional: Buffett hoped for a wider audience than the annual report. The core warning: derivatives are advertised as risk-dispersing but actually concentrate risk in a handful of highly leveraged, interconnected players. The problem grows as derivatives become more complex and usage increases. "When Charlie and I were at Salomon, they hated it if we brought up — and so therefore, we didn't do it — that we were too big to fail." Munger: "In engineering, people put big margins of safety into systems. In the financial world, in derivatives, it's as though nobody gave a damn about safety." *(See supersession flag under Key themes.)* [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md)

★ **M39. Do managers love the business or the money?** — When evaluating a management team, the key question is passion for the business vs. passion for the payout. Financial operators "leveraged it up, played games with the accounting" and now want to sell — "it's a piece of meat to them." Berkshire's managers identify with their businesses the way Buffett identifies with Berkshire: "It wouldn't make any difference what I get paid." Munger: "Probably the passion is more important than the brainpower." [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md)

## Q&A digest — Afternoon session

**A1. Q1 pretax operating earnings clarification** — $1.7B pretax operating gain, $290M pretax underwriting profit; $16B cash. (Administrative clarification, logged for completeness.)

★ **A2. Read everything, ignore management, wait for the fat pitch** — Research method: annual reports, 10-Ks, 10-Qs, daily and weekly publications, almost never talking to management. "Managements are not the best reporting parties in most cases. The figures tell us more than a management does." The Clayton Homes offer was made without visiting the business or meeting anyone — just a 10-K read and a book. The investment game's great advantage: "You only have to get one good idea every year or two." Ted Williams: wait for the pitch in the sweet spot. Munger: "The more basic knowledge you have, the less new knowledge you have to get." [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md)

**A3–A4. Strange things happen — markets are not bounded by past experience** *(Munger)* — Japan's 10-year bond at 5/8 of 1 percent. No one at any Berkshire meeting 20 years ago would have believed it. If that could happen in Japan, unusual outcomes remain possible in the U.S. Berkshire's derivatives warning doubles as opportunity: chaotic markets created by a derivatives blow-up could be "good for us," even if bad for society.

★ **A5. Intrinsic value is "terribly important and very fuzzy"** — Discounted future cash flows are the right framework; fuzziness in the calculation doesn't make it the wrong framework. It means you stick to businesses where future cash flows are predictably estimable. Dexter Shoe was the counter-example: Buffett assumed the future would resemble the past; international competition rewrote the competitive landscape. "20 percent of the Fortune 500 — but I don't know which 20 percent — are going to be earning significantly less money probably five years from now." [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md)

★ **A6. Fannie/Freddie and six-sigma events** — Derivatives are not merely a company-specific hazard; they are a systemic one. The mortgage option embedded in a 30-year fixed mortgage means S&Ls, Fannie, and Freddie all face "this very long-term instrument that can be very short-term if it becomes advantageous to you." When they hedge through derivatives, they depend on counterparties who may themselves fail in exactly the circumstances they're hedging against. "When LTCM had troubles with one type of asset, they had troubles with a lot of types of assets, and everybody else they were doing business with was having a lot of troubles with those same things." Financial models built on past distributions undercount six-sigma events: "there are more six-sigma events that happen in financial markets than any study of probability curves would ever come up with." *(See supersession flag under Key themes.)* [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md)

**A7. Currency: "we do not run a big currency risk"** — Berkshire's liabilities are overwhelmingly in dollars; the few billion in euro liabilities are matched by euro assets. Not worried about near-term dollar collapse, but acknowledges high inflation over a 20-30 year horizon is "not a low probability." Separately, Berkshire had made first currency bets at this point (not yet discussed publicly in detail).

**A8–A9. Acquisition snowball and the Jack Ringwalt story** — Deal flow is earned over decades of good behavior, not assembled through an investment banking network. "It's like being at the top of a very large hill with wet snow and starting with a snowball." National Indemnity purchased for $7M when Jack Ringwalt was in his 15-minute annual window of wanting to sell — Buffett deliberately deflected every attempt by Ringwalt to find a reason to back out. Now the snowball "attracts a lot of snow." [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md)

**A10. Telecom is outside the circle of competence** — "I have no idea how telecommunications shakes out." Knows Wrigley, Hershey, Gillette, Coke — products whose profitability is predictable far out. Telecom's economics were described confidently 3-4 years earlier by those who turned out to be wrong. Could buy telecom junk bonds (and did), but as a different category: "being an insurer of substandard risks — you'll have more accidents, but you can charge a premium that makes it work out." [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md)

**A11. Coca-Cola: still "The Inevitable"** — Global market share has never been higher. Since John Pemberton's 1886 recipe, it has been "getting into the minds of more and more consumers." "I don't know how in the world anybody would successfully dethrone Coca-Cola." [coca-cola](../entities/coca-cola.md)

**A12. Level 3 bonds: a bet on the people** — "We like the people. We think they're smart people, and they owed too much money." Buffett says he has "yet to see an electron" and doesn't claim to understand the technology, but believes he can assess the humans running the company. A different risk category from Berkshire's core strategy, acknowledged explicitly.

**A13. Value Capital: leverage is acceptable if the manager's own money is at risk** *(Supersession flag: Value Capital later wound down; Mark Byrne exited the partnership.)* — Berkshire committed ~95% of capital to a highly leveraged fixed-income hedge fund run by Mark Byrne (son of Jack Byrne, the GEICO rescuer). Leverage of 20:1 assets-to-equity is "a lot compared to the way we operate at Berkshire," but acceptable because: Mark has significant skin in the game, Berkshire's capital is not guaranteed, and Mark has a track record with the Byrne family. Key principle: leverage is not per se disqualifying if downside is shared.

**A14. Clayton Homes: "class player" in a troubled industry** *(Supersession flag: Clayton became the subject of a shareholder proxy controversy at $12.50/share; a lawsuit followed, claiming the price was inadequate.)* — Clayton's key structural advantage: most homes sold through company-owned retail outlets where managers keep 50% of profits and are personally responsible for every loan they originate. "If a dealer takes inadequate down payments or sells to people he shouldn't be selling to, it's going to be his problem." This creates entirely different behavior vs. competitors who had "gone crazy on credit." Berkshire's balance sheet removes the securitization constraint. [clayton-homes](../entities/clayton-homes.md)

★ **A15. Gen Re goodwill impairment: why there isn't one** — GAAP requires a goodwill impairment charge if the asset is worth less than its carrying value. Berkshire has large Gen Re goodwill but does not impair it because, as currently operated, "Gen Re is worth more today than at the time we bought it." Float has increased substantially and is becoming cost-free under Joe Brandon and Tad Montross. Discovery: Gen Re had been discounting workers' comp reserves at 4.5% (not conservative); Berkshire immediately moved to 1%. [general-re](../entities/general-re.md)

★ **A16. Conservative accounting protects business decision-making** *(Munger)* — *Munger:* "We are so horrified by the terrible business decisions we see made all around us by people who are relying on over-optimistic accounting." The Berkshire philosophy: "What is wrong with the world when everything is a little bit under-reported?" Under-reporting keeps managers honest; over-optimistic accounting masks real problems and delays real corrective action. *Buffett:* Gen Re's workers' comp discounting started as a quick fix — "it's like heroin. And you get on it and it's not easy to get off." [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md)

★ **A17. EBITDA = "bullshit earnings"** *(Munger)* — Depreciation is not a non-cash expense — "It's reverse float. You know, you lay out the money before you get revenue." Any management that doesn't regard depreciation as a real expense is "living in a dream world"; Berkshire spends more than its depreciation every year. *Munger:* "I think you would understand any presentation using the word EBITDA, if every time you saw that word you just substituted the phrase, 'bullshit earnings.'" The additional large accounting distortion: pension fund accounting and post-retirement medical liabilities, "horribly understated now in America." [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md)

**A19. Banks can be "surprisingly profitable" in a commodity business** — Returns on tangible equity of 20% in an industry competing on money itself, the ultimate commodity. Partly explained by high leverage, but also by the fact that well-run banks simply don't make the big lending mistakes that derail the bad ones. Munger: "We actually made quite a few billions out of banking, and more in American Express. But basically that was while we were misappraising it."

★ **A20. Opportunity cost replaces cost of capital** — "I have never seen a cost of capital calculation that made sense to me." Munger: the correct framework is freshman economics' opportunity cost — the alternative uses competing for the same dollar. Every acquisition, bond, equity, or repurchase competes against every other potential use. CAPM's standard cost of equity assumes the investor's best alternative is the market; Buffett and Munger assume they can do better, so the cost is endogenous and personal. [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md)

**A21. PetroChina: not a big deal** *(Supersession flag: Berkshire sold PetroChina in 2007 at roughly 5x cost, generating over $3B profit.)* — Berkshire owns ~13% of the H shares, which are 10% of the total company (Chinese government owns 90%). Disclosed only because of a Hong Kong reporting threshold. "We don't make any great judgment about China. We simply look at investments around the world and we try to buy into things that we think offer the most value."

**A22. The womb lottery — why great wealth belongs to society** — Buffett's thought experiment: imagine being in the womb with an identical twin, and a genie forces you to bid what fraction of your estate you'd give to society in exchange for being born in the U.S. vs. Bangladesh. "I think you'd bet a hundred percent." The point: success is built on a society that makes success possible, and returning wealth to that society is not sacrifice but justice. "There's no reason why generations of little Buffetts should be commanding the resources of society just because they came out of the right womb." [warren-buffett](../people/warren-buffett.md)

★ **A23. Capital-light paradox: great businesses can't absorb more capital** — "The ideal business earns very high returns on capital and could keep using lots of capital at those high returns. That becomes a compounding machine. But there are very, very, very few businesses like that." See's Candy has produced ~$1 billion pretax since the early 1970s; reinvesting it in the candy business would have earned terrible returns. The value lies in extracting the cash and deploying it elsewhere. Munger identifies the failure mode: businesses that show 12% return on capital but "there's never any cash — it reminds me of the used construction equipment business ... every year you make a profit, and there it is, sitting in the yard." [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md)

★ **A27. Simple compensation systems; no consultants** — Berkshire has no single comp formula across subsidiaries. Where capital matters, it's included in the measurement. Where it doesn't, it isn't. No comp consultants: "We could spend a million bucks a year on consultants to get an answer we can get in five minutes." In 38 years, no CEO has left Berkshire for another company. "Compensation is not rocket science. I mean, people will want you to think it is." Key: paying See's Candy managers on their own results, not on what Buffalo News does. [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md)

**A30. Intrinsic value: fuzzy but the only right framework** — "The intrinsic value of any financial asset is the stream of cash that it'll produce between now and Judgment Day, discounted by an interest rate that equates between all the different possible assets." Same framework for an oil royalty, a farm, an apartment building, a lemonade stand. Fuzziness doesn't make it wrong; it means staying in businesses where projections can be made with reasonable confidence. Munger: Berkshire's reporting is better than any comparable enterprise at enabling shareholders to calculate intrinsic value themselves. [intrinsic-value-vs-book-value](../concepts/intrinsic-value-vs-book-value.md)

**A32. Burlington Industries bankruptcy: why Berkshire won't bid again** — Submitted a ~$500M bid with a $14M breakup fee (~2.8%). The court rejected it as too large. Without adequate compensation for holding a large bid open for months while the world changes, "we will not be making many bids." Bankruptcy is "a very awkward way to buy a business" vs. calling Walmart's CFO, agreeing in an hour, and shaking hands. [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md)

★ **A33. "We like to go in heavy"** *(Munger)* — Berkshire does not believe in "a little of this and a little of that." When they find something within their circle of competence, they want to put a lot of money in. The limiting factor is availability of shares to buy at an acceptable price, not a portfolio-percentage cap. "Good ideas are too scarce to be parsimonious with once you find them." The great historical mistake: fading on price after a small move. "Those are opportunity-cost billions. They don't show up on the financial statements, but the amount of money that's been blown by dumb decisions at headquarters is awesome." [concentration-vs-diversification](../concepts/concentration-vs-diversification.md)

**A34. Short-term rates don't anchor the discount rate** — Berkshire uses a constant discount rate across all securities (approximately). Won't lower standards because the 91-day T-bill is at 1.25%. Cash at sub-1% is the cost of waiting for a 10%+ opportunity. The spread between current short-term income and long-term Government rates (~5%) is real but taken willingly. "We don't want to get hooked into long-term investments at low rates just because they're a little better than short rates."

★ **A36. Why 10% pretax is the minimum threshold** *(Munger)* — "Basically that's the figure we quit on." The threshold holds regardless of whether short rates are 1% or 6%, because it reflects what Berkshire expects to earn on future opportunities, not what is currently available in Treasuries. Munger: everything comes back to opportunity cost — if rates were to permanently settle at 1%, the hurdle would need revisiting. But so far, 10% pretax remains a defensible minimum that future securities will satisfy. [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md)

★ **A37. Berkshire insures the $1B Pepsi contest — and would insure $2.5B** — Pepsi's billion-dollar prize (present value ~$250M) has a 1-in-1000 chance of being triggered. Berkshire will pay it if it hits and is "willing to assume that for a payment." Would accept even a $2.5B present value if paid appropriately, but not $25B. The lesson: concentration of risk is acceptable if the premium compensates adequately; what is not acceptable is concentration of risk at inadequate premium (the credit-insurance failure mode). Berkshire gets called for this because almost no one else can genuinely commit. [super-cat-insurance](../concepts/super-cat-insurance.md)
