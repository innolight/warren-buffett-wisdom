# Berkshire Hathaway 2000 Annual Meeting

The 2000 meeting is the **tech-bubble meeting** — held in late April/early May, weeks after the NASDAQ's March peak began rolling over, with Berkshire stock itself down (the B shares had touched ~$1,500 from ~$2,800) and the press declaring Buffett "washed up." Across ~58 unscripted exchanges Buffett and Munger answer the standing pressure — *why no tech, why a half-percent year, why not "speculate a little"* — not by defending a stale style but by re-grounding the whole Berkshire system: that a bubble is **wealth transfer, not wealth creation**; that the circle of competence is about being able to predict an *economy ten years out*, not about understanding a product; that a moat must be **widened every year** or it is worthless; that valuation is Aesop's bird-in-the-bush discounted at an interest rate; and that float, deployed against a fortress balance sheet, is fungible with equity. The year's two corporate facts — the **MidAmerican Energy** acquisition and the first full year of **General Re** (whose Unicover reserve was a fresh wound) — anchor the insurance and capital-intensity discussions. Per the wiki's two-layer convention, a short distilled top precedes a **comprehensive Q&A digest** that captures every value-bearing exchange.

## How to read the Q&A digest

- **★** marks a timeless, load-bearing exchange worth citing in the future; unmarked entries still carry investor value but are secondary or period-specific.
- Answers are attributed separately to **Buffett** and **Munger** — a Munger line appears only when he added a distinct lesson (he answered "Nothing to add" to most). This keeps the wisdom fairly credited to both.
- **M#/A#** map to the numbered items in the raw morning/afternoon transcript for traceability.

## Key themes

### A bubble is wealth transfer, not wealth creation

Buffett's cleanest verdict on the mania: a company that "in the end, never makes any money" but trades at a $10–20B valuation creates no wealth — "there's a tremendous amount of wealth *transferred*." "If a company that's not worth anything sells for 20 billion and 5 percent of it changes hands, somebody takes a billion dollars from somebody else. But investors as a whole gain nothing." It is "the same principle as a chain letter," only with frictional costs (postage, trading) that make the group slightly *poorer*. The Nebraska farmland mania of ~1980 — land that could yield $70–80/acre selling for $2,000 at 10% rates — is the local rhyme: "that math will kill you," and it did. Munger's gloss is the most quoted line of the day: mixing "the mathematics of the chain letter or the Ponzi scheme with some legitimate development, like the internet" still leaves you with the chain letter — "if you mix raisins with turds, they're still turds." See [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md), [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

### The circle is about predictable *economics*, not understanding the product

The pressure to "speculate a little, maybe 10 percent of our money" in tech gets the meeting's sharpest circle-of-competence answer (M12, A11). It is *not* that Buffett can't understand the product — "Oh, we understand the product. We understand what it does for people. We just don't know the economics of it 10 years from now." The same problem afflicts a home builder or a carbon-steel company. Understanding means "thinking that we have a reasonable probability of being able to assess where the business will be in 10 years," and on the tech names "my friend, Bill Gates, would say the same thing… Andy Grove… would say, 'That's too hard.'" The discipline is to stay where the bush is forecastable: "if we can't evaluate a chemical plant in Brazil, we don't do it… they would be making a big mistake to do it through us." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

### Value is Aesop's bird-in-the-bush — growth and value are one calculation

There is no growth/value dichotomy: "every business we look at as being a value proposition." The original investment primer "was delivered in about 600 B.C. by Aesop" — "a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush" — and the only two missing variables are *when* you get the birds and *what interest rate* you discount at. Buffett walks the bubble math live: pay $500B implicitly for a business and, at a 10% required return with a one-year delay, it must "disgorge" $55B a year forever — "perhaps $80 billion pretax" — and "you won't find any" company earning that. Every year you wait to take a bird from the bush, "you have to take out more birds." Munger: "all intelligent investing is value investing," and the best kind is finding "a few great companies and just sit on your ass." See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md).

### The moat must be *widened every year* — or it isn't a moat

Risk, for Berkshire, is "what can happen — say five, 10, 15 years from now — that will destroy, or modify, or reduce the economic strengths" of a business; where that is unknowable, "we just forget it." The mental model is the economic castle besieged by capital: "millions of people are out there with capital thinking about ways to take your castle away from you." A great business has a moat that gets *deeper over time* — Chuck Huggins "throws crocodiles, and sharks, and piranhas in the moat" at See's, so "to our managers, we say we want the moat widened every year. You know, that does not necessarily mean that the profit is more this year than last year." The corollary (M22): the number-one question about any business is "whether the competitive advantage could have been made stronger and more durable — and that's more important than the P&L for a given year." See [moat](../concepts/moat.md), [risk-vs-volatility](../concepts/risk-vs-volatility.md).

### Float is the engine — fungible with equity because the balance sheet is a fortress

Float, not stock-picking, is where Buffett spends his strategic attention. Its value is two-dimensional — *cost* and *growth* — and he will trade one for the other: "if you told me I could add $50 billion of float and have a 3 percent cost to that… I would take that any day over adding 10 billion at zero cost" (A22). And the float is "in no way limited to fixed-income securities… available for anything we feel is most intelligent" — it can sit entirely in equities or operating businesses — but "the only reason we can do that is because we have extraordinary capital. And we don't have much debt." Berkshire looks at assets and liabilities "on a consolidated basis… completely absent any linkage" — get the liabilities as cheap as possible without ever endangering the ability to pay, then deploy the assets wherever returns are best (A29). See [float](../concepts/float.md), [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md).

### Pay for owner results; options are lottery tickets that subtract value

Compensation "is our way of speaking to employees" — at GEICO's scale "it speaks to them all the time," telling thousands of people what the business actually values (M17). Stock-price-based pay is "a lottery ticket": a small employee's effort "is not going to move the stock," so an option keyed to the market is an option on the S&P, not on the work. Stated as ownership math (A1): granting 10–15% of the company in options "is like buying an apartment house and letting the seller keep a 10 or 15 percent interest in the upside" — "options subtract value the moment they are granted," and managements hide the cost by gaming Black-Scholes lives (use "about a third of the market value" as the real cost). The CEO-pay ratchet is structural and one-directional — "I have never seen a compensation consultant suggest a plan that, net, reduces the cost of compensation" — driven more by ego than greed: "the gap that is eating at American CEOs is the gap between the rich and the super-rich." See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md), [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md).

### We don't forecast macro — we ask what is *knowable and important*

Asked to position Berkshire for the dollar and rates, Buffett gives the cleanest version of his filter (M13): currencies "might be important, but we don't think it's knowable. Other things are unimportant, but knowable. But what really counts is what's knowable and important" — e.g. whether "more people in Japan are going to drink Coca-Cola." Macro conclusions "just never enter into the discussion" (A7): banks' economics departments are "a lot of nonsense" — "if we ever get an economics department at Berkshire, sell the stock short." Munger: "we have a willful agnosticism on all kinds of things… a very good way to think, if you're as lazy as we are." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

### Buy permanent homes, never sell good ones, and don't try to fix culture

Berkshire's deepest acquisition edge is non-financial: it is "the Metropolitan Museum of businesses," the one buyer that can promise a founder his "masterpiece" will "never be resold" and that "you can keep painting it" (A10). The screen is "do you love the business or do you love the money?" — and the disqualifier is the seller who "cheats on his figures a little… and employs some investment banker who pretends he's getting bids." The same filter governs what Berkshire *won't* fix: "we really don't believe in buying into organizations to change them"; an organization "that lies to itself… is death over time," and it is "not correctable… based on the lifespan of humans" (A39). And the public-company *structure* is itself a moat — unlike Tiger or Quantum, Berkshire faces no redemptions, so "we're perfectly willing to let something we don't understand rage on while a lot of other people make a lot of money" (A38). See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md), [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

## Q&A digest — Morning session

**★ M3 · Aesop's investing primer: growth and value are one calculation.** *Buffett:* no distinction between growth and value — "every business we look at as a value proposition"; a bird in the hand vs. two in the bush, discounted at the right rate, with the live $500B → $55B/yr → ~$80B-pretax bubble math showing why no business justifies the price. *Munger:* "all intelligent investing is value investing"; the ideal is to find great companies and "sit on your ass." See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md).

**★ M4 · Speculation is wealth transfer, not creation.** *Buffett:* Graham's "voting machine… weighing machine"; a worthless company at a $20B valuation "transfers" wealth like a chain letter and destroys a little via friction; the Nebraska farmland mania as the local rhyme — "that math will kill you." *Munger:* "if you mix raisins with turds, they're still turds." See [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md).

**M5 · The internet threatens the businesses you *own* (the 10-year-old's question).** *Buffett:* no "religious belief" against tech; but the internet is "terrific for delivering information," which gutted World Book and now threatens newspapers — "the newspaper world will look very, very different in not that many years." Owners are "schizophrenic," seeing the future but still paying 20-year-ago prices. GEICO, by contrast, may be *advantaged*. *Munger:* "he asked if we were afraid… and the answer is yes." See [media-economics](../concepts/media-economics.md), [buffalo-evening-news](../entities/buffalo-evening-news.md). *(See M20, A14 — internet thread.)*

**M6 · "There's nothing magic about a one-year period."** *Buffett:* the half-percent 1999 gain reflects calendar accident, not a broken machine; down years "will" come; Coke and Gillette had bad *stock* years yet hold record share — still "inevitable" in their core franchises — while his own 1999 capital allocation was "very, very poor." *Munger:* the standing edge is liquidity — "the company has owned marketable securities in excess of its net worth." *(See supersession note on the "Inevitables.")* See [look-through-earnings](../concepts/look-through-earnings.md), [coca-cola](../entities/coca-cola.md), [gillette](../entities/gillette.md).

**★ M7 · "A mathematical edge in every transaction" — risk, the moat, and the castle.** *Buffett:* business risk is whatever can erode economic strength 5–15 years out; Berkshire will take a single $1B+ quake risk "as long as the math is in our favor" but is "very risk averse" about a *group* of bets, wanting positive expectancy that "gets almost to certainty"; every business is "an economic castle" besieged by capital, and managers are told to *widen the moat every year* even when profit doesn't rise. See [moat](../concepts/moat.md), [risk-vs-volatility](../concepts/risk-vs-volatility.md), [super-cat-insurance](../concepts/super-cat-insurance.md).

**M8 · Insurance "attracts chicanery" — surprises are negative.** *Buffett:* the Unicover mistake drew a $275M reserve ("about right"); a worse mid-'70s one (Omni) "would now be worth at least 10 percent more" in present value — "8 or $9 billion" — though it "cost us less than 4 million at the time"; insurance surprises are "unpleasant nine times out of 10," and crooks "come back again and again" because you "give them a little piece of paper" for money. Yet GEICO's own near-fatal early-'70s mistake "enabled us to make many, many billions" — mistakes can be useful. *Munger:* being "taken by a sort of obvious lie" is "the most irritating way to lose money there is." See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md), [general-re](../entities/general-re.md), [national-indemnity](../entities/national-indemnity.md).

**M9 · The 1950s partnership terms.** *Buffett:* "there was never any guarantee" of principal; the guarantee was "common destiny" — he had all his capital in it, no 1% fee; eventually settled on a 6% preferential with a quarter of the profits above it and full carry-forward of deficiencies. *Munger:* his own deal "was a third," but "we were smaller and operating specialist posts." See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).

**★ M10 · American Express: "huge value," but not an "Inevitable."** *Buffett:* the franchise is *share of mind* — AmEx held two-thirds of travelers checks for decades while *charging more* than Citi and BofA, and beat the Diners Club incumbent on a higher-priced card; the test of a great business is whether it "takes a lot of adversity and still does well" (the Tino DeAngelis scandal of 1964; AOL's customers "mad at them" yet growing). *Munger:* "it would be easier to screw up American Express than… Coke or Gillette," but "it's an immensely strong business." See [american-express](../entities/american-express.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**M11 · "We don't think in terms of absolutes" — no P/E or cyclical cutoff.** *Buffett:* no mechanical cutoff; bought 50% of GEICO in 1976 while it was losing money; NetJets is *expected* to lose money establishing Europe — what matters is the 20-year cash stream discounted properly. *Munger:* "a business with something glorious underneath, disguised by terrible numbers that cause cutoff points in other people's minds, is ideal for us, if we can figure it out." See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [netjets](../entities/netjets.md).

**★ M12 · Won't "speculate a little" in tech — and we measure ourselves by the *business*, not the stock.** *Buffett:* "we will never buy anything we don't think we understand"; plenty of others can make money in ways Berkshire can't, "but they'd be making a big mistake to do it through us." Critically: "we don't consider ourselves richer or poorer based on what the stock does. We do feel richer or poorer based on what the business does" — net worth rose "very slightly" in 1999 "no matter what the stock was selling for." *Munger:* "there are worse things in life than being left behind in possession of a lot of lovely money." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ M13 · "What's knowable and important" — currencies and rates.** *Buffett:* if he had a currency view he'd trade currencies directly, not via Coke; but he doesn't — the yen swung from 360 to the high 70s and back, yet "what's really made Coca-Cola strong in Japan" is acceptance and 900k+ vending machines; "currency might be important, but we don't think it's knowable… what really counts is what's knowable and important." *Munger:* "we have a willful agnosticism… a very good way to think, if you're as lazy as we are." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [look-through-earnings](../concepts/look-through-earnings.md).

**M14 · M&T Bank and reduced equity expectations.** *Buffett:* won't comment on trades, but praises M&T's Bob Wilmers — large ownership bought "with his own money, as opposed to options" — and will "probably own M&T" in 10 years; Berkshire "mildly reduced equities as prices… got more and more full" and doesn't expect equities "to be very exciting over the next 10 or 15 years." *Munger:* the Fortune article (Buffett's) on equity returns "should be absolutely must-reading"; better to "reduce expectations to some reasonable level." See [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md).

**★ M15 · "We'll never have a conventional dividend policy."** *Buffett:* pay "very large dividends or none at all" — retain a dollar only while it produces "more than a dollar of market value"; the logic is identical "in a tax-free society," so taxes aren't the reason; conventional payout ratios "strike us as nuts… there is no logic to it whatsoever." *Munger:* this is exactly what business schools and corporate-finance departments *don't* teach — "we're basically saying we're right and all the rest of academia is wrong." See [dividend-policy](../concepts/dividend-policy.md).

**★ M16 · We never sell a business, and rarely a stock.** *Buffett:* the ground rules mean Berkshire won't sell an operating business "even though someone might offer us far more than… they're worth" — a "quirk" that makes it the desirable permanent home; on stocks "we're not quite with Phil Fisher, but very close," holding forever unless the long-run economics change (newspapers and network-TV affiliates "certainly changed"); his first 20 years of selling were only "because I found something else I was dying to buy… Now, I run out of ideas." *Munger:* "we almost never sell an operating business… usually because we've got some trouble we can't fix." See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md), [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**★ M17 · Stock-price-based pay is a "lottery ticket."** *Buffett:* rational comp ties pay to what an employee can actually move (policyholders, satisfaction) — "compensation is our way of speaking to employees," and at GEICO it "speaks to them all the time"; tech-option repricing proves people "know they got a lottery ticket." The CEO-pay ratchet is fed by ego pyramiding down: granting a CEO an option on his own stock is economically like handing him "an option on $300 million worth of S&P futures for the next 10 years." *Munger:* many comp plans work "just about the way things would work… if you put a rat colony in the granary"; promising "free medical care forever" is something no one "who cared about the shareholders" would do. See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).

**M18 · Ignore book value for stock investments.** *Buffett:* "the really wonderful businesses require no book value" — they deliver cash rather than retain it; market price is a far better intrinsic-value proxy than book for Coke or Washington Post, so look-through *book* value "would not mean much"; Berkshire's own book is "a proxy useful in direction and degree," not a substitute for intrinsic value. *Munger:* "I think that's obviously correct." See [intrinsic-value-vs-book-value](../concepts/intrinsic-value-vs-book-value.md).

**M19 · "Wild things create their own truth for a while."** *Buffett:* markets show "everything" — companies worth "tens of billions… that are worthless," and at other times decent businesses at "20 or 25 percent of what they were worth"; the trick is to exploit one wild thing without being "carried away" by the others. A telling 2000 distortion: a public company valued at $10B "could not have borrowed… a hundred million" as a private business, yet its owners "can borrow many billions on their little pieces of paper." *Munger:* "the most extreme that has happened in modern capitalism" — "the ability to monetize shareholder ignorance has never been exceeded." See [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md), [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

**M20 · "It's so easy to copy in the internet" — the newspaper moat erodes.** *Buffett:* industry factors "will overwhelm any specific strategy"; the internet "gives everybody in the world real estate… there are no prime locations," dissolving the structural advantage that once let a single-paper town owner "put your idiot nephew in and he would do fine"; the Buffalo News will play it "as strongly as we can," but the economics may not survive. See [media-economics](../concepts/media-economics.md). *(See A13 — Omaha World-Herald specific.)*

**M21 · "Average is going to be terrible in insurance."** *Buffett:* insurance is "a commodity business in many respects"; an average operator "won't be a good business," and Berkshire's edge at GEICO, General Re and National Indemnity keeps it from average. *Munger:* "every once in a while, we have a business die under us" — trading stamps fell "99¾ percent from peak"; "there is no logical answer, in some cases, except to wring the money out and go elsewhere." *Buffett:* managers "almost never face up to that" — in a public company "he or she may be far better off ignoring that reality than accepting it." See [commodity-business-economics](../concepts/commodity-business-economics.md), [blue-chip-stamps](../entities/blue-chip-stamps.md).

**★ M22 · Competitive advantage matters more than this year's profit.** *Buffett:* hasn't read Porter but "we think alike" — durable competitive advantage is "the core of any business," and "the number one question… is whether the competitive advantage could have been made stronger and more durable… more important than the P&L for a given year"; study *how* moats were built — razor blades, State Farm's George Mecherle, Mrs. B's $500. *Munger:* every business tries to turn this year's success into next year's; "it's hard to see why Microsoft is sinful" for doing so — if that's a sin, every subsidiary at Berkshire is a sinner. *Buffett:* "We declare ourselves for sin." See [moat](../concepts/moat.md), [franchise-vs-business](../concepts/franchise-vs-business.md).

## Q&A digest — Afternoon session

**★ A1 · Most companies hide the true cost of stock options.** *Buffett:* companies game Black-Scholes (short assumed lives) to lower a cost that "doesn't hit the income account"; use "about a third of the market value" of a 10-year option as the real cost; granting 10–15% in options "is like buying an apartment house and letting the seller keep a 10 or 15 percent interest in the upside" — "options subtract value the moment they are granted." Like Galileo, the truth will "finally win out." *Munger:* "Warren's somewhat critical attitude is very understated compared to mine." See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md), [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md), [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md).

**A2–A3 · Self-reinforcing "cachet" moats (Moody's, Harvard).** *Buffett:* Moody's moat is "far wider, deeper, and infested with far more poisonous characters" than the operating company's (Dun & Bradstreet); and the best business he's seen is the Harvard/Stanford Business School — "if you cut the price of admission… you would have *less* demand" because cachet "almost makes it necessary that it be priced toward the top," so "you can throw away the demand and supply curves they teach in Economics 101." *Munger:* "Moody's is a little like Harvard. It's a self-fulfilling prophecy." See [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**★ A4 · Energy and transportation devour capital.** *Buffett:* both are at least *understandable* big fields, but historically "a terrible place to have money" — in rails "the amount of capital required to produce incremental revenues is just horrible," airlines show the opposite (top-line growth, "disastrous" capital, no returns); "if you find a business that has to keep adding huge sums of money every year… the net result after five or 10 or 20 years usually isn't very good." *Munger:* "historically, we've done very little in either field. And mostly, the past is a pretty good guide to the future." *Supersession flag:* contradicted by Berkshire's own trajectory — it had just bought the capital-heavy utility MidAmerican (A28), and in 2010 made the **BNSF railroad** its largest-ever acquisition; the verdict reversed once Buffett reframed regulated capital-sinks as acceptable-return homes for float at scale. See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md), [commodity-business-economics](../concepts/commodity-business-economics.md).

**A5 · Defending Doug Ivester's Coca-Cola exit package.** *Buffett:* voted for it; "with my 35 percent interest in 8 percent of Coca-Cola, I paid almost 3 percent of it myself" — "probably more severance pay than any man in the history of the world"; most of the package was "contractually in place," much arranged under Goizueta, and the change to Doug Daft would leave "Coca-Cola shareholders… many billions of dollars ahead." See [coca-cola](../entities/coca-cola.md), [roberto-goizueta](../people/roberto-goizueta.md). *(Sets up A6/A15.)*

**A6 · Excessive CEO pay creates "hostility" — and only ratchets up.** *Munger:* tiny perks (tax-return prep for 10 years after leaving) make "a terrible impression on shareholders"; "they get sold this stuff by these damn consultants." *Buffett:* "I have never seen a compensation consultant suggest a plan that, net, reduces the cost of compensation"; CEO pay "is not a market system… not subject to market tests," and the top 30 institutions, who could change it, "spend their time on… peripheral issues" of corporate-governance ritual that "don't mean a damn." See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md), [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md). *(Folds A15.)*

**★ A7 · Economists aren't needed — macro never enters the decision.** *Buffett:* "I'm no good on the macro questions" (too worried about inflation for 20 years); "I can't recall a time when Charlie and I have looked at a business… [and] macro conclusions… enter into the discussion"; banks keep economists for show — "the guy comes in and says, 'I think GDP will be 4.6 instead of 4.3.' So what?" — "if we ever get an economics department at Berkshire, sell the stock short." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**A8 · "Take on the qualities of other people you admire."** *Buffett:* the right models "never let me down"; tell students to "write out why you admire" the person they most admire and then figure out "why you can't have those same qualities" — they're qualities of "personality, character, temperament," emulable but "tough to change later on"; apply the reverse (Munger's method) to people you dislike. *Munger:* "there is no reason to look only for living models. The eminent dead are… some of the best models around." See [inversion](../concepts/inversion.md).

**A9 · No interest in running the Fed — "we should pay to have this job."** *Buffett:* an unequivocal "no" (which "alone would disqualify us"); no public job could entice them — "we've got the best job in the world… we should pay to have this job," and the gap between what a comp committee *would* pay and what a CEO *would accept before leaving* is "huge." *Munger:* "I never wanted a job where lying was a required part of the activity."

**★ A10 · "The Metropolitan Museum of businesses" — love the business, not the money.** *Buffett:* the disqualifying seller "cheats on his figures… and employs some investment banker who pretends he's getting bids"; the right one treats the business as a masterpiece — Berkshire promises Jack Ringwalt (1967) it "doesn't get resold… and you can keep painting it"; "if you don't know enough about a business to decide whether to buy it yourself," hiring a banker who gets "a very large check if you buy it" "displays a faith in human nature that would strain Charlie and me." *Munger:* "our culture is very old-fashioned… Ben Franklin and Andrew Carnegie" — "can you imagine Andrew Carnegie calling in a compensation consultant?" See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**A11 · What "can't understand a business" really means.** *Buffett:* "we understand the product… we just don't know the economics of it 10 years from now" — same as a home builder; "every business I look at, I think about its economics… it isn't that we shut off the valve, it's that we don't get anyplace"; even Gates, Noyce, or Grove "would not want to put down on paper… where 10 companies you would choose in the tech field would be in 10 years." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md). *(Reinforces M12.)*

**A12 · Berkshire fine "if I'm hit by a truck."** *Buffett:* his estate would be "99¾ percent invested in Berkshire," and "no one will be more affected… by that truck than me," yet he's "totally comfortable" given the managers and arrangements in place — and (A31) the successor "will be more active… the last thing in the world I would want would be a caretaker." See [berkshire-hathaway](../entities/berkshire-hathaway.md). *(Folds A31.)*

**A13 · Wouldn't buy the Omaha World-Herald now.** *Munger:* "if Warren had been offered [it] 20 or 25 years ago, he would've cheerfully bought it. And now he doesn't want it. And that isn't because of the economics" — owning it would make "life less congenial." *Buffett:* "there'd be no plus in life to owning the World-Herald." *(Mixed motive: part newspaper-economics caution, part reputational cost.)* See [media-economics](../concepts/media-economics.md).

**★ A14 · Internet: good for society, bad for capitalists.** *Munger:* "will the internet, by making competition so much more efficient, make business generally harder for American corporations… lower returns on capital? My guess would be yes." *Buffett:* "for society, the internet is a wonderful thing. And for capitalists, it's probably a net negative" — it "improves the efficiency of American business" without making it more profitable, so it is "way more likely to make American business, in aggregate, worth less." *Munger:* "that's perfectly obvious and very little understood." See [moat](../concepts/moat.md). *(Folds the economic kernel of A26.)*

**★ A16 · Demographics won't drive markets — Rembrandts vs. wheat.** *Buffett:* the retirement "wall of money" thesis "doesn't mean a thing" — what supports the non-productive is that "the pie keeps growing"; "the present savings rate will do just fine." *Munger:* stocks are valued two ways — like *wheat* (practical utility) and like *Rembrandts* (high because they've gone up before); "once you get a lot of Rembrandt element… and fuel the market with massive retirement purchases, you can get stocks selling at very high prices… for a long, long time." See [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md), [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md).

**A17 · The best book on how Buffett invests.** *Buffett:* Larry Cunningham's compilation (*The Essays of Warren Buffett*) "best represents my views" — better than rewrites that "hold out that there's some secret beyond" the annual reports, which there isn't. *Munger:* "As Samuel Johnson said, 'I can give you an argument, but I can't give you an understanding.'"

**★ A18 · Buying Berkshire was "a terrible mistake."** *Buffett:* a "cigar butt… with a free puff left in it" bought at $7½ on the expectation of a tender near working capital; after Seabury Stanton tendered at 11¼ vs. the 11⅜ agreed — "I found that kind of irritating" — Buffett bought control out of spite, making the textile mill "exactly the wrong vehicle" to build around (vs. buying National Indemnity privately in the partnership); "we started out with three disasters [Diversified, Blue Chip, Berkshire] and put them all together." *Munger:* "scrambling out of wrong decisions… is a big part of having a reasonable record in life" — "turn the lemon into lemonade." See [cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses](../concepts/cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses.md), [mistakes-of-omission](../concepts/mistakes-of-omission.md).

**★ A19 · Never trade Berkshire for gold — producing vs. non-producing assets.** *Buffett:* "I can't imagine ever exchanging any of my shares for gold coins" — he'd rather own "really fine businesses… selling products people like to buy" than "some piece of metal that people dig out of the ground in South Africa and then put back in the ground at Fort Knox"; "the idea of exchanging a producing asset for a nonproducing asset would be pretty foreign to me." *Munger:* gold makes sense only "like a Jew in Vienna in 1939" needing "transportable wealth"; otherwise "it's for the birds." See [investment-categories](../concepts/investment-categories.md).

**A20 · Why he ignores predictions.** *Buffett:* prediction is "a whole industry… space fillers"; Berkshire instead *bets against* specific catastrophes happening on its watch — "we know there will be a 7.0 or greater quake in California in the next 50 years… we are willing to pay out a lot of money if it happens tomorrow." See [super-cat-insurance](../concepts/super-cat-insurance.md).

**★ A22 · Float has a *cost* and a *growth* dimension — trade one for the other.** *Buffett:* Berkshire "consciously adds float sometimes at a given cost" and other times at zero; it'll take float "at costs only modestly below the Treasury rate" if that's the only way and it doesn't poison cheaper float — "if you told me I could add $50 billion of float at a 3 percent cost… I would take that any day over adding 10 billion at zero cost." *Munger:* "it is a very wonderful thing to generate… billions and billions of dollars of float at a cost way below the Treasury rate. There are people who would kill for such opportunities." See [float](../concepts/float.md).

**A23 · Wesco succession.** *Munger:* "we have paid almost no attention to the price of Wesco stock," so buying in shares to close any intrinsic-value gap is too tiny to bother with; on succession, "we are gradually making me so useless that I won't be missed." See [wesco-financial](../entities/wesco-financial.md).

**★ A24 · EVA is "twaddle" and "medieval theology."** *Munger:* "an awful lot of twaddle and bullshit" — EVA gets the core idea right (turn retained dollars into more than dollars) but its cost-of-capital machinery "make[s] no sense at all… It's like medieval theology." *Buffett:* "I like that second term better than the earlier one." See [model-risk](../concepts/model-risk.md), [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md).

**A25 · How he met Bill Gates.** *Buffett:* introduced via Meg Greenfield at the Hood Canal in July 1991; Gates "didn't want to meet me" but "wanted to meet Kay Graham" — they "hit it off immediately," now play bridge and golf ("quite competitive"); declines to speak for Microsoft on the antitrust suit. *(Mostly biographical.)* See [kay-graham](../people/kay-graham.md).

**A27 · GEICO benefits from *lower* industry margins.** *Buffett:* recent fat auto-insurance profits were "unduly benign" — "a big umbrella over the industry" that let "less-efficient competitors still do very well"; Berkshire "do[es] not like having a huge umbrella," wants margins to fall so "the most efficient… do well and the less-efficient have plenty of problems," and will "pour it on," spending to add policyholders even as near-term figures look worse — "the low-cost producer in a huge industry is going to do very well over time." Praises Tony Nicely: "everything Tony says and does makes sense." *(Folds A36 — internet shopping makes brand + low cost decisive; A32 — inflation lifts GEICO's dollar volume but risks a rate-backlash that legislatures over-react to.)* See [geico](../entities/geico.md), [tony-nicely](../people/tony-nicely.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**★ A28 · MidAmerican Energy: "decent" but not "extraordinary."** *Buffett:* you can't dramatically change embedded generation cost, and "you shouldn't expect to make extraordinary profits in a business selling an essential like electricity… the whole idea behind the utility industry is not to allow extraordinary profits"; but it's "a very good business," it can absorb a "$5 billion check" ("not the jelly bean business"), and Dave Sokol's "batting average is very high." See [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md), [dave-sokol](../people/dave-sokol.md), [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md).

**★ A29 · Float is available for *anything* — because the capital is extraordinary.** *Buffett:* "the float, in no way, is limited to fixed-income securities… available for anything we feel is most intelligent" — it has been entirely in equities; this works "only because we have extraordinary capital… and don't have much debt." Berkshire views assets and liabilities "on a consolidated basis… completely absent any linkage" — get the liabilities as cheap as possible without ever endangering the ability to pay, then deploy assets at the best returns. *Munger:* "we have used that edge in the past. And we hope to use it in the future." See [float](../concepts/float.md), [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md).

**A30 · Lou Simpson runs a separate book.** *Buffett:* the Jones Apparel position was Lou Simpson's at GEICO — he runs a "well over 2 billion" portfolio "100 percent on his own," compensated on its results, "without talking to me at all, which is the way we like it"; Liz Claiborne came from an off-exchange London block "at a very attractive price." (Declines the Freddie Mac question — "a lot of political overtones.") See [lou-simpson](../people/lou-simpson.md).

**★ A33 · "Products that travel" — and the puzzle of local brand strength.** *Buffett:* good businesses with room to expand at similar economics are ideal, and most mature U.S. products "have more opportunity around the rest of the world than here" (Coke and Gillette grew faster abroad); but "some travel well, some don't" — soft drinks and razor blades travel, boxed chocolates don't, and even *within* the U.S. Dr Pepper holds "18 percent of the supermarket in Dallas" but "six-tenths of one percent in Boston." See's "can't crack" the East despite trying "a lot of things." When the chance to expand comes, "you ought to just pound and pound and pound." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [sees-candies](../entities/sees-candies.md).

**★ A34 · Owner earnings: separate maintenance spend from growth spend.** *Buffett:* GEICO's marketing is split — only part is "required to maintain the business" (his "best guess" ~$50M, "could be 70… could be 30"), and the rest is "spent to build the business for the future, which we don't capitalize on the balance sheet"; every decision is "can we lay out a dollar… [for] $1.10," constrained by infrastructure (over-straining service "may hurt the business already on the books"). NetJets' Europe-then-Asia spend is the same logic at smaller scale. See [owner-earnings](../concepts/owner-earnings.md), [geico](../entities/geico.md).

**★ A37 · Real estate — billions, or nothing, with no captive department.** *Buffett:* a *WSJ* ad drew three $100M+ real-estate deals; Berkshire is "willing to put billions and billions in, if… the right opportunities" — but unlike firms with "a budget" and "a whole bunch of people who don't have a job unless they" deploy it, "we don't have anybody whose job is dependent on keeping busy"; Mike Goldberg is next door, so "we'll discuss it for three minutes" — "we don't care whether we make another deal or not." See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md), [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**★ A38 · Berkshire is *not* a fund — and the structure is a moat.** *Buffett:* "we're not a fund. We are an operating business that generates a lot of capital and uses that to buy other businesses"; unlike Tiger or Quantum (Robertson, Druckenmiller, Soros — just forced to close/restructure in the "vicious cycle of underperformance and… redemptions"), Berkshire can hold through anything; the structure is "poor from a tax standpoint" but it removes the redemption trap. *Munger:* "Soros, in the end, was not willing to have a lot of people make money in high-tech stocks and not be part of that game. And they got killed. We're perfectly willing to let something we don't understand rage on." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**★ A39 · We won't buy a company that lies to *itself*.** *Buffett:* "we really don't believe in buying into organizations to change them" — General Re recognized the Unicover loss "just as quickly if we hadn't owned it" (while Aon pushed it into 2000); Berkshire wants people "who tell us the truth but tell *themselves* the truth, which is even more important," because "once you get an organization that lies to itself… that is death over time," and it's "not correctable… based on the lifespan of humans." *Munger:* "we didn't improve behavior at General Re. They already had a behavior just like ours." See [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md), [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), [general-re](../entities/general-re.md).

**★ A40 · Reinsurance advantages — Ajit, reputation, and no volume goals.** *Buffett:* 30 years in reinsurance; "all excess returns will not be competed away" — "there will be people who get killed," producing wide deviation from the mean, and Berkshire's edge is Ajit Jain plus an *attitude*: when a competitor underprices, "it's our job to let them have the business" (harder at relationship-driven General Re — Munger's "necessary evil," where the owner underlines "evil" and the front-line man underlines "necessary"). The decisive edges: reputational + actual certainty of payment, and "no volume goals… none" to write more next year. *Munger:* the customer is "making a big prediction about the insurer's willingness *and* ability to pay" years later — "we have a huge edge in reputation and actuality." See [super-cat-insurance](../concepts/super-cat-insurance.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md), [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md).

## Concepts introduced or discussed

## Connections to other sources

- The **bubble-as-wealth-transfer** argument (M4, M19) is the spoken companion to the November 1999 *Fortune* article Buffett wrote (referenced repeatedly here, M14/A16) and the most forceful live statement of the [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md) voting/weighing distinction first invoked at [berkshire-1995-meeting](berkshire-1995-meeting.md).
- The **circle-of-competence-as-forecastable-economics** framing (M12, A11) sharpens the 1995–1998 "Graham's principles work in tech, we just don't" position ([berkshire-1995-meeting](berkshire-1995-meeting.md) M10, A24) — by 2000 the bubble has turned the abstinence from a quirk into a vindicated discipline.
- The **moat-and-castle** image is the same metaphor given its fullest form at [berkshire-1995-meeting](berkshire-1995-meeting.md) (M25), now extended with the managerial directive to *widen the moat every year* and the [moat](../concepts/moat.md) concept's cachet/self-fulfilling subtype (Moody's, Harvard).
- The **float cost-vs-growth** and **consolidated-balance-sheet** discussions (A22, A29) extend the float-as-equity reasoning of [berkshire-1995-meeting](berkshire-1995-meeting.md) (M23, A22) and the cost-of-float framework on the [float](../concepts/float.md) page.
- The **USAir / "make it back the way you lost it"** behavioral spine of the 1995 meeting recurs in spirit in the 2000 "terrible mistake" Berkshire-origin story (A18) and Munger's "scramble out of wrong decisions."
- *Supersession to watch:* the **capital-intensity verdict** (A4 — rails "horrible," transportation "a terrible place to have money") is reversed by Berkshire's own later path (MidAmerican here, BNSF in 2010); and the **"Inevitables"** confidence on Coke/Gillette (M6, M10) is partly contradicted by the next decade — Gillette merged into P&G (2005) and Coca-Cola was dead money for years.
