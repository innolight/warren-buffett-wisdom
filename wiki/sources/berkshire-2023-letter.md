# Berkshire Hathaway 2023 Annual Letter

Warren Buffett's letter for fiscal year 2023, released February 24, 2024 — the first letter written after the death of Charlie Munger (November 2023), opened with a tribute ("He and I were of one mind regarding our responsibilities to Berkshire shareholders"). A reflective, late-period letter that re-articulates Berkshire's settled doctrine rather than coining new frameworks. Buffett frames the entire letter around his sister **Bertie** as the model shareholder — smart, sensible, savings-minded, "nobody's fool" — and the obligation to give her honest answers. Its centerpiece is the "Operating Results, Fact and Fiction" essay on why mandated GAAP net income is "worse-than-useless." Two candid earnings disappointments (BNSF and BHE — the latter an admitted "costly mistake") and a records-setting insurance year fill the scorecard. The letter also gives the fullest account to date of the five Japanese trading houses and the Occidental position.

## Key themes

- **Operating earnings vs. GAAP net income** — the proverbial "bottom line" read $90B (2021), –$23B (2022), $96B (2023); operating earnings read $27.6B / $30.9B / $37.4B. The difference is mostly unrealized capital gains, which "at times can exceed $5 billion a day." GAAP net income is "worse-than-useless" as a guide to business value, even though it is "present[ed] fairly, in all material respects" and legally sanctified. Buffett's preference was "pretty much the rule until 2018, when the 'improvement' was mandated." **EBITDA is "a banned measurement at Berkshire."** See [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md).
- **Capital gains still matter** — the GAAP critique is not a dismissal of equities: "I expect them to be a very important component of Berkshire's value accretion during the decades ahead." Buffett's first stock purchase was March 11, 1942 (Dow below 100, now ~38,000); "America has been a terrific country for investors. All they have needed to do is sit quietly, listening to no one." See [american-tailwind](../concepts/american-tailwind.md). Graham reprised: "In the short run the market acts as a voting machine; in the long run it becomes a weighing machine." See [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md).
- **What We Do** — own all or part of businesses with "good economics that are fundamental and enduring"; "particularly favor the rare enterprise that can deploy additional capital at high returns in the future." Add able, trustworthy management — "a more difficult judgment." McCulloch's 1863 warning quoted: "Never deal with a rascal under the expectation that you can prevent him from cheating you." See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md), [economic-goodwill](../concepts/economic-goodwill.md).
- **The size anchor** — Berkshire's $561B GAAP net worth is the largest of any American business, ~6% of the aggregate net worth of the S&P 500. "Doubling our huge base is simply not possible." Only a handful of domestic candidates can "move the needle," and abroad "there are essentially no candidates." Realistic aspiration: "a bit better than the average American corporation" with "materially less risk of permanent loss of capital." "Anything beyond 'slightly better' … is wishful thinking." See [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md).
- **Our Not-So-Secret Weapon** — markets can "seize up or even vanish" (four months in 1914, days in 2001, September 2008); today's participants exhibit "far more casino-like behavior." The one immutable rule: **"Never risk permanent loss of capital."** Berkshire holds cash and Treasury bills "far in excess of what conventional wisdom deems necessary," took no help from commercial paper / bank lines / debt markets in 2008, pays no dividend, runs 100%-discretionary repurchases, and carries no material debt maturities. "Extreme fiscal conservatism is a corporate pledge." **"Berkshire is built to last."** See [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md), [risk-vs-volatility](../concepts/risk-vs-volatility.md).
- **Stick with wonderful businesses (Coke & AMEX)** — neither share bought or sold in 2023, "extending our own Rip Van Winkle slumber that has now lasted well over two decades"; Berkshire's share of AMEX 2023 earnings "considerably exceeded the $1.3 billion cost of our long-ago purchase." The lesson: "When you find a truly wonderful business, stick with it. Patience pays, and one wonderful business can offset the many mediocre decisions that are inevitable." Buyback caveat: "What is sensible at a discount to business-value becomes stupid if done at a premium." See [cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses](../concepts/cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses.md), [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md), [tax-deferral](../concepts/tax-deferral.md).
- **BNSF — railroads eat capital** — carried at $70B but "would cost at least $500 billion to replicate … and decades to complete"; bought in 2010 "at a small fraction of its replacement value." Must spend above depreciation to stand still: $22B of above-depreciation outlays over 14 years (~$1.5B/year), so dividends to Berkshire "regularly fall considerably short of BNSF's reported earnings." 2023 earnings fell (lower revenues; Washington-promulgated wage increases "far beyond the country's inflation goals"); margins have slipped relative to all five peers since purchase. "A century from now, BNSF will continue to be a major asset of the country and of Berkshire."
- **BHE — an admitted "costly mistake"** — the more severe disappointment. For over a century, electric utilities raised capital on a state-by-state promise of a "fixed-but-satisfactory return" — the regulators' own "margin of safety" approach. "Now … the pact has been broken in a few states," raising "the specter of zero profitability or even bankruptcy" (PG&E bankruptcy actual, Hawaii a current threat). Forest-fire losses are rising with convective storms; underground transmission is staggeringly costly. "I did not anticipate or even consider the adverse developments in regulatory returns and … I made a costly mistake in not doing so." Possible endgame: utilities forced into the public-power model (Nebraska chose it in the 1930s). See [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md).
- **Insurance carried the day** — records in sales, float, and underwriting profits. P/C, the "core of Berkshire's well-being," has grown ~5,000-fold (from $17M to $83B) over 57 years. Lesson learned painfully: underwriters "can't be optimists at the office." P/C surprises "are almost always negative." Buffett directs newcomers to the operations history and credits [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md) (joined 1986) and the GEICO experience (began early 1951); Berkshire's insurance bench "is to P/C insurance what Cooperstown's honorees are to baseball." See [float](../concepts/float.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).
- **Japanese trading houses & Occidental** — two passive positions described at length as illustrations of Berkshire's thought process (see [japanese-trading-houses](../entities/japanese-trading-houses.md), [passive-ownership-advantage](../concepts/passive-ownership-advantage.md)).

## Notable quotes

> So sanctified, this worse-than-useless "net income" figure quickly gets transmitted throughout the world via the internet and media. All parties believe they have done their job – and, legally, they have.

> America has been a terrific country for investors. All they have needed to do is sit quietly, listening to no one.

> Within capitalism, some businesses will flourish for a very long time while others will prove to be sinkholes. It's harder than you would think to predict which will be the winners and losers. And those who tell you they know the answer are usually either self-delusional or snake-oil salesmen.

> One investment rule at Berkshire has not and will not change: Never risk permanent loss of capital.

> Extreme fiscal conservatism is a corporate pledge we make to those who have joined us in ownership of Berkshire. … Berkshire is built to last.

> The lesson from Coke and AMEX? When you find a truly wonderful business, stick with it. Patience pays, and one wonderful business can offset the many mediocre decisions that are inevitable.

> All stock repurchases should be price-dependent. What is sensible at a discount to business-value becomes stupid if done at a premium.

> BNSF must annually spend more than its depreciation charge to simply maintain its present level of business. This reality is bad for owners, whatever the industry in which they have invested, but it is particularly disadvantageous in capital-intensive industries.

> I did not anticipate or even consider the adverse developments in regulatory returns and, along with Berkshire's two partners at BHE, I made a costly mistake in not doing so.

> [Underwriters] can't be optimists at the office, however desirable that quality may generally be in life.

> EBITDA is a banned measurement at Berkshire.
