# Berkshire Hathaway 2008 Annual Meeting

The 2008 meeting is the most explicitly crisis-flavoured session in the corpus — held in early May 2008, six weeks after the Federal Reserve's emergency rescue of Bear Stearns but five months before Lehman Brothers failed and the crisis entered its full rout. Buffett and Munger are speaking in the middle of events whose final shape they cannot see, which makes this transcript unusually rich in both confirmed analysis and superseded prediction. The major threads: what the Bear Stearns episode revealed about derivative interconnection; why "too big to fail" and "too big to manage" cannot coexist; how extreme market dislocations in auction-rate municipal bonds offered a brief and actionable window; what it means to be genetically programmed against risk; and the clearest on-record formulation from Munger that "diversification is for the know-nothing investor." The crisis context also validates lessons from earlier meetings — the float-like structure Ajit Jain built in municipal bond insurance in weeks, the PetroChina "financially fat" valuation discipline — while simultaneously producing supersession flags that only the September–October 2008 aftermath would resolve.

## How to read the Q&A digest

- **★** marks a timeless, load-bearing exchange worth citing in the future; unmarked entries carry investor value but are secondary or period-specific.
- Answers are attributed separately to **Buffett** and **Munger** — a Munger line appears only when he added a distinct lesson (he said "Nothing to add" to most).
- **M#/A#** map to the numbered items in the raw morning/afternoon transcript for traceability.
- **⚑ Supersession flag** marks an exchange where subsequent events (specifically the September–October 2008 crash and Lehman failure) validated or contradicted what was said here in real time.

## Key themes

### The mid-crisis diagnostic: what Bear Stearns revealed about derivatives

By May 2008 Berkshire had already lived through the derivative unwind at General Re (400 contracts that took four or five *years* to exit) and was watching the March Bear Stearns rescue from a ringside seat. Buffett's account of what would have happened if the Fed had *not* intervened is the most concrete systemic-risk anatomy in the whole transcript: Bear had roughly $14.5 trillion of derivative contracts whose counterparties, by contract, would have been required to establish their claims and undo positions "in four or five *hours*" — simultaneously, with everyone knowing everyone else was doing the same thing. "It would have been a spectacle … of unprecedented proportions," Buffett said, and would likely have pulled one or two more investment banks down within days. The conclusion: the Fed made the right call. ⚑ *Supersession:* Five months later the Fed did NOT save Lehman Brothers, triggering exactly the cascade Buffett described as the tail risk. The September 2008 seizure in money markets, the CP freeze, and the coordinated global intervention that followed confirmed that the systemic fragility was at least as large as Buffett had modelled. His mid-May verdict that the CDS market "probably not" would cause chaos (A23) was overtaken within the quarter. See [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md), [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md).

### Risk management is a culture, not a committee

A recurring theme across both sessions: the gap between institutions that have a *Chief Risk Officer* (title) and those that have a *risk culture* (reality). Buffett had told the 2006 annual report that investment candidates needed to be "genetically programmed to recognize and avoid serious risks, including those never before experienced," and by 2008 that language had proved prophetic: the major banks had weekly risk committees, models, printed columns of statistics — and "didn't have the faintest idea what risk they were involved" (A7). Berkshire's alternative: Buffett calls himself the CRO, will not farm risk out to a committee or a mathematical model, and has structured the balance sheet to "not notice" if the world suddenly stopped liking Berkshire's credit for months. Munger's gloss: the "dumb soothsayer" Chief Risk Officer convinces management to feel comfortable while doing dumb things — "you torture reality into fitting some mathematical model which really doesn't match, particularly, under extreme conditions." The double layer (deserve good credit → also be immune to sudden loss of credit) is the operating principle. See [risk-vs-volatility](../concepts/risk-vs-volatility.md), [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md), [model-risk](../concepts/model-risk.md).

### Crises create the fish; you must throw the spear before it swims past

The auction-rate municipal bond market (a $330 billion segment) froze in February 2008 when the monoline bond insurers that backstopped the weekly repricing auctions were themselves impaired. The result was that the same LA County Museum of Art bond repriced from 3.5% one week to 8% the next week to 10% the week after — and Berkshire found itself able to buy the same issue at 11.3% on one sheet and 6% on another sheet from Citigroup, simultaneously, for the same instrument. Berkshire put roughly $4 billion into this. The lesson Munger crystallised: "You're like a man standing by a stream trying to spear a fish and the fish just comes by once a week or once a month or once every ten years. And you've got to be there to throw that spear fast before the fish swims on." The opportunity for crisis-era dislocations of this kind was almost entirely gone by the time the meeting was held — the window was weeks, not months. Munger: "It happens about twice a century." See [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md), [arbitrage](../concepts/arbitrage.md).

### The one metric that survives any crisis: businesses that drown in cash

When an audience member asked what single financial metric Buffett would use if he could not speak to management, could not see the stock price, and had only the financial statements, Munger delivered what may be the most compressed statement of the Berkshire quality test in the corpus: "We tend to prefer the business which drowns in cash. It just makes so much money that one of the main principles of owning it is you have all this cash coming in." The counterexample is the construction-equipment business of Munger's old friend John Anderson: "You work hard all year, and at the end of the year there's your profit sitting in the yard." All that work, all that capital, and what you have is more used construction equipment — never any cash. A capital-intensive business that converts earnings into equipment, receivables, and inventory is structurally inferior to one that converts earnings into a cheque to the parent. This is the owner-earnings insight restated from the opposite direction: the right question is not what did the income statement report but what cash can the owner remove without impairing the business? See [owner-earnings](../concepts/owner-earnings.md), [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md).

### Concentration is the professional's tool; diversification is for the know-nothing investor

Munger's formulation at M29 is the sharpest in the corpus: "They've got it exactly back-ass-ward. The whole secret of investment is to find places where it's safe and wise to non-diversify. It's just that simple. Diversification is for the know-nothing investor; it's not for the professional." Buffett completes the thought: a know-nothing investor *should* diversify — that is exactly right for someone who cannot assess individual businesses. But for someone who genuinely understands, putting 20% in a "lead-pipe cinch" is wasting the opportunity of a lifetime. The Berkshire case: Cap Cities in 1974 sold at a third to a quarter of underlying property value with the best manager in the world; Coca-Cola around the time Berkshire bought it. In both cases putting 100% of net worth in would have been less dangerous than what brokers were recommending. The LTCM cautionary: their positions would have worked at 100% or 200% of net worth; at 2500% of net worth — effectively leveraging the cinch — they couldn't play out the hand. See [concentration-vs-diversification](../concepts/concentration-vs-diversification.md), [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md).

### Valuation discipline doesn't bend to crisis: the "financially fat" principle

Buffett's account of PetroChina at A30 is the cleanest statement of the required margin of safety for large purchases. He read the annual report, never contacted management, never got a broker report, and concluded the company was worth $100 billion while it traded at $35 billion. "What's the sense of talking to management? … I didn't need to know whether it was worth 97 billion or 103 billion if I was buying it at 35 billion." The analogy: "If somebody walked in the door here and they weighed somewhere between 300 and 350 pounds. I might not know how much they weigh, but I would know they were fat. That's all I'm looking for — something that's financially fat." He sold when oil rose and PetroChina's implied market value reached roughly $275–300 billion. The principle: if the decision depends on carrying the analysis to three decimal places, it's not a good idea; real opportunities shout. Munger's corollary: "lower due diligence expenses than anybody else in America, and we have had less trouble because we had less expense." See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [margin-of-safety](../concepts/margin-of-safety.md).

### The Ajit principle: from zero to market leader in months — confirmed again

The most striking operational fact in the transcript: Berkshire entered the municipal bond insurance business from a cold start just months before the meeting, and by the end of Q1 2008 had written over $400 million in premium volume — "not only larger than any other municipal bond insurer in the United States, I wouldn't be surprised if it's as large as all of the rest of them combined." Run out of an office of 29–30 people who were also doing other things. The mechanics were distinctive: almost all of it was *second-to-default* insurance — customers who already had triple-A coverage from AMBAC or MBIA were paying Berkshire an additional premium (averaging ~2 fraction percent, double the primary insurer's ~1%) for reinsurance that only paid if the municipality *and* the first insurer both failed. The fact that sophisticated institutional buyers were willingly paying double for Berkshire's wrap was, Buffett said, a live comment on what triple-A meant in the bond insurance field in Q1 2008. Munger had said in earlier meetings that the best investment ever made was the recruiter fee to find Ajit Jain; this was the confirmation. See [float](../concepts/float.md), [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

## Q&A digest — Morning session

**M3 · The Intelligent Investor as the antidote to lemming behaviour.** *Buffett:* he wandered for eight years with technical analysis before reading Graham at 19; Chapters 8 and 20 of *The Intelligent Investor* are the three big lessons — stocks as parts of businesses, the market as servant not instructor, margin of safety. "You will never be a lemming if you do that." See [ben-graham](../people/ben-graham.md), [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md).

**M4 · Cologne Re integration.** *Buffett:* operational continuity unchanged; once Berkshire owns 100%, he will take over Cologne's investment portfolio (currently the only two he doesn't run are GEICO's equity portfolio and Cologne's). Routine business; no management change. See [general-re](../entities/general-re.md).

**★ M5 · "Forget about the word 'stock.'"** *Buffett:* Charlie and I haven't the faintest idea where the market goes next week, month, or year — we don't talk about it; it never comes up at directors meetings. "What we see when we look at the stock market is thousands and thousands of companies priced every day, and we ignore 99.9 percent." When they buy a stock, they'd be happy if the market closed for a couple of years. The farm analogy: you don't get daily prices on a farm; you look at what it produces relative to what you paid. "Quotes would have nothing to do with it." See [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md), [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

**★ M6 · We don't cultivate great managers — we keep them.** *Buffett:* "We cheat. We buy businesses with great managers." With 19 people at headquarters and 250,000 employees worldwide, Berkshire cannot run its subsidiaries — it is *dependent* on them. The trick is not selection (the record is already visible) but *retention*: most managers are independently wealthy, so "our job is to make sure they have the same enthusiasm, excitement, passion … after the stock certificate changes hands." Can't be done by contract. "We have to see the passion in their eyes." *Munger:* analogy to Howard Ahmanson advising a hungry student — "I always keep a few million dollars laying around in case a good opportunity suddenly turns up." See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md), [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**★ M7 · Teaching option pricing is "totally nonsense"; markets as gambling parlors.** *Buffett:* option techniques to enter or exit a position mean missing a fifth of the transactions you want. "If you want to buy or sell a stock, you should buy or sell the stock." The one exception — the long-dated equity puts Berkshire has written — is a completely different instrument. Business schools waste vast time on option formulas: "The instructors know the formulas, and you don't when they come, and so they've got something to fill the time." Two courses suffice: how to value a business and how to think about market fluctuations. *Munger:* Buffett wrote a letter against option exchanges early on — "the idea of turning financial markets into gambling parlors so the croupiers can make more money has never been very attractive to us." See [investment-friction](../concepts/investment-friction.md), [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md).

**M9 · Reputation standard: the unfriendly-reporter test.** *Buffett:* Berkshire gives managers one message — would your neighbours and family be comfortable reading a story written by an unfriendly but intelligent reporter? No earnings targets, no budgets to hit. "We do not have a shred of reputation more than we need, and we never want to trade away reputation for money." Plays the Salomon movie every year precisely to keep that message live. See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**M10 · Raw material costs pass through; ISCAR confirmed.** *Buffett:* tungsten cost changes have a 3–6 month lag before a high-value-added manufacturer like ISCAR passes them through. "I had very high expectations when we bought it. It's exceeded it in every way — financial performance, human relations. It's been a dream acquisition." *Munger:* while inflation is bad for society, Berkshire will probably make more money because of it. See [berkshire-hathaway](../entities/berkshire-hathaway.md).

**★ M11 · Size is a permanent handicap on future returns.** *Buffett:* Berkshire's investable universe has shrunk to companies with market caps of at least $50 billion to move the needle; a $10B position that doubles saves only ~$325M after tax — less than 0.2% on Berkshire. "Our universe has shrunk enormously, and we will not do as well in that universe — remotely as well — as we would if we were operating in a much wider universe." Anyone expecting past returns should sell. *Munger:* "We are very happy making money at a rate in the future that is way less than the rate at which we made money in the past. And I suggest that you adopt the same attitude." For small capital, the universe of "thousands of possibilities" remains open. See [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md).

**M17 · Long-short strategy: modest, then abandoned.** *Buffett:* in the early 1960s partnership he shorted the general market (borrowed stocks directly from universities — Harvard, Columbia, Chicago — paying a fee) while going long undervalued situations, not shorting individual names. Ben Graham had run a pairs-trading hedge fund in the 1920s and found it worked "modestly — very modestly — well because he was right four times out of five, but the fifth time tended to kill him on the other four." Buffett stopped after about five years. *Munger:* "We made our money by being long some wonderful businesses. We didn't make it by a long-short strategy." See [arbitrage](../concepts/arbitrage.md).

**★ M18 · Big dislocations are brief; you must be ready to throw the spear.** *Buffett:* the auction-rate municipal bond market (a $330B segment) froze in February 2008 when monoline backstops failed. The LA County Museum of Art bond repriced 3.15% → 4.0% → 3.5% → 8% → 10% in consecutive weekly auctions. On one Citigroup bid sheet, the same issue in the same week was bid at 11.3% by Berkshire on one page and 6% by someone else on another page. Berkshire deployed ~$4 billion. *Munger:* "You're like a man standing by a stream trying to spear a fish and the fish just comes by once a week or once a month or once every ten years. … If you can't think fast and act resolutely, it does you no good." Munger: "It happens about twice a century." See [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md), [arbitrage](../concepts/arbitrage.md).

**M19 · Compounding is "an automatic formula for getting ahead" — not galloping ahead.** *Buffett:* Berkshire has never tried a master stroke to quadruple in size; it has simply kept doing the same thing consistently. The formula: all current businesses, most doing better, with a few additions. "That's an automatic formula for getting ahead, but it's not an automatic formula for galloping ahead." *Munger:* most small businesses will never be big; most big businesses eventually fall into mediocrity. Berkshire created exactly one large business from scratch — Ajit's reinsurance operation: "we're a one-trick pony." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**★ M20 · Municipal bond insurance: Ajit builds the market's dominant player from zero in months.** *Buffett:* in Q1 2008 Berkshire's municipal bond insurance wrote over $400M in premium volume — probably larger than all other bond insurers combined — run by 29–30 people in an office also doing other things. Almost all business was second-to-default: buyers who already had AMBAC or MBIA coverage paid Berkshire ~2% on top of the original ~1% for wrap insurance that would only pay if both the municipality AND the primary insurer defaulted. "It tells you something about the meaning of triple-A in the bond insurance field in Q1 2008." Berkshire's primary Detroit Sewer and Water policies (each ~$370M) traded in the secondary market at better yields than any other insurer's. ⚑ *Supersession:* The monolines were severely impaired or effectively insolvent by late 2008, confirming that buyers' instinct to pay extra for Berkshire's wrap was rational — the first-insurer risk was real. See [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md), [float](../concepts/float.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**★ M21 · "We prefer the business which drowns in cash."** *Munger:* the best single metric from a financial statement alone is cash generation. "One of the main principles of owning it is you have all this cash coming in." The counterexample — Munger's friend's construction-equipment business: "You work hard all year, and at the end of the year there's your profit sitting in the yard." Never any cash, just more used equipment. *Buffett:* "It's a lot easier to understand a business that's mailing you a check every month." A business that gives you cash lets you value it from its financials; one that eats all earnings in working capital and equipment does not. See [owner-earnings](../concepts/owner-earnings.md), [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md).

**M23 · Reading advice for a 12-year-old.** *Buffett:* read a daily newspaper; "you can't learn enough in life." The more you learn, the more you want to learn. *Munger:* "The young person that just spoke has already figured out how to succeed in life. You've got it made." Nothing substantive added; banter-adjacent.

**★ M24 · "We never urge people to sell good businesses."** *Buffett:* if you have a wonderful private business worth $1B, you already have $1B — "just in the farm." There is no reason to sell a wonderful business except extraneous factors (family, taxes, succession). Berkshire's pitch to European family-business owners is not "sell now" but "when the event does occur that causes you to think about selling, we want to be on your radar screen." *Munger:* a friend built a wonderful southern California business over a lifetime and then sold it to a known crook for a slightly higher price — "I think that's an insane way to live a life." See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**M25 · Dollar likely to weaken; no need to hedge long-lived businesses.** *Buffett:* if he landed from Mars today with a billion to invest, he wouldn't put all of it in US dollars. The US has been following policies that weakened the dollar in recent years and is likely to continue. Berkshire is happy owning businesses that earn in euros, sterling, or other major currencies unhedged because "we do not think our opinion on currencies is any good." Their ~200M Coca-Cola shares earn ~$600M annually, with ~$500M from abroad — a net plus in recent years. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**M26 · Small capital opens the full universe.** *Buffett:* with small sums, the investable universe is thousands of possibilities: small stocks (including non-US — Korea a few years ago was "ridiculously cheap"); specialist bond situations including mispriced junk; tax liens. "An enterprising person can find a lot of different ways to make money." *Munger:* "Sure." See [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md).

**M27 · Politicians pander on the campaign trail and behave better in office.** *Buffett:* largely off-domain political commentary; the load-bearing investment insight is the throwaway: "You want to buy stock in a business that's so good that an idiot can run it because sooner or later one will. And we live in a country that is so good that your children and grandchildren will live a lot better than you live, even though an idiot or two runs it." *Munger:* post-Enron, Sarbanes-Oxley "was shooting at an elephant with a pea shooter," and the present convulsion "makes Enron look like a tea party." Regulation will change and "won't work perfectly." See [american-tailwind](../concepts/american-tailwind.md).

**M28 · Succession: "there will be no gap after my death."** *Buffett:* the board has three CEO candidates (any one could step in tomorrow, though the best candidate might change over two or three years); four investment-officer candidates, all with good current jobs they'd leave immediately if the board called. No retention incentive is needed — all are wealthy, all would take the job for less money than they currently make. The board will decide whether to have one, two, three, or four investment officers depending on the incoming CEO's preference. "There will be no gap after my death in terms of having somebody managing the money, and they'll probably be a lot more energetic than I am now." *Munger:* "We still have a rising young man here named Warren Buffett." See [warren-buffett](../people/warren-buffett.md).

**★ M29 · "Diversification is for the know-nothing investor."** *Munger:* finance students at elite business schools are taught that the whole secret of investment is diversification — "they've got it exactly back-ass-ward. The whole secret of investment is to find places where it's safe and wise to non-diversify. It's just that simple. Diversification is for the know-nothing investor; it's not for the professional." *Buffett:* a know-nothing investor *should* diversify — that is the right answer for someone who cannot assess individual businesses. But a professional who puts only 20% in a once-in-a-lifetime cinch "will have wasted the opportunity of a lifetime." Examples: Cap Cities in 1974 at a third to a quarter of property value + best manager in the world = safe to put 100% in. Coke around when Berkshire bought it: same. LTCM cautionary: brilliant men with genuinely convergent positions, but at 25× net worth instead of 2–3× net worth, couldn't play out the hand. See [concentration-vs-diversification](../concepts/concentration-vs-diversification.md), [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md).

**M31 · Think of all accounts as one pot; high-yield in tax-deferred is the main exception.** *Buffett:* treat spouse + self as one financial unit; don't worry about which legal entity holds which asset. "Look at your overall financial condition and not worry about where the location of the assets will be." *Munger:* the main exception is investments that generate heavy taxable income (high-yield junk bonds, for instance) — better in tax-deferred accounts. See [tax-deferral](../concepts/tax-deferral.md).

**M32 · Oil won't "run out" — but daily production will eventually decline.** *Buffett:* oil doesn't work like an on-off switch; productive capacity will plateau then decline gradually. Berkshire is "very much closer to producing almost as much as our productive ability is in the world … than we've ever been." Surplus capacity is at historic lows. What matters is whether the world hits peak at 5 years from now or 50 years. *Munger:* production will be "down" in 25 years; the eventual answer is the sun. Brief energy-sector context; no investable conclusion stated.

**M34 · Ethanol from corn is "monstrously dumb."** *Munger:* his previous year's comment that turning American corn into motor fuel was "one of the dumbest ideas … I'd ever seen" upgraded: "This idea was so monstrously dumb that I think it's probably on its way out." The head of an academic institution visiting Nebraska called it "stunningly stupid." A classic Munger inversion of popular policy consensus. See [inversion](../concepts/inversion.md).

**★ M35 · Amateurs should use low-cost index funds and forget it.** *Buffett:* for someone who is not a full-time investor and has a first million — "I'd probably have it all in a very low-cost index fund … probably Vanguard … somebody I knew was reliable, somebody where the cost was low." Unless bought in a strong bull market, an index fund will outperform bonds over the long period, and "then I'd forget it and go back to work." *Munger:* "A great horde of professionals [are] taking croupier profits out of the system, most of them by pretending to be professional investors." If you have no rational prospect of being a skilled professional investor, "of course you should compromise on some simple thing like an index fund." *Buffett:* "You will not get that advice from anybody because nobody gets paid to give you that advice." See [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md), [investment-friction](../concepts/investment-friction.md).

## Q&A digest — Afternoon session

**★ A2 · "Too big to fail" cannot coexist with "too big to manage."** *Buffett:* investment and commercial banks have likely become too large to manage their risk effectively. When he acquired Gen Re with ~23,000 derivative contracts, he concluded he "probably could have spent full time on that and not really been able to get my mind around how much risk we were running." At major banks with many more contracts: the CEO who acknowledges knowing of such activities "is embarrassed either way — less embarrassing to say I didn't know." OFHEO had 200 employees whose *sole* job was supervising Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac — "two of the biggest accounting misrepresentations in the history of the world." Bear Stearns had ~$14.5 trillion of derivative contracts; if it had filed in bankruptcy Sunday night, counterparties would have had "four or five hours" to undo what Berkshire needed four or five *years* to unwind from Gen Re — simultaneously, with everyone knowing. "Another investment bank or two going down within a matter of days." The Fed did the right thing. *Munger:* "It's crazy to have people get so big and so important that you can't allow them to fail, and allow them to be run with as much knavery and stupidity as permeated the major investment banks." The accounting profession "utterly failed us." Good-until-reached-for assets at Salomon: "$400 million of that 'good until reached for' assets … and they were behaving honorably." ⚑ *Supersession:* September 2008 — the Fed did NOT save Lehman. The cascade Buffett described as the tail risk of a Bear Stearns failure occurred in modified form: Lehman's CDS and repo counterparties scrambled to establish claims, money-market funds broke the buck, the commercial paper market froze, and central banks worldwide deployed coordinated interventions of historic scale. The "too big to manage" thesis was directly confirmed. See [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md), [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md), [model-risk](../concepts/model-risk.md).

**★ A3 · Why Berkshire does no conventional due diligence on large acquisitions.** *Buffett:* PetroChina in 2002–2003: read the annual report, never contacted management, never got a broker report. Concluded the company was worth ~$100 billion; checked the price at ~$35 billion. "What's the sense of talking to management? … I didn't need to know whether it was worth 97 billion or 103 billion if I was buying it at 35 billion." The analogy: a man weighing 300–350 pounds — "I might not know how much they weigh, but I would know they were fat. That's all I'm looking for — something that's financially fat." On the Mars-Wrigley financing call: "We got a call, it made sense, and we said yes … we don't say yes with a material adverse change clause. We don't say yes, if financing is available. We just say yes." *Munger:* "We have lower due diligence expenses than anybody else in America, and we have had less trouble because we had less expense." The alternative — paying outsiders to do fine-grain judgments and relying on their fee-motivated work — is "a very dicey process." Buffett: "I've made plenty of big investment mistakes. I've never made one, in my view, that would have been avoided by conventional due diligence." See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), [margin-of-safety](../concepts/margin-of-safety.md).

**A5 · Berkshire takeover after Buffett is "very unlikely."** *Buffett:* his stock distributes over ~12 years after death; a $600–700B+ (and growing) takeover would be as unlikely as taking over General Electric or Exxon — not impossible, but approximately so. The concentrations of like-minded large holders reduce the probability further. Structural response to a reasonable shareholder concern. See [berkshire-hathaway](../entities/berkshire-hathaway.md).

**A6 · A well-established popular brand is almost impossible to dislodge.** *Buffett:* Coke will sell a billion and a half eight-ounce servings today worldwide; since 1886 the name has been "associated with happiness and good value." "It's just about impossible … to take on a product like that." RC Cola has been around a long time — not going anywhere. Richard Branson's Virgin Cola couldn't crack the market despite massive celebrity. Kraft, Kool-Aid, See's — runaway leaders in their fields. "If you buy in with good branded products and you don't pay too much, you're probably going to do OK." But don't expect super-rich — these attributes are widely recognized. See [moat](../concepts/moat.md), [coca-cola](../entities/coca-cola.md).

**★ A7 · Investment candidates must be genetically programmed against risk — including risks never before experienced.** *Buffett:* quoted his 2006 annual report criteria for investment officer candidates — "genetically programmed to recognize and avoid serious risks, including those never before experienced" — and noted it had "proved to be somewhat prophetic." Major banks had risk committees that met weekly, models with statistics in nice columns, and "didn't have the faintest idea what risk they were involved." The rarer quality: actually contemplating "problems that haven't popped up yet but which are starting to become possibilities, in terms of new financial instruments or new behaviors." *Munger:* "You call a man the Chief Risk Officer, but often he is functioning as a guy that makes you feel good while you do dumb things … a dumb soothsayer." Berkshire's double layer: (1) behave so no rational person worries about Berkshire's credit; (2) also behave so that if the world suddenly disliked the credit, Berkshire "wouldn't even notice it for months" due to liquidity. "That double layering of protection against risk is like breathing around Berkshire." See [risk-vs-volatility](../concepts/risk-vs-volatility.md), [model-risk](../concepts/model-risk.md), [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md).

**★ A8 · "If we can't make a decision in five minutes, we can't make it in five months."** *Buffett:* the universe of actionable ideas is bounded tightly by what they already understand; anything that passes the initial filter gets a near-instant decision. "We can make a decision in five minutes very easily. … If we can't make a decision in five minutes, we can't make it in five months. We're not going to learn enough in the following five months to make up for the fact that we went in deficient in the first place." *Munger:* "There's such an enormous other lot of things that we won't allow ourselves to think about at all. … We don't do startups. They don't exist." Blotting out whole categories means the remaining territory, while smaller, is manageable. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**A11 · Coal dependency will be long-lasting; renewables are growing at Berkshire.** *Buffett:* in the short-to-medium term, the world uses more coal, and switching fast is impossible — "if you shut down the coal-generated utilities in the United States, we would not be able to hold this meeting in a room with the lights on." MidAmerican has added substantial wind capacity. *Munger:* better to burn coal than oil and gas — coal is less valuable as a chemical feedstock; the long-run answer is solar. Context on energy mix for the utility business. See [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md).

**★ A12 · Don't lump banks — DNA of the institution is what matters.** *Buffett:* "You should know something about the culture of the management and the institution to make a firm buy decision on a bank, and that's hard to do for 99 percent of the banks." Berkshire owns Wells Fargo, US Bank, and M&T — in all three cases he understands "quite well the DNA of the institution." Gene Abegg's Rockford bank: no matter what category it would be filed under, Abegg "could run nothing other than a super-sound bank." Banks misbehaving at the top "have cast a pall of disgrace over the whole industry" and pounded down stocks of small banks with nothing wrong. "There are more banks than bankers." *Munger:* "I think you're prospecting in a likely territory." ⚑ *Supersession:* This exchange was happening as the regional-bank sector was broadly depressed by the monolines crisis; the October 2008 TARP was still months away. The "DNA" framing proved correct — quality small banks navigated the crisis, while culture-impaired institutions did not. See [lending-discipline](../concepts/lending-discipline.md), [wells-fargo](../entities/wells-fargo.md).

**A14 · The one-car analogy: invest in yourself.** *Buffett:* the most important investment is in your own mind and body. "Imagine you're 16 and I was going to give you any car you wanted — but it's the only car you're going to get in the rest of your life." How would you treat it? "You get one body and one mind, and it's going to have to last you a lifetime." He asks university groups: if you were going to own 10% of one classmate for life, which would you pick? Not the highest IQ; the person other people want to work with and be around. Human qualities — generosity, honesty, punctuality, not overclaiming credit — are habits formed when young. *Munger:* recommended Cialdini's *Influence* for learning to recognise and resist manipulation.

**A16 · The U.S. may be too rich to need to save.** *Buffett:* the US savings rate has fallen and may be negative, yet real national wealth keeps rising decade to decade — "so something good has happened." The trade deficit ($700B in imported goods and services) means foreigners are doing Americans' saving for them in exchange for ownership claims. Consequences will come over time, "but we are so rich that it may not be really apparent." *Munger:* nothing to add. Interesting macro context; no actionable investment lesson stated.

**A17 · Berkshire goes to Germany to get on radar screens of family-business owners.** *Buffett:* Berkshire is not trying to get anyone to sell now — "most people shouldn't sell now." But when an event occurs that *does* cause a family to consider selling a multi-generational business, Berkshire wants to be the first call. It is more prominent in the US than Europe; the European tour is to correct that. The pitch: "if you care about your business, we are your best call." See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**★ A18 · The housing crisis was "a particularly foolish mess" — variations will recur.** *Buffett:* the residential real estate bubble created shock waves unlike any he could remember — exposing weaknesses elsewhere through propagation. "Some stupid things were done that won't be done soon again, and they won't be done exactly the same again, but variations of them will pop up at some point. Because humans are what lead to stupidity in behavior, and there are these sort of primal urges … in terms of getting rich and using leverage." *Munger:* "It was a lot smarter than what the people did in this mortgage field" — compared the mortgage stupidity unfavourably to the Webvan internet-grocery failure. "I just wish we had those brilliant people back that gave us Webvan." And: "The politicians are never so bad you don't live to want them back." See [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md), [institutional-imperative](../concepts/institutional-imperative.md).

**★ A19 · Value crisis-era assets at market; the CDO-squared madness.** *Buffett:* strongly prefers mark-to-market even when markets are dislocated — "you get into more troubles as a society when you start openly valuing things at prices that make no sense in terms of what's going on." The discipline of forced marking "may keep [managements] from doing things that they would otherwise do that would be very stupid." Anatomy of the CDO problem: a residential mortgage-backed security has thousands of mortgages and 30 tranches — a complex document. A CDO takes the junior tranches of 50 such securities — 15,000 pages to understand. A CDO-squared takes the junior tranches of 50 CDOs — requiring, in theory, 750,000 pages to evaluate one security. "That was madness." Valuing such a thing at cost rather than the market's ten cents on the dollar would be "an abomination." *Munger:* part of the trouble is people who "overdosed on Ayn Rand and what they learned in Economics 101" and believed that if something happened as a natural response of human nature in a free market, "even if it was an ax murder, it was a desirable development." Greenspan had that tendency. "I think there's some things that should be forbidden." See [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md), [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md), [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md).

**★ A23 · CDS market: dangerous, and probably not the trigger — but Munger more alarmed.** *Buffett:* the credit default swap market was reported at ~$60 trillion (with double-counting). Berkshire has written two types: insurance paying on default by companies in listed indices (high-yield series), and the long-dated equity put options. On systemic risk: a swap of dollars is different from a subprime loss — when one party is up $1B the other is down $1B, so it's a zero-sum transfer if counterparties pay. The Fed's Bear intervention "significantly reduced" the probability of a cascade. "I do not think — particularly if the Fed is going to step in — I don't think that will probably be the cause of the problem." *Munger:* "Could we have a big-time mess out of credit default swaps — is yes, we certainly could." The key perversity: if a $100M bond issue has $3B of CDS written against it, there are parties with $3B of incentive for the issuer to fail — potentially incentivizing manipulative behavior. "It used to be illegal for people who had no insurable interest just to buy life insurance on people they didn't know … because society was afraid that people would do that and then kill the person. And that happened in Los Angeles." To allow bets ten to twenty times the size of the underlying bond in an unregulated market is "crazy." ⚑ *Supersession:* Buffett's "probably not the trigger" hedged on Fed willingness to intervene; Lehman's September 2008 failure showed the Fed would NOT always step in, and the CDS market's scramble to establish claims (with Lehman as counterparty to hundreds of billions of CDS) was one of the most chaotic elements of the immediate crisis. Munger's alarm proved the better-calibrated read. See [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md), [model-risk](../concepts/model-risk.md).

**★ A24 · The dividend test: retain only when you can create more than a dollar per dollar retained.** *Buffett:* he believes in dividends in many situations, including for companies Berkshire owns stocks in. "The test about whether to pay dividends is whether you can continue to create more than one dollar of value for every dollar you retain." See's Candy distributes everything because it cannot use large sums intelligently in its own business — so retaining would be an error. Berkshire then takes that cash and reinvests where $1 becomes $1.10 or $2.10 in present-value terms. *Munger:* Berkshire plans to pay large dividends "in the mode of Saint Augustine when he said, 'God give me chastity, but not yet.'" See [dividend-policy](../concepts/dividend-policy.md), [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md), [sees-candies](../entities/sees-candies.md).

**A26 · Berkshire's international acquisitions: one or two in three to four years if lucky.** *Buffett:* odds of buying a major business outside the US in any given year are against them, but over three to four years he hopes for one or two. India and China restrict foreign insurance ownership to 25% — Berkshire is unlikely to enter at that ceiling. Europe is more promising. *Munger:* German engineering dominance — "you go into an American printing plant now, and the names on the machines are all German." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**★ A28 · Large institutional shareholders should withhold votes from egregious executive pay.** *Buffett:* individual shareholders can do little on executive pay, but the half-dozen largest institutional owners could move the needle: "if they would simply withhold their votes and issue a short statement as to why they're doing it, that has an effect — particularly on boards of directors. Big shots don't like to be embarrassed." The press is more effective at changing corporate behavior than Sarbanes-Oxley. Small shareholders' persuasive letters are turned over to the corporate secretary — it takes real, effective institutional pressure. *Munger:* "I would argue that, when you rise high enough in American business, you get a moral duty to be underpaid, not to get all you can, but to actually be underpaid." Generals, archbishops, and public servants accept low pay — why not leaders of great enterprises? See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md), [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

**A29 · Pharma: buy a group at a reasonable price, not individual pipeline bets.** *Buffett:* Berkshire cannot assess which pharma company will have a blockbuster drug three to four years from now, and doesn't try. "If we have a group of those companies bought at reasonable prices, that overall pharma will do well." In pharma — unlike banking — a group approach makes sense precisely because pipeline outcomes are unknowable. *Munger:* "You speak from a position where you have a monopoly of our joint knowledge about pharmacology." See [concentration-vs-diversification](../concepts/concentration-vs-diversification.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ A30 · Why Berkshire sold PetroChina: the price-value gap closed.** *Buffett:* bought PetroChina in 2002–2003 at an implied ~$35–40B market cap when he thought it was worth ~$100B; sold when oil rose and the implied value reached ~$275–300B — "we no longer felt it was undervalued compared to other oil companies." Shortly after sale the company became "the most valuable company in the world measured by market value" via the A-share listing. "If it went down to a price that was a significant discount to its valuation, we would buy PetroChina again." The exit was not a change of thesis — it was the thesis completing itself. See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [mistakes-of-omission](../concepts/mistakes-of-omission.md).

**A31 · Berkshire's fondest hope: culture outlasts tenure.** *Buffett:* two hopes — decent performance, and maintenance of a culture that is "both shareholder-oriented and manager-oriented" and is regarded as "the best home in the world for large, wonderful, family businesses." Performance and culture will be intertwined. *Munger:* would like to see Berkshire "even more deserved to be an exemplar" with more influence on corporate practices generally. Brief but thematic coda. See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

## Connections to other sources

- The Bear Stearns anatomy at A2 is the spoken companion to the "weapons of financial mass destruction" warning in [berkshire-2002-meeting](berkshire-2002-meeting.md) and the derivative-unwind account in the letters from the same period — the 2008 session shows the warning operating in real time rather than in theory.
- The auction-rate dislocation at M18 echoes the junk-bond opportunity of 2002 Buffett mentions in passing — both are examples of the "brief, extreme" dislocation pattern the 2002 session established; the 2008 municipal version is the more recent live case.
- The "genetically programmed against risk" criterion at A7 is the real-world application of the model-risk lessons embedded in the Gen Re acquisition narrative across [berkshire-2002-meeting](berkshire-2002-meeting.md) and [berkshire-2003-meeting](berkshire-2003-meeting.md) — the criterion was written in 2006 and validated by 2008.
- The Ajit municipal bond insurance account at M20 is the latest instalment of the float-building story first told in the reinsurance context — where the 2001 and 2002 sessions show Ajit building the Berkshire super-cat book from scratch, and this session shows him doing the same in a new line of business inside a crisis.
- The "diversification is for the know-nothing investor" at M29 sharpens the concentration-vs-diversification reasoning in [berkshire-1994-meeting](berkshire-1994-meeting.md)'s M50 and A11 — those earlier exchanges established the principle; Munger's 2008 formulation is the most unambiguous encapsulation of it in the corpus.
- The PetroChina exit account at A30 resolves the purchase story first told in the letters around 2002–2003 and demonstrates the valuation discipline operating symmetrically on exit as on entry — the counterpart to the 1994 meeting's Coke analysis, applied to a non-US business off a single annual report.
- The dividend-test formulation at A24 extends the owner-earnings and capital-allocation frameworks of the letters — the See's Candy example here is the same asset used in the letters to illustrate franchise economics, now deployed as the clearest statement of when dividends are *right*.
