# Berkshire Hathaway 2007 Annual Meeting

The 2007 meeting — held 5 May, three months before the August 2007 credit seizure — is the most historically charged in the corpus. Buffett and Munger had been warning about derivative leverage and mark-to-model accounting for years; here those warnings approach their most complete form on the very eve of their vindication. At the same time, their assessment of the subprime problem — diagnosis correct, systemic consequences underestimated — makes this the richest supersession opportunity in the post-2000 transcript run. The meeting also delivers Munger's most explicit account of what produced Berkshire's exceptional compounding ("a ferocious learner with power concentrated and a very long run"), the clearest short-form statement that volatility is not risk, Munger's forceful case for opportunity cost over hurdle rates, and a scathing but precise board-governance critique built on nineteen boards of lived experience. Context: ISCAR was acquired in 2006 and visited in the film; the Equitas (Lloyd's run-off) transaction closed at the end of Q1 2007; Berkshire would sell its PetroChina stake — acquired at ~$488M, sold for ~$3.5B — later in 2007.

## Key themes

### Derivatives: invisible leverage, the crowded trade nobody knows they are in, and deficient accounting

The 2007 meeting is the fullest pre-crisis Buffett-Munger statement of the derivative danger, and it proved accurate within months. Buffett's structural argument: derivative leverage is essentially unregulated because no one calls it margin. After the 1929 crash the Fed was empowered to regulate leverage and it was taken seriously for decades — "if you went to a bank and tried to borrow money on a stock, they made you sign certain papers." Derivatives and index futures have made those margin rules "a joke." The 1987 crash was a preview: portfolio insurance was a collective stop-loss order disguised as innovation; when it triggered, "a dead hand was selling as each level got hit" — a 22 percent shrink in one day. Now the same dynamic operates informally. Funds respond to the same stimuli without knowing they share a "crowded trade." "That's not a formal crowded trade. It's just that they're all ready to sell if a certain given signal or a certain given activity occurs." Coupled with extreme leverage, "you will someday get a very, very chaotic situation." Munger's accounting critique: mark-to-model lets traders report whatever profit they want by expanding positions. On the same contract, different auditors attest to different marks on each side. "As sure as God made little green apples, this is going to cause a lot of trouble in due course." See [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md) and [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md).

**Supersession:** Validated almost immediately. The August 2007 credit seizure, the 2008 collapse of structured credit, and AIG's derivatives book epitomized precisely the crowded-trade / mark-to-model dynamic Buffett and Munger had identified. The Gen Re derivatives portfolio — sold at a ~$400M loss from inflated marks — had already given Berkshire its own small preview.

### Volatility is not risk — and farmland proves it

The meeting's sharpest restatement of the risk-vs-beta argument uses an agricultural anchor unavailable in abstract financial theory. In the early 1980s, farmland that had sold for $2,000 an acre crashed to $600. Beta shot up. Standard finance theory says you were buying a riskier asset at $600 than at $2,000. "Now, people, because farmland doesn't trade often and prices don't get recorded, they would regard that as nonsense." In stocks, because "prices jiggle around every minute, and because it lets the people who teach finance use the mathematics they've learned," volatility gets translated into risk measures. "It's nonsense. Risk comes from the nature of certain kinds of businesses… and it comes from not knowing what you're doing." Munger: corporate finance and investment management courses "are at least 50 percent twaddle," and the reason smart people keep teaching it is that "it lets them use the mathematics they've learned." The practical record: Berkshire has essentially never suffered a permanent loss in marketable securities exceeding 1 percent of net worth — not because they avoided volatile securities, but because they understood the businesses they bought. See [risk-vs-volatility](../concepts/risk-vs-volatility.md).

### Subprime: securitization removes the discipline that local lending enforces

Buffett's May 2007 diagnosis of the subprime problem was mechanically precise. The core failure: securitization decoupled the originator from consequence. He had seen the exact template in manufactured housing six years earlier: "somebody in Grand Island, Nebraska, selling a mobile home to someone and they needed a $3,000 down payment and the salesman was going to get a $6,000 commission — the local banker would have seen what was happening. But once you just package those things and securitize them so they get sold through major investment banking houses and sliced up in various tranches, the old — the discipline leaves the system." He noted the prevalence of loans where borrowers made 20-30 percent of normal payments in year one, betting on price appreciation. Munger attributed the enablement to accounting: "if the accountants lay down on their basic job, huge excess and folly is going to come inevitably." Yet Buffett concluded the subprime problem was "unlikely to trigger anything of a massive nature in the general economy" and would "not be any huge anchor to the economy."

**Supersession:** This is the meeting's central historical irony. Buffett had every piece of the structural diagnosis correct — securitization destroying discipline, accounting enabling folly, the manufactured-housing prelude — but severely underestimated the systemic spread. The conduit was CDO-squared structures and repo-market collateral chains that had no equivalent in manufactured housing. Within three months, the August 2007 credit seizure proved the contagion global; by 2008 the crisis consumed far more capital than Buffett's "very big problem for those involved" framing. His conditional ("if unemployment doesn't rise significantly, and interest rates don't move up dramatically") was met — neither happened initially — but systemic bank-funding failure created a channel his framework didn't anticipate. See [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md), [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md), [lending-discipline](../concepts/lending-discipline.md).

### Extreme long-run results: concentrated power plus a ferocious learner with a very long run

Munger's most explicit account of Berkshire's compounding appears in response to an intrinsic-value question. "Berkshire has gotten very extreme, in terms of investment results. In fact, it's gotten so extreme that it's hard to think of another similar precedent in the history of the world." His explanation: "the young man who was reading everything he could read when he was 10-years-old became a learning machine, and he got a lot of power early, and then he got a very long run when he kept learning." Without continuous learning, "the record would be a pale shadow of what it is." The generalized lesson is structural: most successions pass power from one 65-year-old to another 59-year-old, capturing no compounding on the practitioner. "You get an enormous advantage from practice in this field." Buffett's corollary: at the capital-allocation scale Berkshire operates, "we won't get to do brilliant things… maybe once in a blue moon. But we will need somebody that never does basically any dumb things, and occasionally does something that's reasonably good. That can be done." The existing culture — owner-orientation, rationality, rejection of "animal spirits" — is structural and outlasts the individual. See [warren-buffett](../people/warren-buffett.md), [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

### Opportunity cost, not hurdle rate, is how real decisions get made

Munger's clearest pedagogy on investment decision-making: "The concept of opportunity cost is so little taught in investment. They teach it in the freshman course in economics in all the major universities, but when you get to the corporate finance departments and so forth, it doesn't lend itself with the kind of mathematics they want to use, so they ignore it." The hurdle rate is a formal abstraction that creates the illusion of rigor; the operating approach is simpler and more honest — compare each candidate to everything else available. "If I have something available that I think will give me 8 percent for sure and I can buy all I want of it, and you've got a perfectly good investment that I think will earn 7, I don't have to waste 5 minutes with you." Buffett: on 19 corporate boards, "every one of them had a calculation of internal rate of return — if they'd burned them all, the boards would have been better off," because the presenter knows the answer the CEO needs and reverse-engineers the inputs. See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md), [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md).

### Capital-light businesses are the only path to persistently high returns

Buffett's cleanest statement of the capital-intensity law: "You can't have a business that has huge capital expenditures year after year and end up with a high-return business. It just doesn't work in this world." The contrast is See's Candy — small, but "far better business, relative to size, than any steel business or oil business." This is why Berkshire has no bias toward commodity businesses despite holding POSCO and PetroChina: owning the best steel company in the world at four or five times earnings is fine as a value purchase, but it can never sustain the returns a capital-light franchise generates. The same logic governs inflation hedging: the best protection is your own earning power, "the second best is to own a wonderful business — not a metals business, not a raw material business — but a wonderful business that requires relatively low capital investment and has real pricing flexibility." See [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md), [sees-candies](../entities/sees-candies.md), [owner-earnings](../concepts/owner-earnings.md).

### Corporate governance: the CEO is the lever; the comp committee is the sideshow

Buffett's verdict from 19 boards: "There are more problems with having the wrong manager than with having the wrong compensation system." The board's three real jobs are (1) install the right CEO, (2) prevent CEO overreaching, and (3) exercise independent judgment on major acquisitions. That third function is where most boards fail most badly. By the time a major deal reaches the board, management has already committed to it; investment bankers then run a "little ritual" — "I have never seen one come in and make a presentation that says it's a dumb idea. I mean, they know what the answer is supposed to be." On stock deals specifically: "I have very seldom heard a discussion — in fact, I don't think I've virtually ever heard of a discussion — of weighing what you were actually giving away on a stock deal versus what you're getting." His self-indictment: Dexter Shoe was paid for with 2 percent of Berkshire stock — "2 percent of the present Berkshire Hathaway company. You'd all be 2 percent richer." Gillette ran ten consecutive deals that never met the case presented at the time; it never appeared in a shareholder report. See [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md), [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md), [stock-as-acquisition-currency](../concepts/stock-as-acquisition-currency.md).

## Q&A digest — Morning session

**M4 · Float at all-time high; underwriting profit is frosting.** *Buffett:* the true insurance model is to break even on underwriting and earn on the float — "what we really hope over time is more or less to break even on the underwriting of insurance." An underwriting profit is "frosting on the cake," not to be expected year after year. Float is at an all-time high as of Q1 2007; even at zero underwriting profit "we do very, very well because we generate lots of float and we earn money on that float." The Equitas transaction ($7B receivable at March 31, converted to cash and securities in early April) is noted as a one-time balance-sheet event. See [float](../concepts/float.md), [national-indemnity](../entities/national-indemnity.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**M5 · Private equity structure: not a bubble that "bursts."** *Buffett:* private equity cannot "burst" in the classic sense because positions are not priced daily and investors are locked up for five-plus years — "even if you do a poor job, it's going to take many years before the score is put up on the scoreboard." What slows activity is a widening of junk-to-high-grade credit spreads, which "has historically widened quite dramatically." Fee incentives (2 percent on committed capital) also push rapid deployment regardless of deal quality. *Munger:* "It can continue to go on a long time after you're in a state of total revulsion." Not a competitor Berkshire can out-work — Berkshire owns forever and prices conservatively. See [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md).

**★ M7 · CEO compensation is a "joke" — comp ratcheting driven by envy, not bargaining.** *Buffett:* "I ask you, when have you heard of a comp committee working until 4 in the morning, declaring an impasse for a week?" The CEO cares enormously; the comp committee treats it as "play money." The ratcheting mechanism: envy ("probably the dumbest of the seven deadly sins" because "you feel terrible and the other guy isn't bothered at all"), the HR department beholden to the CEO's view of them, the comp consultant beholden to the HR department's future referrals — "can you imagine that it's anything like a fair fight? It's a joke." Boards need Dobermans on comp committees, but "they're looking for cocker spaniels with their tails wagging." *Munger:* "And that reminds me of the old story where the mother asked the child why she told the census taker that the man of the house was in prison for embezzlement. And the child said, 'I didn't want to admit he was a compensation consultant.'" **Also M8 (pay for what's in their control):** the correct fix is simple: pay for things "under their control that we care about." An oil executive whose finding cost per barrel is $6 (vs. a peer's $10) earns big pay; one whose stock went up because oil moved from $30 to $60 "had nothing to do with it, and to hand them huge checks because oil goes up" is exactly wrong. See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md), [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

**M9 · Credit contraction: "extraordinary things happen when people panic."** *Buffett:* LTCM in fall 1998 — "all kinds of people with high IQs on Wall Street, all kinds of people with cash available" — produced "some really extraordinary things happen in credit markets simply because people panicked and they felt other people were going to panic." Those "second- and third-degree type reactions" will recur. "As Mark Twain said, history doesn't repeat itself but it rhymes." *Munger:* the last credit contraction let Berkshire "make a quick 3 or $4 billion." A "really big credit contraction" after excess also produces legislation "most of us wouldn't like." See [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

**M11 · Corporate profits at record highs — unsustainable, and partly built on consumer credit.** *Buffett:* corporate profits at 8-plus percent of GDP are unprecedented outside "maybe two or three years in the last 75." The math of many banks earning 20-plus percent ROE on what is "basically a commodity — money — we would have said that condition just wouldn't persist." *Munger:* much of the profit sits in the financial sector — banks, investment banks, investment management. Consumer credit has been "pushed to extremes that we've never before seen in the history of this country. Korea had one [collapse], which caused chaos for two or three years." "I don't think this is a time to just swing for the fences." **Supersession:** corporate profit margins and consumer credit stress did break sharply in 2008-09.

**★ M14 · How to be a better investor — the Gottesman test and the yellow pad.** *Buffett:* "read everything you can" and then actually invest — "like the difference between reading a romance novel and doing something else." A framework formed at 19 still governs at 76. *Munger:* Sandy Gottesman (Berkshire director) asks every candidate, no matter how young: "What do you own and why do you own it?" If the answer isn't there, "he'd rather you go somewhere else." *Buffett:* the yellow pad exercise — before buying 100 shares of GM at $30, write: "I'm going to buy the General Motors company for $18 billion. Here's why." If you can't give a good essay, you have no business buying any shares. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md).

**★ M15 · For a great business, you don't need a huge margin of safety.** *Buffett:* "We favor the businesses where we really think we know the answer." For a business whose competitive position is too uncertain to project five to twenty years, Berkshire doesn't try to compensate with a larger discount — it moves to something it understands better. For a business like See's Candy or Coca-Cola, "we don't think we need a huge margin of safety because we don't think we're going to be wrong about our assumptions in any material way." The "fat" test: "If you see somebody walk in the door, you don't know whether they weigh 300 pounds or 325. You still know they're fat. If we see something that's fat, financially, we don't worry about being precise." *Munger:* margin of safety "boils down to getting more value than you're paying. And that value can exist in a lot of different forms." "High school algebra." See [margin-of-safety](../concepts/margin-of-safety.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ M17 · What drove Berkshire's extreme success — concentrated power and a ferocious learner.** *Munger:* "Berkshire has gotten very extreme, in terms of investment results. In fact, it's gotten so extreme that it's hard to think of another similar precedent in the history of the world." His explanation: the young man who was reading everything he could read at age 10 "became a learning machine, and he got a lot of power early, and then he got a very long run when he kept learning." Without continuous learning, "the record would be a pale shadow." Most corporations pass power "from one 65-year-old to one 59-year-old… but you get an enormous advantage from practice in this field." *Buffett:* the existing culture of rationality and owner-orientation "will go on long after I'm not around." The successor doesn't need to be brilliant — "basically doesn't do any dumb things, and occasionally does something that's reasonably good. That can be done." See [warren-buffett](../people/warren-buffett.md), [charlie-munger](../people/charlie-munger.md), [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**★ M18 · Derivatives: invisible leverage, the crowded trade, and deficient accounting.** *Buffett:* derivatives and index futures have made margin regulation "a joke" — they introduce "more and more leverage into the system" that is "largely invisible." The 1987 crash illustrates: portfolio insurance was a collective stop-loss order that when triggered "a dead hand was selling as each level got hit" — a 22 percent single-day move. Now funds with "billions in aggregate, trillions of dollars, leveraged… will respond to the same stimulus. They have what we would call a crowded trade, but they don't know it." "I have no idea when. I have no idea what the exogenous factor will be. But it will happen." Gen Re portfolio: when unwinding derivatives positions, Berkshire found reserves running into the hundreds of millions — "I just wish I'd sold the portfolio to the auditors that day. I'd be 400 million better off." *Munger:* mark-to-model accounting lets traders report phantom profits indefinitely by expanding positions. "Most of the accounting profession doesn't even recognize how stupidly it is behaving." The same auditing firm often attests to the mark on both sides of the same contract; the two marks do not sum to zero, and "it won't be to the disadvantage of the trader on the other side." "As sure as God made little green apples, this is going to cause a lot of trouble in due course." **Supersession:** validated within 18 months — CDO marks, AIG credit default swaps, repo-market collateral chains, and the Lehman collapse all fit the crowded-trade / mark-to-model template precisely. See [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md), [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md), [model-risk](../concepts/model-risk.md), [general-re](../entities/general-re.md).

**★ M19 · The electronic herd, short-term mandates, and Gaussian distributions in markets.** *Buffett:* "There is an electronic herd of people around the world managing huge amounts of money who think that a decision on everything in their portfolio should be made, basically, daily or hourly or by the minute." NYSE turnover has risen from ~15-20 percent forty years ago to over 100 percent. These participants "are playing a different game" with different consequences — when measured weekly, you watch what the next person is about to do and try to hit the key faster. "I think it's a fool's game." *Munger:* the idea that market disasters follow Gaussian distributions "makes everything so easy to compute." But "they don't follow Gaussian distributions. You have to believe in the Tooth Fairy to believe that. And the disasters are bigger and more irritating than Gauss would have predicted." Teaching obsolete methods persists because "it's so wonderful to teach." *Buffett:* five-sigma and six-sigma events are meaningless when you get human behavior involved — "intelligent, educated people do things that are totally irrational, and they do them en masse." See [risk-vs-volatility](../concepts/risk-vs-volatility.md), [model-risk](../concepts/model-risk.md).

**★ M20 · The farm analogy and Aesop — all investing is "a bird in the hand."** *Munger:* intrinsic value cannot be computed by "a simple mechanical formula." It takes "multiple techniques and multiple models, and a lot of experience." You can't become a great investor rapidly any more than you can become a great bone-tumor pathologist rapidly. *Buffett:* the farm analogy — estimate yield per acre (120 bushels corn, 45 bushels soybean), expenses, and what you'd pay for $70/acre net income — is exactly what business valuation is. "The mathematics of investment were set out by Aesop in 600 BC. He said, 'A bird in the hand is worth two in the bush.'" The only questions are: when do we get the two? How certain are there two (or more)? What is the right discount rate? "We measure one against the other that way." *Munger:* "We throw almost all decisions into the too-hard pile, and we just sift for a few decisions that we can make that are easy. And that's a comparative process." *Buffett:* "We know how to step over one-foot bars. We don't know how to jump over seven-foot bars. But we do know how to recognize, occasionally, what is a one-foot bar." See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ M21 · What Buffett wants in portfolio managers — wired to see risks that haven't occurred yet.** *Buffett:* "We're not looking for someone to teach. We're looking for somebody that we think knows how to do it." The key filter: "we really are interested in being sure that we have somebody that, under conditions that people haven't even seen yet, will not blow it. Anything times zero is zero. And I don't care how many wonderful figures are in between." They want someone "wired in a way that they see risks that other people don't see that haven't occurred." In 1969, three stand-out candidates: Munger, Sandy Gottesman, and Bill Ruane. In 1979, [Lou Simpson](../people/lou-simpson.md) for GEICO — "a few hours at the airport, it was clear he was a steward of capital." *Munger:* the Mozart anecdote — when a young man asked for advice on writing symphonies, Mozart said, "You're too young." Young man: "But you were writing them at ten." Mozart: "Yes, but I wasn't asking anybody else for advice on how to do it." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [concentration-vs-diversification](../concepts/concentration-vs-diversification.md).

**★ M24 · Easy decision: stocks over bonds for 20 years.** *Buffett:* "If I were managing a very large endowment fund, for one thing it would either be a hundred percent in stocks or a hundred percent in long bonds or a hundred percent in short-term bonds." No layering: "I don't believe in layering things and saying I'm going to have 60 percent of this and 30 percent of that. Why do I have the 30 percent if I think the 60 percent makes more sense?" Forced to choose between the S&P 500 index and a 20-year bond for 20 years, "it would not be a close decision with me. I would buy the equities." Not because stocks can't go down a lot, but because the comparative return expectation over that horizon is clear. *Munger:* "That's the answer." *Buffett:* "We've bought $5 billion worth of equities in the first quarter… they're not anywhere like 1974, but we decided we would rather have them than cash." See [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md), [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

**M27 · With small capital, do entirely different things.** *Buffett:* "If I were working with a very small sum — and you should all hope I'm not — I would be doing almost entirely different things." The universe of options is "thousands and thousands of times as many" with $10,000 than with $100 billion. "You can earn very high returns with very small amounts of money, and it will always be such." With a million or two, "we would find little things here and there — and it wouldn't always be stocks — where we would earn very high returns on capital." *Munger:* "There's no point our thinking about that now." See [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md), [cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses](../concepts/cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses.md).

**★ M28 · Subprime won't be "huge anchor" — the mechanism right, the systemic reach wrong.** *Buffett:* the subprime problem follows the manufactured-housing template: securitization decoupled origination from consequence. "Once you just package those things and securitize them so they get sold through major investment banking houses and sliced up in various tranches, the old — the discipline leaves the system." He diagnosed the fundamental error: loans where borrowers can only make 20-30 percent of normal payments in year one are implicitly betting on price appreciation — "that worked for a while until it didn't work." Yet: "I don't think it's going to be any huge anchor to the economy" — conditional on unemployment and rates not spiking. *Munger:* "a lot of what went on was a combination of sin and folly," enabled by accountants "who allowed lending institutions to show profits on loans where nobody in his right mind would have showed any profit." "I don't see how the people did it and still shaved in the morning, because looking back at them was a face that was evil and stupid." **Supersession:** Buffett's conditional proved too narrow. Securitization had spread subprime exposure throughout money-market funds, repo markets, and bank balance sheets globally — channels his manufactured-housing frame didn't capture. August 2007 credit seizure and the 2008 system freeze were orders of magnitude larger than "very big problem for those involved." His structural diagnosis was correct; his containment call was not. See [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md), [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md), [lending-discipline](../concepts/lending-discipline.md).

## Q&A digest — Afternoon session

**★ A1 · "Areas don't make opportunities; brains make opportunities."** *Buffett:* in response to a question about managed futures funds — "any entity devoted to a limited segment of the financial market we would regard as being at a disadvantage." Berkshire buys futures, bonds, currencies, and businesses — the universe is fully open. "There is no form that produces investment results. Hedge funds don't produce investment results. Private equity doesn't produce investment results. Mutual funds don't produce it." What matters: "whether the person running it knows what their limitations are, knows where their strengths are, plays when they have the opportunity to play advantageously, and stays out when they don't see any opportunities." *Munger:* the average return per dollar per year from managed futures funds would be "somewhere between lousy and negative." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**A2 · Read, find inefficient markets, avoid catastrophe.** *Buffett:* "Our best ideas have not been better than other people's best ideas, but we've never had a lot of things that pulled us way back. We never went two steps forward and one step back. We probably went two steps forward and a fraction of a step back." Avoiding catastrophes is as important as finding great ideas. The RTC was an example of an inefficient market: a seller with no economic interest in the assets, during a period when the natural buyers had been wiped out. *Munger:* "When you're young, look in the inefficient markets. You shouldn't be trying to guess whether one drug company has a better drug pipeline than another." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [mistakes-of-omission](../concepts/mistakes-of-omission.md).

**A3 · Florida catastrophe insurance — the political tussle over socialized wind risk.** *Buffett:* Florida has expanded its public reinsurance fund and is moving risk from private to public market, which depresses private rates and frees up capacity. The long-run problem: "there is no free lunch, and the state and the citizens of Florida are taking a tremendous amount of risk." Joe Brandon (General Re CEO) at the meeting: Florida previously assumed ~$18B, added another $12-16B. If a $100B storm hits: "The state may issue 60 or 70 billion of bonds, they may go to the federal government and say this really isn't our fault." Berkshire writes less Florida exposure than it did — sees moral-hazard risk in government re-underwriting. See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md), [super-cat-insurance](../concepts/super-cat-insurance.md), [national-indemnity](../entities/national-indemnity.md).

**A4 · Still looking for elephant-sized acquisition.** *Buffett:* buying $5.3B in stocks in Q1 2007 doesn't signal abandoning hope of a big acquisition — "we're as prepared as we've ever been to buy a big business outright." Also acquired TTI (passive electronic components, run by Paul Andrews) in Q1 — "I wish it was five times the size, but maybe it will be someday." *Munger:* "the one thing I think we can promise is that we won't make returns, on average, in the kind of stuff we're buying now like those that we made 10 or 15 years ago. It's a different world with more modest expectations." See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md).

**★ A6 · "Volatility is not a measure of risk."** *Buffett:* "The problem is that the people who have written and taught about risk do not know how to measure risk. The nice thing about beta… is that it's nice and mathematical and wrong in terms of measuring risk." The farmland example: Nebraska farms that sold for $2,000 an acre crashed to $600 in the early 1980s — beta shot up. "According to standard economic theory, I was buying a much more risky asset at $600 an acre than the same farm at $2,000. Now, people would regard that as nonsense." But in stocks, daily prices allow economists to do the same thing unchallenged. Risk actually comes from "the nature of certain kinds of businesses" and from "not knowing what you're doing." Berkshire has essentially never suffered a permanent loss in marketable securities exceeding 1 percent of net worth. *Munger:* "It's at least 50 percent twaddle… One of the reasons we've been able to do pretty well is that we early recognized that very smart people do very dumb things, and we tried to figure out why, and wanted to know who so we could avoid them." See [risk-vs-volatility](../concepts/risk-vs-volatility.md), [margin-of-safety](../concepts/margin-of-safety.md).

**★ A7 · What an annual report tells you about management quality.** *Buffett:* on a large oil company whose annual report never disclosed finding cost per barrel — "the most important figure in an oil and gas company over a period of years, but every year counts." The omission was intentional; where it was touched on, it was done "in a dishonest manner." Finding costs under the executives' control should be what drives their pay; the price of oil is not. The annual letter test: "if it's clearly the product of some investor relations department or outside consultant… that tells you something about the individual. If he's not willing to talk once a year through a few pages to the people who gave him their money to invest, I've got some questions." *Munger:* "There are two things: the quality of the business and the quality of the management. If the business is good enough, it will carry a lousy manager. And the converse — a really good manager in a really lousy business — he'll ordinarily have a very imperfect record." See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md), [franchise-vs-business](../concepts/franchise-vs-business.md).

**A8 · Stocks over bonds — modest expectations, but better than 4.75 percent.** *Buffett:* the 1999 Fortune article used two extreme 17-year periods for dramatic contrast; nothing is magic about that span. Writing now, he would say: equities over 20-year bonds, but "I would not have high expectations." He does not expect a repeat of 1982-1999 — "people were bound to be disappointed. They simply had an unrealistic view by extrapolation." *Munger:* "Warren has been right so far, and he's probably right now when he says modest expectations." See [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md).

**★ A11 · Beware "it's so easy" — the trust filter.** *Buffett:* when evaluating people, "just the very things they talk about, what they regard as important and not important, there are a lot of clues that come as to subsequent behavior." Over ninety percent are ruled out. "The important thing is whether the ones we're ruling in we're right about." In 1969, there were dozens of hedge fund managers with good records, but Buffett identified only three he would trust with his partners: Munger, Gottesman, Ruane. *Munger:* "we're deeply suspicious when the proposition is too good to be true." He was once introduced to a promoter who wanted to write fire insurance "on concrete bridges covered by water." He said, "It's like taking candy from babies." *Buffett:* "Anytime I find somebody with what I regard as an unfair fee structure, and saying, 'Well, I can get it,' I rule them out." See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md), [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**★ A12 · Opportunity cost, not hurdle rate, is the right framework.** *Munger:* "The concept of opportunity cost is so little taught in investment. They teach it in the freshman course in economics in all the major universities, but when you get to the corporate finance departments and so forth, it doesn't lend itself with the kind of mathematics they want to use, so they ignore it." "The trouble with the hurdle rate concept — not that we don't have one in a sense — is it doesn't work as well as a system of comparing things. If I have something available that I think will give me 8 percent for sure and I can buy all I want of it, and you've got a perfectly good investment at 7, I don't have to waste 5 minutes with you." *Buffett:* "I've been on 19 corporate boards. I would say that of the presentations I've seen — and every one of them had a calculation of internal rate of return — if they'd burned them all, the boards would have been better off." Because "presenters know what listeners are desirous of hearing and what is needed in order to get through something the CEO wants to do anyway." See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md), [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md).

**A14 · Thinking all the time — portfolio review frequency.** *Buffett:* in the partnership days with more ideas than money, he thought about every position constantly — "my opportunity cost was the least attractive stock which I would give up to buy something more attractive." Now with more money than ideas, "we're not re-examining something every minute because the option is cash and not doing something we're really excited about." Continuous accumulation of information nonetheless: "every business we're in — whether wholly owned or partially owned through stocks — we think about them all the time. We keep getting little incremental bits about that company or the competition." *Munger:* even then, Buffett spent little time on his number-one choice — "he could put that aside and devote his efforts to other subjects." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**A15 · It is genuinely hard to buy billions of dollars of stock.** *Buffett:* "we usually can buy something like 20 percent of the daily trading volume and feel that we're not causing the price to be violently different." Buying $5B of a given stock means $25B must trade — "a lot for many stocks." *Munger:* when accumulating Coca-Cola, Berkshire was buying 30-40 percent of daily trading and "it took us a long, long time to get our position." "We have no easy way of moving these elephants around." The ocean liner disadvantage. See [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md), [coca-cola](../entities/coca-cola.md).

**A22 · Best hedge against inflation: your own earning power, then a wonderful capital-light business.** *Buffett:* metals investing is not necessarily protection against inflation. The hierarchy: (1) your own earning power — "somebody that is a first-class surgeon, or lawyer, or teacher, or salesperson, whether the currency is seashells or paper money, will do all right"; (2) own a wonderful business — "not a metals business, not a raw material business, but a wonderful business" that people keep paying for out of current income and that requires relatively low capital reinvestment to maintain pricing. Coke, Snickers, Hershey bars. Berkshire itself would not do as well in real terms under high inflation but "would do better than a good many companies." See [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md), [bonds-and-inflation](../concepts/bonds-and-inflation.md).

**A25 · Global competitors matter; don't buy high-labor businesses exposed to offshore competition.** *Buffett:* "We do not want to buy into a business that has a very high labor content and that has a product that can be shipped in from abroad very easily. Because sooner or later, that will probably be trouble." USAir was the example — 12 cents per seat-mile versus Southwest's 8 cents — "they get there." *Munger:* the shoe business taught this lesson painfully. "Will Rogers believed that you should learn not to pee on an electrified fence without actually trying it." See [commodity-business-economics](../concepts/commodity-business-economics.md), [dexter-shoe](../entities/dexter-shoe.md), [usair](../entities/usair.md).

**★ A27 · Corporate directors aren't doing their real job — and stock deals are the worst failure.** *Buffett:* three jobs for a good director: (1) ensure you have the right CEO; (2) ensure the CEO isn't overreaching; (3) exercise independent judgment on major acquisitions. On (3): "by the time it gets there, they've made the deal anyway. They have investment bankers there who go through a little ritual. I've never seen one come in and make a presentation that says it's a dumb idea." On stock deals specifically: "Most people are giving a significant percentage of the business which they wouldn't sell [at current market prices]… but they hand away a piece of the business because they want to own something else. You just have to be sure you're getting as much as you're giving. I have very seldom heard — in fact, I don't think I've virtually ever heard of — a discussion of weighing what you were actually giving away on a stock deal versus what you're getting." Dexter Shoe: "When I gave away 2 percent of Berkshire Hathaway to acquire Dexter Shoe, that was one of the dumbest deals in the history of the world." Gillette: "literally, we had ten deals in a row that never came close to meeting the case presented at the time they were presented to the board. Was that ever mentioned to shareholders? Never. It never will." *Munger:* "The self-serving delusional nature of even some very good minds… is amazing." The best board Buffett sat on (Data Documents): "every one of them had a significant percentage of their net worth in the business. Every decision was made for business reasons." See [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md), [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md), [stock-as-acquisition-currency](../concepts/stock-as-acquisition-currency.md).

**A28 · Why Berkshire avoids deal partners.** *Buffett:* "We normally don't want to do deals with partners. If we like a deal, we want to own it all." No money partner needed; no knowledge partner wanted — "we really wouldn't want to be going into something where we were relying on somebody else to be the brains of the deal." One exception: the Leucadia partnership, which Leucadia brought to Berkshire. Managers as partial owners in their businesses are a different case — "they're in on the same terms we are, and they're owners." See [passive-ownership-advantage](../concepts/passive-ownership-advantage.md), [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**★ A29 · No opinion on commodities; prefer capital-light businesses that can earn high returns.** *Buffett:* "We basically like best the businesses that require very little capital, because they're the only ones that have a chance of earning really high returns on capital. You can't have a business that has huge capital expenditures year after year and end up with a high-return business. It just doesn't work in this world." See's Candy is small but "far better business, relative to size, than any steel business or any oil business." Berkshire has "no bias toward businesses involved in commodities — if anything, a bias against." POSCO was purchased on value terms (four to five times earnings, debt-free, lowest-cost producer) and a currency play (Korean won), not as a commodity bet. *Munger:* "We're going to be investors in businesses, not commodities, by and large. And that has to work better over time." See [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md), [sees-candies](../entities/sees-candies.md), [return-on-equity-yardstick](../concepts/return-on-equity-yardstick.md).

**A30 · Dual-class shares at the New York Times — the newspaper structural decline is the real issue.** *Buffett:* "I don't think I'd necessarily blame the Sulzbergers for the woes of the newspaper business." The structural problem is that if Gutenberg were proposing the newspaper business today — "chop down trees, haul them great distances, run them through expensive newsprint machines all night, send delivery trucks through the snow to deliver pieces of paper about what happened yesterday" — no rational investor would back it. World Book sales fell from 300,000 sets to 22,000 sets; it isn't that the product isn't worth the price, "it's just not worth what it sells for to most people who can go on the internet and get an awful lot of that information free." The Buffalo News — among the highest-penetration large metropolitan papers — is still seeing earnings down over 40 percent from peak. *Munger:* once a dual-class structure was in the initial contract, "the idea that you can just stamp your foot and take away the contract is a kind of immature idea." See [media-economics](../concepts/media-economics.md), [buffalo-evening-news](../entities/buffalo-evening-news.md).

**A34 · PetroChina / Darfur shareholder proposal — the investment logic.** *Buffett* (on the subsidiary-cannot-control-parent principle): "Control flows downward. Berkshire controls Wesco. We can tell Wesco what to do. Wesco cannot tell Berkshire what to do. The same is true of PetroChina and CNPC — the Chinese government owns 88 percent of PetroChina and controls it; PetroChina does not and cannot control CNPC." On the "sell to force change" argument: "All that would be doing is giving a bargain to somebody else who is buying the stock. It doesn't change the funds available to them at all." The full Darfur debate — from multiple shareholders and religious organization representatives — is the meeting's longest single topic. The investment logic of the subsidiary-vs-parent distinction is noted here; the underlying political and ethical debate is off-domain per the wiki's scope. The vote: ~98 percent against divestiture (roughly 25-to-1 margin). *Note:* Berkshire sold its full PetroChina position later in 2007, realizing approximately $3.5 billion against an original cost near $488 million — an exit driven by valuation, not the Darfur resolution. See [berkshire-hathaway](../entities/berkshire-hathaway.md).

## Connections to other sources

- The derivatives danger articulated at M18 is the direct continuation of the 2002 meeting's "financial weapons of mass destruction" framing and the 2002 and 2003 letters, adding in 2007 the "crowded trade nobody knows they're in" mechanism and the mark-to-model dual-auditor fraud. Within months, [berkshire-2008-letter](berkshire-2008-letter.md) would recount the crisis from inside it.
- The subprime diagnosis at M28 — securitization removes discipline; manufactured housing is the template — has its earliest antecedent in [berkshire-2006-meeting](berkshire-2006-meeting.md)'s "earliest stick-built mortgage forecast in corpus." The 2007 meeting is the fullest statement; the 2008 letter provides the post-crisis verdict.
- The "volatility is not risk" / farmland argument at A6 restates [berkshire-1994-meeting](berkshire-1994-meeting.md)'s canonical M50 ("risk is permanent loss over the horizon, beta is not risk") with a concrete agricultural example unavailable in any prior meeting or letter, making it the most teachable single illustration in the corpus.
- Munger's "ferocious learner" portrait at M17 is the analytical complement to the character portrait in [berkshire-2005-letter](berkshire-2005-letter.md) (Charlie's approach to multi-disciplinary learning) and elaborates the continuous-improvement theme running through [charlie-munger](../people/charlie-munger.md).
- The corporate-governance / stock-deal critique at A27 is the spoken companion to the institutional-imperative and stock-as-acquisition-currency discussions threading through the 1980s and 1990s letters, here made most personal through the Dexter and Gillette confessions.
- The capital-intensity law at A29 grounds the abstract "great-good-gruesome" taxonomy (introduced in the letters) in the concrete POSCO/See's comparison, making it the best single reference for that distinction in the meeting corpus.
