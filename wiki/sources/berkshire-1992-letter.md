# Berkshire Hathaway 1992 Annual Letter

The 1992 letter is the most analytically concentrated of the early-1990s series. Three standalone essays define it: a full discounted-cash-flow argument dissolving the "growth vs. value" false dichotomy; the canonical articulation of margin of safety as the "cornerstone of investment success"; and the most sustained early case that stock options are an expense — a fight the accounting profession would not resolve for another twelve years. Together they form a self-contained curriculum in valuation discipline. Per-share book value grew 20.3%.

## Key themes

**Growth and value are joined at the hip.** The letter's central intellectual contribution is a full-dress rejection of the "growth vs. value" taxonomy as a category error. The framework: any asset's value is the present value of future cash inflows and outflows discounted at an appropriate rate — the John Burr Williams equation from *The Theory of Investment Value* (1938). Under that framework, growth is a variable in the cash-flow estimate, sometimes positive and sometimes negative. A business that requires incremental capital but earns below-market returns on it *destroys* value with each unit of growth. Airlines are the defining counter-example: decades of industry expansion made shareholders progressively poorer because capital consumed exceeded capital returned. Buffett's corollary: "value investing" is redundant as a label — all rational investing is value investing. The phrase "value investor" describes not a style but any investor who bothers to calculate.

**Margin of safety: cornerstone, not technicality.** This letter delivers the standalone articulation that the margin-of-safety concept page treats as canonical: "If we calculate the value of a common stock to be only slightly higher than its price, we're not interested in buying." The discipline is inseparable from intellectual honesty about limits. Predicting future cash flows requires that a business be "relatively simple and stable in character"; for complex or rapidly changing businesses, the right response is not to try harder but to stay out. "What counts for most people in investing is not how much they know, but rather how realistically they define what they don't know. An investor needs to do very few things right as long as he or she avoids big mistakes." Margin of safety and circle of competence are, in this letter, explicitly one framework.

**Stock options are an expense.** The 1992 letter is the definitive middle chapter of Buffett's options-accounting campaign. The three-step syllogism: if options aren't compensation, what are they? If compensation isn't an expense, what is it? If expenses don't go into earnings, where do they go? The lobby's counter-arguments — options are hard to value; out-of-the-money options have no value at issuance; expensing injures start-ups — are addressed on a common principle: accounting is full of imprecision (depreciation, loan-loss provisions, casualty reserves), and imprecision has never before been accepted as grounds to omit a major cost from earnings. The offer: Berkshire would buy any "worthless" option at fair value. The accounting profession and the SEC would capitulate to management lobbying for another decade before FASB mandated expensing in 2004.

**Float: sub-government-rate funding in 21 of 26 years.** Hurricane Andrew generated roughly $125 million in super-cat losses — approximately equal to Berkshire's entire annual super-cat premium income — yet the insurance operations still funded themselves at 4.76% against a 7.39% long-term government bond yield. In 21 of Berkshire's first 26 years in the insurance business, float cost less than U.S. Treasury long-term borrowing. The super-cat operation, run by Ajit Jain, rejects over 98% of offered business. Buffett's verdict: Jain is "simply the best in this business." Hurricane Andrew is framed not as a disaster but as confirmation of how the model should be expected to work — years of near-zero cost punctuated by occasional large losses.

**Acquisition discipline: patience, then decisiveness.** Two lessons from the 1992 acquisition activity. First, patience: Buffett compares the acquisition search to choosing a spouse — "it pays to be active, interested and open-minded, but it does not pay to be in a hurry." The toad-kissing parable targets acquisition-hungry managers who overpay for mediocre businesses and compound the error ("practice doesn't make perfect; practice makes permanent"). Second, the General Dynamics episode is the mirror lesson: how to recognize quality mid-stream and move decisively. Buffett began buying General Dynamics as a pure arbitrage on a Dutch tender offer; when he studied CEO Bill Anders's "clearly articulated and rational strategy," he abandoned the arbitrage mindset entirely and used the tender's elevated trading volume to accumulate 14% of the company in a single month. Slow with toads; fast with great managers.

## Notable quotes

> In our opinion, the two approaches are joined at the hip: Growth is always a component in the calculation of value, constituting a variable whose importance can range from negligible to enormous and whose impact can be negative as well as positive.

> Growth benefits investors only when the business in point can invest at incremental returns that are enticing — in other words, only when each dollar used to finance the growth creates over a dollar of long-term market value. In the case of a low-return business requiring incremental funds, growth hurts the investor.

> What is "investing" if it is not the act of seeking value at least sufficient to justify the amount paid? Consciously paying more for a stock than its calculated value — in the hope that it can soon be sold for a still-higher price — should be labeled speculation (which is neither illegal, immoral nor — in our view — financially fattening).

> What counts for most people in investing is not how much they know, but rather how realistically they define what they don't know. An investor needs to do very few things right as long as he or she avoids big mistakes.

> If we calculate the value of a common stock to be only slightly higher than its price, we're not interested in buying. We believe this margin-of-safety principle, so strongly emphasized by Ben Graham, to be the cornerstone of investment success.

> If options aren't a form of compensation, what are they? If compensation isn't an expense, what is it? And, if expenses shouldn't go into the calculation of earnings, where in the world should they go?

> Moreover, it is both silly and cynical to say that an important item of cost should not be recognized simply because it can't be quantified with pinpoint precision.

> Practice doesn't make perfect; practice makes permanent.

> It pays to be active, interested and open-minded, but it does not pay to be in a hurry.

## Connections to other sources

- The growth-vs-value essay explicitly cites the 1977 annual report: "Our equity-investing strategy remains little changed from what it was fifteen years ago, when we said in the [1977 annual report](berkshire-1977-letter.md): 'We select our marketable equity securities in much the way we would evaluate a business for acquisition in its entirety.'" The 1992 essay extends that credo into a full DCF argument and names John Burr Williams as the theoretical anchor.
- The margin-of-safety articulation here is the canonical statement in the early series; [berkshire-1990-letter](berkshire-1990-letter.md) canonized the Graham quote ("Forty-two years after reading that…") while this letter integrates it with the DCF framework and explicitly ties it to the circle-of-competence prerequisite.
- The stock-options essay is the middle chapter: [berkshire-1985-letter](berkshire-1985-letter.md) mounted the structural critique of fixed-price options; 1992 is the accounting-honesty argument; [berkshire-1994-letter](berkshire-1994-letter.md) presents the Ralph Schey arrangement as the positive alternative model.
- The General Dynamics pivot — arbitrage-to-long-term-stake via one month of tender-elevated trading volume — directly parallels the Wells Fargo accumulation in [berkshire-1990-letter](berkshire-1990-letter.md): both use a transient market event to build an outsized stake quickly, then commit to indefinite ownership.
- Look-through earnings ($604M in 1992, target $1.8B by 2000) extend the framework introduced in [berkshire-1990-letter](berkshire-1990-letter.md); the explicit 15% compounding target becomes the capital-allocation benchmark framing all portfolio decisions through the mid-1990s.
