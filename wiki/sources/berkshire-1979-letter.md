# Berkshire Hathaway 1979 Shareholder Letter

The most philosophically rich of the first three letters. Operating earnings of 18.6% on beginning equity — good but slightly below 1978's 19.4%. Book value per share compounded at 20.5% annually from September 1964 ($19.46) to yearend 1979 ($335.85). Two original frameworks debut: the [investor-misery-index](../concepts/investor-misery-index.md) (inflation + tax as the true test of real returns) and a prescient extended critique of long-term bonds as structurally unsuitable instruments in an inflationary world. The [turnarounds-seldom-turn](../concepts/turnarounds-seldom-turn.md) principle crystallizes via an honest admission about Waumbec Mills.

## Key themes

- **Investor's misery index.** Even a 20% compounded ROE produces near-zero real purchasing power gains at 14% inflation once taxes on nominal returns are factored in. Buffett defines the misery index as: inflation rate + tax cost of converting nominal returns to cash. When the index exceeds the business's ROE, real capital shrinks even if you consume nothing. See [investor-misery-index](../concepts/investor-misery-index.md).
- **Long-term bonds as obsolete instruments.** In an inflationary world, every other commercial contract has moved to floating or annually-reviewed pricing — except the long-term bond. The insurance industry's contradiction: switching to 6-month auto policies (can't predict 12 months of costs) while buying 30–40 year bonds (locking in money's price for decades). Buffett's policy: avoid straight long-term bonds; use convertibles for the embedded exit option. See [bonds-and-inflation](../concepts/bonds-and-inflation.md).
- **Turnarounds seldom turn.** Waumbec Mills admitted as a mistake: bought below working capital, "got very substantial amounts of machinery and real estate for less than nothing" — but structurally broken businesses stay broken. "The same energies and talent are much better employed in a good business purchased at a fair price than in a poor business purchased at a bargain price." See [turnarounds-seldom-turn](../concepts/turnarounds-seldom-turn.md).
- **Insurance cycle worse than expected.** Industry combined ratio rose from 97.4% to 100.7% in 1979. Buffett's contrarian forecast: high interest rates will create a "new threshold of tolerance" for underwriting losses, pushing the industry average combined ratio to ~105 over the next five years. An early description of what later becomes known as "float at negative cost during soft markets."
- **Owner communication philosophy.** The reporting-to-owners principle stated at length: Buffett should report directly (not via PR consultants), build on prior letters, match the candor of internal management reporting. Phil Fisher's restaurant analogy: companies attract the shareholder constituency they seek and deserve.

## Notable quotes

> Just as the original 3% savings bond, a 5% passbook savings account or an 8% U.S. Treasury Note have, in turn, been transformed by inflation into financial instruments that chew up, rather than enhance, purchasing power over their investment lives, a business earning 20% on capital can produce a negative real return for its owners under inflationary conditions not much more severe than presently prevail.

> Both our operating and investment experience cause us to conclude that "turnarounds" seldom turn, and that the same energies and talent are much better employed in a good business purchased at a fair price than in a poor business purchased at a bargain price.

> Having decided that one year is too long a period for which to set a fixed price for insurance in an inflationary world, [insurers] then have turned around, taken the proceeds from the sale of that six-month policy, and sold the money at a fixed price for thirty or forty years.

> Neither a short-term borrower nor a long-term lender be. — Polonius, restated.

> In some businesses — a network TV station, for example — it is virtually impossible to avoid earning extraordinary returns on tangible capital employed in the business. Despite a fancy price tag, the "easy" business may be the better route to go.
