# Berkshire Hathaway 2021 Annual Meeting

Held May 1, 2021, virtually but staged from Los Angeles so that Charlie Munger rejoins the table after his COVID-year absence. Greg Abel and Ajit Jain are both on stage throughout; Becky Quick relays pre-submitted shareholder questions with no live floor. Five historically significant moments distinguish the 2021 meeting from its predecessors: Munger's unscripted on-stage confirmation of Greg Abel as Berkshire's succession heir; the sharpest formulation yet of interest rates as the gravitational constant of all asset valuation; an early real-economy inflation alarm — "we're seeing very substantial inflation" — months before most forecasters acknowledged it; the bluntest SPAC and fee-driven-market critique the pair had given to that point; and Buffett's explicit self-indictment for trimming Apple, called "probably a mistake" with Munger's blunt "Yes" as confirmation. The transcript has a clear morning/afternoon split (item numbering resets after M31), giving the M#/A# convention used throughout; M1–M9 are the introductory opening remarks before Q&A begins at M10.

## How to read the Q&A digest

- **★** marks a timeless, load-bearing exchange worth citing in the future; unmarked entries still carry investor value but are secondary or period-specific.
- Answers are attributed separately to **Buffett**, **Munger**, **Abel**, and **Jain** — a named line appears only when that person added a distinct lesson (Munger returned to full participation this year after being absent in 2020).
- **M#/A#** map to the numbered items in the raw morning/afternoon transcript for traceability. M1–M9 are pre-Q&A introductory remarks; Q&A opens at M10.

## Key themes

### The top-20 companies vanish over thirty years — certainty is the enemy of humility

Buffett opens with two slides (L-1 and L-2) addressed explicitly to the wave of new retail investors who joined markets in 2020. L-1 shows the 20 largest companies by market cap on March 31, 2021, led by Apple at just over $2 trillion — five of the top six American. L-2 shows the same list from 1989: "None of the 20 from 30 years ago are on the present list. None. Zero." Japan dominated the earlier list; it has zero companies on the 2021 list. The U.S. had six then — GE, Exxon, IBM, Merck, and others still operating today — yet none survived the cut. The moral is double-edged: broad equity ownership over thirty years was "absolutely the place to be," a vindication of index-fund participation. But stock-picking did not vindicate itself — the companies that seemed like the obvious winners failed to sustain. The auto-industry parallel makes the same point from the other direction: over 2,000 companies entered the business (just the MA-segment fills a slide); in 2009, three remained, and two went bankrupt. Knowing a great industry is coming tells you almost nothing about which company to buy. "It's not as easy as it sounds." See [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

### Interest rates are to asset values what gravity is to matter

The most compressed formulation of the discount-rate principle in the entire meeting corpus: "interest rates, you know, basically are to the value of assets what gravity is to matter, you know, essentially." Buffett produced a 1973 Paul Samuelson economics textbook — the definitive work of its era — whose index entry for "zero interest rates" declared them theoretically conceivable but practically impossible. Yet that very week, the U.S. Treasury sold four-week bills at an average price of exactly $100.0000000 — six zeroes — and Berkshire's $100B+ in T-bills earned "20 million" instead of the $1.5B earned pre-pandemic. Under that gravity-free regime, companies that generate enormous cash on tiny capital bases — Apple, Google, Microsoft — are not irrationally priced; they are "very, very cheap" *if* near-zero rates persist. The pivotal uncertainty is "And then what?" — large-scale fiscal stimulus combined with near-zero monetary policy had never been tested before, and Buffett and Munger said plainly: "we don't know what the consequences are." See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md).

### SPACs and fee-driven buying — a structural killer for permanent capital

Competition from SPACs is "a killer" for Berkshire's acquisition strategy. The mechanism is incentive design: SPACs must deploy capital within two years, creating forced buyers who will overpay rather than return money. Private equity shares the architecture: "if you're running money for somebody else, and you're getting paid a fee, and you get the upside, and you don't have a downside, you're going to buy something." The result is what Keynes diagnosed in 1936 — read aloud by Buffett from a slide: "When the capital development of a country becomes a by-product of the activities of a casino, the job is likely to be ill-done." Munger frames it as a civilizational defect: "I call it fee-driven buying… the more of that you get, the sillier your civilization is getting. And to some extent, it's a moral failing, too. … It's not just stupid, it's shameful." The practical consequence for Berkshire: roughly $70–80B in deployable capital that "we'd love to put to work" but that will not find a home "under these conditions." See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

### "Very substantial inflation" — Berkshire's businesses as a real-time early-warning system

A question about raw-material costs in May 2021 draws the plainest early-warning of what became the inflation surge of 2022–2023: "We're seeing very substantial infla — it's very interesting. I mean, we're raising prices. People are raising prices to us. And it's being accepted." Across nine homebuilders, manufactured housing, the Nebraska Furniture Mart, Dairy Queen, and BNSF freight, costs were "up, up, up" — steel especially. The remarkable detail: Berkshire's companies were caught off-guard. "We weren't expecting it." The post-COVID demand surge kept accelerating past the expiry of the initial stimulus checks and beyond all internal forecasts. Abel adds a physical scarcity layer: the Texas winter storm had knocked out petrochemical plants supplying much of the country, compressing supply at the same moment demand surged. "It is not a price-sensitive economy right now, in the least. And I don't know exactly how one shows up in different price indices. But there's more inflation going on than — quite a bit more inflation — going on than people would have anticipated just six months ago." Munger: "There's one very intelligent man who thinks it's dangerous. And that's just the start." This exchange, dated May 1, 2021, is among the earliest documented ground-level warnings from a major corporate source of what CPI statistics would confirm in 2022. See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

### "Greg will keep the culture" — Munger's unscripted succession confirmation

Asked whether Berkshire's decentralized model can survive at scale, Munger and Buffett affirm it. But Munger, in the middle of his answer, makes an unguarded statement that resolves the succession question no shareholder had been able to force a direct answer to: "But Greg is — … And Greg will — and Greg will keep the culture." Buffett interrupts with "— and it's dependent on it" but neither corrects nor qualifies Munger's statement. For the first time on record, the heir to the CEO role is named in public at the annual meeting, not by design but by Munger's characteristic impatience with indirection. Buffett's deflection — "As Charlie says to me, you won't know" — is the close. *Supersession flag:* Abel was formally confirmed as designated CEO successor at the 2023 annual meeting. Munger died November 28, 2023. See [greg-abel](../people/greg-abel.md), [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

### Selling great businesses is almost always a mistake — Apple as exhibit A

Berkshire trimmed its Apple position in 2020, reducing to roughly a 5.3% stake. Buffett called it plainly: "I sold some stock last year, although our shareholders still had their percentage interest go up because we repurchased shares. But that was probably a mistake." Munger, when asked if he agreed: "Yes." Buffett: "I kind of used them up between Costco and Apple. And he was very likely right in both circumstances. It's an extraordinary business." The company description justifying the regret: Tim Cook is "one of the best managers in the world," Apple has near-100% customer satisfaction, and customers confronted with an alternative device simply "can't be sold" it. The lesson generalizes: once you own an extraordinary business run by an extraordinary manager, the threshold for exit is far higher than the threshold for entry, and a sale requires a level of certainty that almost never exists. *Note:* In 2023, Buffett sold far more Apple (reducing to ~2.4% stake), giving this regret an unresolved sequel the 2023 meeting and letters will address. See [mistakes-of-omission](../concepts/mistakes-of-omission.md), [cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses](../concepts/cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses.md), [apple](../entities/apple.md).

### Capital-light businesses are the universal dream — and correctly priced as such

"We've always known that the dream business is the one that takes very little capital and grows a lot." The 2021 data point: Apple has $37B in property, plant, and equipment; Berkshire has $170B — yet Apple earns far more. Google, Microsoft, and Facebook show the same structure. Berkshire discovered the principle in 1972 when it bought See's Candies: "See's Candy just doesn't require that much capital. It doesn't have big inventories, except very seasonably for a short period. It doesn't have a lot of receivables." The corollary: "they command the best prices, too. And there aren't that many of them. And they don't always stay that way." The constraint on acquiring them is not awareness — "everybody's looking for them. That's what capitalism is about" — it is that the price will reflect what everyone already knows. See [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md), [return-on-equity-yardstick](../concepts/return-on-equity-yardstick.md), [sees-candies](../entities/sees-candies.md).

## Q&A digest — Morning session

**★ M7/M8 · Top-20 companies: none from 1989 survived to 2021 — index investing vindicated, stock-picking humbled.** *Buffett:* Presents L-1 (2021 top-20 by market cap) and L-2 (1989 top-20): zero overlap. Japan had multiple entries in 1989; zero in 2021. U.S. had six then (GE, Exxon, IBM, Merck, others) — none survived the cut. The auto-industry parallel: 2,000+ companies entered (just the MA-names fill a slide); two of the three survivors went bankrupt in 2009. "The main thing to do was to be aboard the ship… you couldn't help but do well if you just had a diversified group of equities — U.S. equities would be my preference — to hold over a 30-year period." Lesson: industry foresight is not stock-picking foresight. "It's not as easy as it sounds." See [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**M10 · Airlines exit defended — "I still wouldn't want to buy the airline business."** *Buffett:* Sold the entire ~10% positions across the four major airlines in early 2020. In hindsight "it would have been better to be buying" broadly — "I do not consider it a great moment in Berkshire's history." But: (1) a major Berkshire stake would have complicated the government relief the airlines needed ("you might not have gotten the same result"); (2) the entire big-four combined was selling for less than $100B — "they wouldn't be in the top 50" by market cap. Structural dislike unchanged: international travel still impaired; business travel uncertain. "I still wouldn't want to buy the airline business." *Munger:* on Berkshire's ability to deploy at the crisis bottom — "almost nobody else can, either." *Buffett:* "Not with tens of billions… Or hundreds of billions" — the scale of Berkshire's deployable capital, not just the timing, is what narrows the field to essentially no one. See [usair](../entities/usair.md).

**M11 · "We don't want to depend on anybody" — self-reliance under uncertainty.** *Buffett:* Did not aggressively deploy the cash hoard in late March 2020 because the Fed had not yet acted, money markets were running, and the government bond market was disrupted — "a run on money market funds, a very substantial run" echoing September 2008. "The one thing about Berkshire is we never want… we don't want to depend on anybody. We're not a bank. We can't go to the Federal Reserve if we need money." The anti-model: Blanche DuBois in *A Streetcar Named Desire* — "I depend on the kindness of strangers." *Munger:* "It's crazy to think anybody's going to be smart enough to husband money and then just come out on the bottom tick in some crazy crisis and spend it all." The self-reliance standard justifies large cash balances that critics read as laziness. See [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md).

**M12/M13 · Buffett recommends the S&P 500 index to individuals — never Berkshire.** *Buffett:* "I've never recommended Berkshire to anybody. Because I don't want people to buy it because they think I'm tipping them." His estate instructions: 90% to an S&P 500 index fund for his widow (less than 1% of the estate), 10% Treasury bills. The 99.7% going to philanthropy: he would prefer that remain in Berkshire. Two different vehicles for two different purposes — the index for a widow who needs simplicity; Berkshire for patient lifetime capital. "The average person cannot pick stocks." See [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md), [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**M14 · Chevron — moral lines on stocks are difficult to draw and easily inconsistent.** *Buffett:* Owns Chevron without compunction. Parallel: once declined a chewing-tobacco acquisition that was "probably the best business we've ever seen" — a personal moral line they don't regret. But hydrocarbons are different in kind, the causal chain to harm is less direct, and "there's something about every business that, if you knew it, you wouldn't like." "If you own an index fund, you're going to own Chevron. Believe me, Chevron is not an evil company in the least." *Munger:* would prefer a son-in-law from Chevron over an English professor "sight unseen." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**M18 · Insurance: "Practically all of the surprises are unpleasant."** *Jain:* Contract certainty is the core vulnerability — both long-tail and property policies can be "tortured" by regulators and courts, "and normally when they are tortured, they end up going against the insurance industry." Boy Scouts example: claims grew from under 2,000 to nearly 100,000 after states unilaterally extended statutes of limitations, "funded by plaintiff lawyers who are now very well-funded." *Buffett:* "Most of those surprises in insurance, practically all of them, are unpleasant." Berkshire's self-imposed ceiling: willing to lose up to $10B in a single event — but only where that was the priced exposure; the failure mode is a $10B loss from a position priced for $50M. See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md), [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md).

**M20 · Abel and Jain's working relationship — culture check, not only business update.** *Jain:* The Buffett-Munger relationship is "unique and not going to be duplicated." He and Abel talk every quarter on their respective businesses; cross-refer mid-quarter for unusual situations. *Abel:* Beyond business: "any time we see anything unusual in one of our businesses, it's Ajit who I'm going to call and say, are you comfortable that we're taking this approach? Is it going to be consistent with how you think about it in insurance?" Culture maintenance is the durable function. See [greg-abel](../people/greg-abel.md), [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md).

**M23 · GEICO missed telematics; catching up on margins but not yet on growth.** *Jain:* Progressive had margins and growth rates "almost twice as much as GEICO's" a year ago. GEICO's brand and expense discipline remain leading. But: "GEICO had clearly missed the bus and were late in terms of appreciating the value of telematics" — matching rate to individual driving behavior. Telematics initiatives are underway. *Buffett:* "Five years from now, it's very likely that the top two will be GEICO and Progressive. And in which order, we'll see." *Supersession flag:* GEICO's telematics deficit proved deeper and more persistent than indicated; underwriting losses ran through 2022–2023 with Todd Combs installed as CEO to address the structural problem. See [geico](../entities/geico.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**★ M24 · Apple sale "probably a mistake" — Munger confirms in one word.** *Buffett:* Trimmed Apple to ~5.3% stake in 2020. "I sold some stock last year, although our shareholders still had their percentage interest go up because we repurchased shares. But that was probably a mistake." Tim Cook assessed as "one of the best managers in the world." The iPhone has ~99% customer satisfaction; customers at the Nebraska Furniture Mart "can't be sold" an Android if they want Apple. *Munger:* "Yes." *Buffett:* "I kind of used them up between Costco and Apple. And he was very likely right in both circumstances. It's an extraordinary business." See [mistakes-of-omission](../concepts/mistakes-of-omission.md), [apple](../entities/apple.md), [cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses](../concepts/cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses.md).

**★ M25 · Interest rates are to asset values what gravity is to matter.** *Buffett:* "Interest rates, you know, basically are to the value of assets what gravity is to matter, you know, essentially." Produced a 1973 Paul Samuelson economics textbook declaring zero rates theoretically conceivable but practically impossible. Yet the four-week T-bill that week cleared at $100.0000000 — six zeroes — and Berkshire's $100B+ T-bill portfolio earned "20 million" instead of the $1.5B earned pre-pandemic. Under that gravity-free regime, capital-light cash-generating companies like Apple, Google, and Microsoft are "very, very cheap" if near-zero rates are appropriate. The pivotal unknown: "We've never really seen what shoveling money in, on the basis that we're doing it… [with] something close to zero interest rates" produces. "You can never do one thing. You always have to say, 'And then what?'" *Munger:* Professional economists "were so confident about everything. It turns out the world is more complicated than they thought." See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md).

**M26 · Munger: MMT may be more feasible than thought, but has hard limits.** *Munger:* "The Modern Monetary Theorists are more confident than they ought to be, too." His reading: extreme fiscal-monetary stimulus "is more feasible than everybody thought" — but "if you keep just doing it without any limit, it will end in disaster." Notes that Larry Summers is "courageous" for raising the inflation concern as nearly the only voice doing so. A contemporaneous marker from May 2021 of where Munger stood before inflation materialized. See [bonds-and-inflation](../concepts/bonds-and-inflation.md).

**★ M28 · SPACs: "It's a killer" — fee-driven buying makes permanent capital uncompetitive.** *Buffett:* SPACs must deploy within two years: "If you put a gun to my head and said, 'You've got to buy a big business in two years,' you know, I'd buy one. But it wouldn't be much of one." This is the private-equity problem in concentrated form — "you get the upside, and you don't have a downside, you're going to buy something." Read aloud Keynes (1936): "Speculators may do no harm as bubbles on the steady stream of enterprise. But the position is serious when enterprise becomes the bubble on a whirlpool of speculation. When the capital development of a country becomes a by-product of the activities of a casino, the job is likely to be ill-done." *Munger:* "I call it fee-driven buying… the more of that you get, the sillier your civilization is getting. And to some extent, it's a moral failing, too. … It's not just stupid, it's shameful." Practical consequence: "We're not going to have much luck on acquisitions while this sort of period continues." See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

**M29 · $70–80 billion "we'd love to put to work."** *Buffett:* Holds roughly 10–15% of total assets in cash beyond the safety minimum — "70 or 80 billion, something like that, maybe, that we'd love to put to work." Cannot deploy it at current prices without overpayment. "Conditions change very, very, very rapidly sometimes in markets." The consolation: "we're very happy about the other 700 billion." See [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md), [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

## Q&A digest — Afternoon session

**★ A1 · Share repurchase morality: motivation is everything — "deeply immoral" vs. "highly moral act."** *Buffett:* Repurchases distribute cash to the owners who want liquidity while others reinvest — analogous to four partners buying out the one who wants to leave, at a fair price, when the other three want to keep building. "I find it almost impossible to believe some of the arguments that are made that it's terrible to repurchase shares from a partner if they want to get out of something and you're able to do it at prices advantageous to the people who are staying." The Berkshire shareholder vote: ~97% chose no dividend. *Munger:* "If you're repurchasing stock, just to bull it higher, it's deeply immoral. But if you're repurchasing stock because it's a fair thing to do in the interest of your existing shareholders, it's a highly moral act. And the people who are criticizing it are bonkers." See [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md), [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**A2 · Tax law changes would not alter the no-dividend policy.** *Buffett:* Even at a 43.4% capital-gains rate, Berkshire's ownership base (assembled over 55 years as a lifetime savings vehicle) has voted ~97% for reinvestment over dividends. The tax regime does not define what the right policy is; the composition of owners does. Secondary. See [dividend-policy](../concepts/dividend-policy.md).

**★ A8/A9 · Pandemic risk "totally, totally underpriced" — the systemic-correlation failure.** *Jain:* "I think the big lesson for us, having gone through what we've gone through recently, is that, while we were aware of the fact that pandemic risk is a risk factor, it was totally, totally underpriced by all of us in the industry." The industry assumed once-in-a-hundred-years; even that proved far too optimistic. The correlation failure: pandemic risk was priced in individual lines (life insurance, event-cancellation) but never aggregated across the full portfolio. Olympics-cancellation insurance was priced against earthquake and terrorism — "we would never factor something like what portion of the price should come from the pandemic exposure." Berkshire's COVID reserve as of Q1 2021: $1.6B; industry estimate of ultimate losses: ~$100B — "our number, therefore, of 1.6 that we have as of now, is going to be a lot, lot higher." See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md), [super-cat-insurance](../concepts/super-cat-insurance.md), [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md).

**★ A11 · Successful acquisitions naturally concentrate; disappointments naturally shrink.** *Buffett:* On Precision Castparts overpayment: "Berkshire didn't make the mistake, I made the mistake." But the broader portfolio principle matters more: "when we're disappointed in a business, it usually becomes a smaller and smaller percentage of our business, just by the nature of things, because it isn't going anyplace. And when we get a successful business… they become a proportionally much more important part of our mix. So, you really get, through just natural forces, you get more of your money in the things that have developed more favorably than you thought. You actually end up getting a greater concentration in the ones that work out." The three original Berkshire businesses — textiles, a department store, trading stamps — all failed. The conglomerate endured because success compounded forward automatically. See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [mistakes-of-omission](../concepts/mistakes-of-omission.md).

**★ A12 · "The number one risk factor" — getting the wrong management; never in any prospectus.** *Buffett:* Every prospectus lists "risk factors" but omits the most important: "The number one risk factor — you never see it — the number one risk factor is that this business gets the wrong management. And you get a guy or a woman in charge of it that are — they're personable, the directors like them. They don't know what they're doing, but they know how to put on an appearance." A decade of mismanagement from such a person is more destructive than any industry headwind the prospectus does list. Reinforces Berkshire's refusal to cede CEO selection to process or committee. See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [institutional-imperative](../concepts/institutional-imperative.md), [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

**A14 · Munger: "Of course, I hate the Bitcoin success."** *Munger:* "Of course, I hate the Bitcoin success. And I don't welcome a currency that's so useful to kidnappers and extortionists and so forth, nor do I like just shoveling out a few extra billions and billions and billions of dollars to somebody who just invented a new financial product out of thin air. So, I think I should say modestly that I think the whole damn development is disgusting and contrary to the interest of civilization." *Buffett*, who had declined to answer to avoid antagonizing the large Bitcoin-holding viewer base: "I'm all right on that one!" Secondary for investment analysis; the quote is frequently cited. See [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md).

**★ A17 · Capital-light businesses are everyone's dream — and priced accordingly.** *Buffett:* "We've always known that the dream business is the one that takes very little capital and grows a lot." Apple has $37B in PPE; Berkshire has $170B — Apple earns far more. Google, Microsoft, Facebook show the same structure. Berkshire found the principle at See's Candies in 1972: "See's Candy just doesn't require that much capital." The corollary: "they command the best prices, too. And there aren't that many of them. And they don't always stay that way." The constraint is not awareness — "everybody's looking for them. That's what capitalism is about. People getting a return on capital." See [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md), [return-on-equity-yardstick](../concepts/return-on-equity-yardstick.md), [sees-candies](../entities/sees-candies.md).

**A19 · Munger on China: "They deserve a lot of credit."** *Munger:* China's leadership looked at the prosperity of places like Singapore and "just accepted Adam Smith and added it to their communism." Lifted 800 million people from poverty — "there was never anything like it in the history of the world." Deng Xiaoping's "black cat, white cat" pragmatism praised. *Buffett:* Three of the 20 most valuable companies are Chinese; probably more in thirty years. Secondary; documents where Munger stood in May 2021 before BYD's 2022–2023 run made the investment household news. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**A24 · National Indemnity's unique franchise narrowing — the market caught up.** *Buffett:* after 9/11, only one or two firms could write massive catastrophe limits at speed and be unquestionably good for the check — "perhaps AIG, when Hank Greenberg was there, he would do the same thing," but the field was tiny and Berkshire's word-is-good reputation was a genuine moat. *Jain:* that edge has eroded from the supply side, not just demand: "There are a lot of people who can put up big limits — not as much as we do — but they can syndicate a program and put up a billion dollar very easily. So that competitive advantage we had, we still have, but it's no longer as big a deal as it used to be." A durable lesson on moats: a capacity advantage built on scarce balance-sheet strength decays as capital markets learn to syndicate around it. See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md), [national-indemnity](../entities/national-indemnity.md), [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md).

**★ A26 · The danger of CEO myths — corporate prattle that becomes belief.** *Buffett:* One of the most underappreciated risks in business: a CEO who repeats a narrative about the company — through investor-relations channels, analyst calls, quarterly scripts — until it is internalized as true even when it is wrong. "Nobody's going to go on two months after the CEO has said one thing and say, well, actually that really isn't the way." The false belief then passes to the successor CEO who cannot contradict the departed leader. *Munger:* cites British actor Sir Cedric Hardwicke: "I have been a great actor for so long, that I no longer know what I truly think on any subject." And: "The way you prattle out all the time, you're pounding back in even if it's wrong." This is the mechanism by which the institutional imperative does its damage — not conspiracy, but repetition becoming conviction. See [institutional-imperative](../concepts/institutional-imperative.md), [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

**★ A27 · Haven failed — "we were fighting a tapeworm in the American economy and the tapeworm won."** *Buffett:* Haven (the Berkshire/JPMorgan/Amazon healthcare JV) was disbanded in early 2021. The structural reason: the U.S. spends 17% of GDP on healthcare vs. no other major country above 11%; Singapore spends 20% of the U.S. per-capita rate and achieves better outcomes. The withholding-tax insight from Beardsley Ruml (1941) explains the structural barrier: because employers pay healthcare as a hidden pre-tax cost, most Americans never see the price and feel no pressure to reform the system. "We were fighting a tapeworm in the American economy and the tapeworm won." Haven did achieve internal efficiency gains for Berkshire — "we found some dumb things we were doing" — but failed at the macro ambition. *Munger:* "You were at least shooting at an elephant." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**★ A28 · Munger unintentionally confirms Abel succession — "Greg will keep the culture."** *Munger:* While explaining why Berkshire's decentralized structure can scale indefinitely, unprompted: "But Greg is — … And Greg will — and Greg will keep the culture." *Buffett* interrupts: "— and it's dependent on it. I mean —" *Munger:* "And the culture is part of it. But assuming we keep the culture, it can go on quite a ways." Neither corrects nor qualifies Munger's designation. For the first time on record at an annual meeting, the succession heir is named — not by design but by Munger's characteristic impatience with circumlocution. Buffett's close: "As Charlie says to me, you won't know." *Supersession flag:* Abel was formally confirmed as designated CEO successor at the 2023 annual meeting. Munger died November 28, 2023. See [greg-abel](../people/greg-abel.md), [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [charlie-munger](../people/charlie-munger.md).

**A29 · "You really got to be in love with your business."** *Buffett:* Describing Abel and Jain's capacity for absorbing enormous information about their businesses: "they're terribly interested in it. I mean, you know, and it's theirs." And: "you really got to kind of be in love with your business. And that makes a huge difference. And that means that we've got to have the conditions that allow that love to flourish." The TTI example: Paul Andrews ran the business for 15 years, octupled earnings, and each year when offered a raise or bonus responded, "We'll talk about that next year, Warren." Berkshire's management model depends on finding people who feel this way — and then not interfering. See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md), [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**A30 · Robinhood and the casino critique — "God-awful… deeply wrong."** *Buffett:* Robinhood has become "a very significant part of the casino aspect of the market." 12–13% of its users trade puts and calls. "I looked up on Apple… the number of seven-day calls and 14-day calls outstanding. And I'm sure a lot of that is coming through Robinhood. And that's a bunch of people… gambling on the price of Apple over the next seven days or 14." No condemnation of individual gamblers: "gambling is a very human instinct." The indictment is structural — "the degree to which a very rich society can reward people who know how to take advantage, essentially, of the gambling instincts of the… worldwide public — it's not the most admirable part of the accomplishment." *Munger:* "I think it's just God-awful that something like that would draw investment from civilized men and decent citizens. It's deeply wrong. We don't want to make our money selling things that are bad for people." "The states replaced the Mafia as the proprietor of the numbers game." See [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md), [investment-friction](../concepts/investment-friction.md).

**★ A31/A32 · "Very substantial inflation" — Berkshire's businesses as an early-warning system.** *Buffett:* "We're seeing very substantial infla — it's very interesting. I mean, we're raising prices. People are raising prices to us. And it's being accepted." Steel costs up daily; carpet prices rising; supply chains "all screwed up." Across homebuilders, furniture stores, Dairy Queen, BNSF: "it's red-hot." "There's more inflation going on than — quite a bit more inflation — going on than people would have anticipated just six months ago." *Abel:* Scarcity compounds demand: the Texas winter storm took down petrochemical plants the rest of the country depends on, adding physical shortage on top of stimulus-driven demand. *Munger:* "There's one very intelligent man who thinks it's dangerous. And that's just the start." This exchange, May 1, 2021, is among the earliest public corporate-level warnings of what became the 2022 inflation surge. See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [bnsf](../entities/bnsf.md).

**A34 · Portfolio turnover: "There's way too much."** *Munger,* unprompted: When asked whether buy-and-hold philosophy had changed given recent equity portfolio turnover: "There's too much. It's still too much." *Buffett* agrees. The operated businesses ($400–500B) never turn over; turnover resides entirely in the equity portfolio. "We'd do better if I'd done less." A recurring theme in Munger's running critique of Buffett's trading. See [concentration-vs-diversification](../concepts/concentration-vs-diversification.md).

## Connections to other sources

- The self-reliance / Blanche DuBois framing at M11 directly echoes [berkshire-2020-meeting](berkshire-2020-meeting.md) Q20, where the same passage appeared in the context of the airline sale; here it gains the fuller context of the money-market run and the narrow window before the Fed acted in March 2020.
- The "interest rates as gravity" formulation at M25 is the spoken companion to the rate-sensitivity discussions in annual letters; this meeting produces the most compressed, quotable statement of the discount-rate principle in the corpus — Samuelson's textbook as the foil makes it memorable.
- The SPAC/casino/fee-driven-buying critique at M28/A30 extends the derivatives critique in [berkshire-2002-meeting](berkshire-2002-meeting.md) and the "healthcare tapeworm" from [berkshire-2017-meeting](berkshire-2017-meeting.md) to a new instrument that directly threatens Berkshire's acquisition model — a sharper framing than in any prior year.
- The Apple "probably a mistake" self-indictment at M24 anticipates a paradox: Buffett then substantially reduced the Apple position in 2023, making the 2021 regret incomplete; the 2023 meeting and [berkshire-2023-letter](berkshire-2023-letter.md) will need to address this evolution.
- The inflation observations at A31/A32 form a chain: [berkshire-2020-meeting](berkshire-2020-meeting.md) flagged the QE-to-inflation risk as theoretical; this meeting makes it concrete from Berkshire's operating companies — "red-hot" costs — months before CPI statistics caught up.
- Munger's A28 succession confirmation resolves a question shareholders had pressed since [berkshire-2019-meeting](berkshire-2019-meeting.md), when Abel and Jain first appeared on stage together for the first time; 2021 is when Munger ended the ambiguity, unintentionally but clearly.
