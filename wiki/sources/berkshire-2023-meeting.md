# Berkshire Hathaway 2023 Annual Meeting

May 6, 2023 · Omaha — Warren Buffett, Charlie Munger, Greg Abel (non-insurance operations), and Ajit Jain (insurance) on stage; Abel and Jain present for the morning session, Buffett and Munger returning alone after lunch. Three crises converged on this meeting: Silicon Valley Bank's March 2023 collapse and the first smartphone-speed bank run; the rise of ChatGPT and large-language-model AI; and Berkshire's own rapid exit from Taiwan Semiconductor, completed within months of purchase. Munger's commercial-real-estate warning proved early but prescient. The durable lessons run through banking-system fragility and misaligned CEO incentives; why Apple's ~$150 billion position is not over-concentrated when measured as a business stake rather than a portfolio weight; why geopolitical risk demanded an unusual fast reversal on TSMC; how de-dollarization and fiscal excess threaten even a reserve currency; and how Greg Abel's mandate now explicitly includes capital allocation. This proved to be Charlie Munger's final annual meeting; he died November 28, 2023, aged 99. His contributions here — on banking culture, AI skepticism, China trade, and investing wisdom — are captured fully.

## Key themes

### Banking runs at smartphone speed: the system's messaging has "been very poor"

What changed between the bank panics of 1907 and 2023 is not the psychology — "fear is contagious, always" — but the mechanics. A 1930s depositor had to stand in a physical queue; today "you know, press a button, you don't have to get in a line… you're going to have a run in a few seconds." SVB collapsed over a weekend. Buffett was categorical: allowing SVB's depositors to take losses "would've been catastrophic." Yet the system's communication around deposit safety has been "very poor" from politicians, agencies, and the press alike. The $250,000 FDIC ceiling has never described how the U.S. government actually behaves, so every regional shock triggers fresh confusion. Buffett offered a $1 million bet, unreturned, that demand depositors at any U.S. bank will not lose their money regardless of account size. The right fix is explicit universal guarantee — and punishing the executives who cause failures. At First Republic, the fatal strategy was "in plain sight" in the 10-K: fixed-rate jumbo mortgages on long terms, handing borrowers the optionality. "The CEO gets the bank in trouble, both the CEO and the director should suffer. The stockholders of the future shouldn't suffer. They didn't do anything." *Munger:* "a banker should be more like an engineer. He's more, like, into avoiding trouble than he is getting rich." See [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md), [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md), [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md).

### Apple as a business stake, not a portfolio weight

The question Munger pre-empted ("I think he's out of his mind") was whether Apple's ~35% of Berkshire's equity portfolio represents dangerous concentration. Buffett dissolved the framing: "Apple is not 35% of Berkshire's portfolio. Berkshire's portfolio includes the railroad, the energy business… they're all businesses." Apple is simply a better business than BNSF or any wholly-owned Berkshire subsidiary — the iPhone has a grip on consumers such that "if they had to give up a second car or give up their iPhone, they'd give up their second car. I mean, it's an extraordinary product." And crucially, Apple's buybacks make Berkshire's ownership (~5.6% → ~6% without Berkshire spending a dollar) grow automatically — every tenth of a percent is "like adding $100 million to our share of the earnings." *Munger* added the framework: "one of the inane things that's taught in modern university education is that a vast diversification is absolutely mandatory in investing in common stocks. That is an insane idea… if you've only got three, I'd rather be in my best ideas instead of my worst." *(Supersession: Berkshire sold approximately half of its Apple stake in mid-2024, citing tax and portfolio-weight considerations — context Buffett's 2023 bullishness did not anticipate.)* See [concentration-vs-diversification](../concepts/concentration-vs-diversification.md), [apple](../entities/apple.md).

### AI and the atom-bomb problem: what you can't un-invent

The AI question drew Berkshire's most memorable 2023 formulation. *Munger:* "I am personally skeptical of some of the hype that has gone into artificial intelligence. I think old-fashioned intelligence works pretty well." Buffett went deeper: Bill Gates had shown him a version. It could do remarkable things — legal research, pattern recognition at scale — "but it couldn't tell jokes." Then the analogy: the atom bomb was invented for overwhelmingly good reasons in 1942, but "is it good for the next 200 years of the world that the ability to do so has been unleashed? We didn't have any choice." Einstein after Hiroshima: "This has changed everything in the world except how men think." Buffett's formulation for AI: it "can change everything in the world except how men think and behave." The restraint is not a technology prediction; it is an irreversibility warning. When something can "do all kinds of things, I get a little bit worried because I know we won't be able to un-invent it." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

### TSMC: when geopolitics overrides business quality

Taiwan Semiconductor was described as "one of the best managed companies and important companies in the world" — one Buffett expected to be able to say the same of five, ten, or 20 years out. Yet Berkshire purchased a large TSMC position in late 2022 and sold almost all of it by early 2023 — within months. The reason: "I don't like its location. And I've reevaluated that." Japan, with comparable-quality businesses and no Taiwan Strait risk, is preferable: "I feel better about the capital that we've got deployed in Japan than Taiwan. I wish it weren't so, but I think that's the reality." *Munger:* "My view is that Warren ought to feel comfortable if he wants to." This is one of the rarest patterns in Berkshire history — a position taken then abandoned not because the business deteriorated but because a geopolitical risk was initially underweighted. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

### Japan trading houses: patient reading of simple arithmetic

Buffett described the discovery as almost embarrassingly simple: five large, understandable companies earning ~14% on Berkshire's cost, paying decent dividends, repurchasing shares — financed by yen bonds at ~0.5%, locking in a ~13.5% arbitrage. "If you get 14% on one side and a half a percent on the other side, and you've got money forever… we just started buying them." He hadn't told Abel for six months. By the 2023 meeting: 7.4% in each of the five (Itochu, Marubeni, Mitsubishi, Mitsui, Sumitomo), ~$4–5 billion ahead plus dividends, ¥164 billion of yen bonds outstanding (now the largest non-domestic corporate yen borrower), and Berkshire had made its first visit to Japan — partly to introduce Abel to the management teams for "10, 20, 30, 40 years from now." *Munger:* "It made $10 billion simply in that way." The lesson: public markets consistently misprice businesses that require patient, boring reading. See [japanese-trading-houses](../entities/japanese-trading-houses.md).

### Reserve currency and the genie of fiscal excess

A 13-year-old Berkshire shareholder asked the most pointed fiscal question of the afternoon: with the U.S. national debt at $31 trillion (~125% of GDP), is de-dollarization coming? Buffett was careful: no substitute reserve currency exists; Powell understands the situation. But "nobody knows how far you can go with a paper currency before it gets out of control… you don't want to try and pick out the point at where it does become a problem, because then it's all over." He cited the post-WWII parallel: ~1% inflation in January 1946, ~15% by December. *Munger:* "At some point printing money to buy votes will be counterproductive. And we don't know exactly where that comes." The personal defense: "your best defense is your own earning power" — the doctor, lawyer, or teacher who delivers real value survives currency erosion. Berkshire's defense is owning businesses that produce real goods and services whose value persists across currency regimes. See [american-tailwind](../concepts/american-tailwind.md).

### Succession: Abel's mandate is capital allocation

The most concrete succession update in recent years. Asked whether Abel would make capital-allocation decisions including buybacks, Buffett was unequivocal: "Greg understands capital allocation as well as I do, and that's lucky for us. He will make those decisions, I think, very much in the same framework as I would make them." Abel confirmed: "I don't see that framework changing." Buffett had also introduced Abel to the Japanese trading-house management teams specifically because "we're going to be with them 10, 20, 30, 40 years from now." The practical implication: the Berkshire model — retain earnings, buy wonderful businesses, repurchase only when price is below intrinsic value — transfers to Abel not by instruction but by demonstrated understanding. See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md), [greg-abel](../people/greg-abel.md).

## Q&A digest — Morning session

**M4 · Q1 operating earnings; float and cash overview.** *Buffett:* Q1 2023 operating earnings ~$8 billion. Most Berkshire businesses will report lower earnings this year as the post-COVID demand surge ends — "an extraordinary period" where customers bought without waiting for sales, now over. Two offsets: investment income will surge from ~$50M/year (at four basis points) to ~$5B as $125B+ in T-bills now yields ~5.9%; and insurance underwriting is uncorrelated to the business cycle. Float at $165 billion; GAAP net worth at $504 billion — "more under GAAP accounting now than any other company in the U.S." Share count declining every year through repurchases. See [float](../concepts/float.md), [berkshire-hathaway](../entities/berkshire-hathaway.md).

**★ M5 · Failure to guarantee SVB's deposits would have been "catastrophic."** *Buffett:* "It would've been catastrophic. And that's why they were covered." The $250,000 FDIC statutory limit has never described actual U.S. government behavior; no legislator wants to "explain to the American public why we're keeping only $250,000 insured, and we're going to start a run on every bank in the country and disrupt the world financial system." This is the implicit deposit guarantee made explicit. *Munger:* nothing to add. See [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md).

**M6 · No emotional investing decisions — ever.** *Buffett:* "I can't recall any time in the history of Berkshire that we made an emotional decision… you definitely want to be a no emotion person in making an investment or business decision." *Munger:* "No." Only possible exception: occasionally keeping a manager a little longer than strictly optimal out of loyalty — but Berkshire's businesses are strong enough that the cost has been negligible.

**M7 · GEICO telematics: "still a work in progress."** *Ajit Jain:* 90% of new GEICO business now has telematics input, but fewer than half of policyholders opt in. The real bottleneck is legacy technology: 600+ systems that don't communicate, being compressed to 15–16. Q1 combined ratio was ~93, but aided by prior-year reserve releases and seasonal patterns; sustainable year-end target is ~96 by end of 2024 — and reaching it will cost policyholders (growth sacrificed for profitability). "It will not be until two years from now that we'll be back on track, fighting the battles on both the profitability and growth fronts." See [geico](../entities/geico.md).

**M8 · BNSF: "reset the railroad" was deliberate.** *Abel:* The 2022 operating-metric decline was a choice — the team prioritized resetting the railroad for long-term safety and service over short-term metric optimization during post-pandemic supply disruptions. BNSF had made significant efficiency gains 2019–2021. Precision scheduled railroading peers are tracked closely. "There'll be continual progress. There'll be years where it's not as quick, or even we go backwards. But over the long-term, we'll see exceptional results." See [bnsf](../entities/bnsf.md).

**M9 · Berkshire Hathaway Specialty Insurance: $12 billion float at near-zero cost.** *Buffett:* Todd Combs is running GEICO and "doing a wonderful job… made a very, very big change in multiple ways at GEICO." Then the pivot: BH Specialty, launched ~10 years ago by Ajit Jain and four colleagues, has built $12 billion of float at essentially zero underwriting cost, employs ~1,500 worldwide, and has outperformed every other insurance startup of its era. "No one has really successfully entered this space." Only Berkshire could supply the capital and the credibility simultaneously. *Ajit Jain:* confirmed the $12B figure. See [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**★ M10 · AI: "old-fashioned intelligence works pretty well" / the atom-bomb analogy.** *Munger:* "I am personally skeptical of some of the hype that has gone into artificial intelligence. I think old-fashioned intelligence works pretty well." *Buffett:* Bill Gates demonstrated a version. It could do remarkable things — but "it couldn't tell jokes." More seriously: "When something can do all kinds of things, I get a little bit worried because I know we won't be able to un-invent it." The atom-bomb analogy: invented for overwhelmingly good reasons in WWII, but "is it good for the next 200 years of the world that the ability to do so has been unleashed?" Einstein's line — "This has changed everything in the world except how men think" — rephrased for AI: it "can change everything in the world except how men think and behave." The framing is not a technology prediction; it is an irreversibility argument. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ M11 · Commercial real estate: "hollowing out of the downtowns" will be "quite significant and quite unpleasant."** *Munger:* "The hollowing out of the downtowns in the United States and elsewhere in the world is going to be quite significant and quite unpleasant. I think the country will get through it all right, but as they say, it will often involve a different set of owners." *Buffett:* the structural driver is non-recourse borrowing — commercial real estate operators build their whole model around "whatever they can borrow without signing their name." When rates rise, losses fall on lenders, who "tend to extend and pretend." Buildings don't go away; owners do. See [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md).

**★ M12 · "What gives you opportunities is other people doing dumb things."** *Buffett:* "New things coming along don't take away the opportunities. What gives you opportunities is other people doing dumb things." The number of dumb things has increased because capital access expanded: "you could start ten or 15 dumb insurance companies in the last ten years, and you could become rich if you were adroit at it, whether the business succeeded or not." Short-termism creates permanent openings for five-to-twenty-year thinking: "if you go to an investor relations call, they're all trying to figure out how to fill out a sheet to show the earnings for the year… And the management is interested in feeding them expectations, so we'll slightly be beaten." *Munger:* "There is so much money now in the hands of so many smart people, all trying to outsmart one another… a radically different world" that will "have some unpleasant episodes." See [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

**M13 · AIG liability assumption: "good but not great."** *Ajit Jain:* Berkshire assumed up to $20 billion of AIG long-tail liabilities in 2016 for ~$10 billion upfront. Through 2023, actual payouts run at 96% of projected — slightly better than expected. If final payouts land below projections, Berkshire will have borrowed at below 4% and earned a fee. *Buffett:* the $10 billion goes into Berkshire's general pool, not matched against bonds — "no other insurance company could do it." *Munger:* Berkshire's casualty subsidiaries carry "four times as much stockholder capital behind each dollar of premium value" versus normal carriers — the structural moat for large-liability transactions. See [float](../concepts/float.md), [national-indemnity](../entities/national-indemnity.md).

**M15 · BNSF tribal-land breach: "there are lessons learned."** *Abel:* BNSF breached a crude-oil train-volume agreement with a tribal nation and had a derailment on tribal land. Acknowledged the breach was real; discussions are ongoing. Derailments occur industrywide (1,000+/year) and BNSF takes each seriously — "we take them incredibly seriously." *Buffett:* a common carrier "has to carry certain products we wish we didn't have to carry." Not ethically neutral, but structurally constrained. See [bnsf](../entities/bnsf.md).

**★ M16 · BHE clean energy and the WW2 lesson: we need a "czar."** *Abel:* BHE has $70 billion of known capital projects over the next decade to achieve its 50% carbon-reduction target by 2030 (vs. 2005 baseline). PacifiCorp's six-state transmission project illustrates the bottleneck: a $6B plan now costs ~$7B for one-third built because coordination across six regulatory regimes is genuinely hard. *Buffett:* the U.S. solved this once in WWII — "we brought a bunch of people to Washington at a $1 a year… we reordered the industrial enterprise of the United States in a way that was unbelievable. Because they had the power of the federal government." In peacetime, "the capital's there. The people are there. The objective is obvious. And we just don't seem to be able to do it." *(Supersession: BHE's 2024 results were materially impaired by PacifiCorp wildfire-liability write-downs in Oregon, raising doubt about the utility business model under climate risk — acknowledged by Buffett in the 2024 letter.)* See [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md).

**M17 · Succession pipeline: Abel decides the next layer.** *Buffett:* Abel will succeed him. Who fills Abel's and Jain's roles will be Abel's decision — "Greg will probably be the one that will make the final decision" — with Jain's advice. No pre-announcement warranted because "a lot can change between now and then." *Munger:* Berkshire changes managers far less frequently than other conglomerates — "that's helped us." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**M19 · A corporate raider can't afford Berkshire.** *Buffett:* by the time his shares distribute to foundations over 12–15 years, Berkshire's market cap will require "a trillion and a half" to acquire — eliminating the raider threat mathematically. The deeper defense: "Berkshire be regarded as a national asset rather than a national liability." *Munger:* "I don't spend much time worrying about something that happened 50 years ago after I'm dead." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**M20 · Discovery stories: Ajit Jain and Ben Rosner.** *Buffett:* Ajit Jain walked in on a Saturday in 1986 with zero insurance experience — "after talking with him, I knew I'd struck gold." Then the Ben Rosner story: self-educated (dropped out at fourth grade), hauled a WWI submarine down Chicago streets in 1933 for a World's Fair attraction, and when a cop demanded to know where they were going, Rosner claimed Al Capone as his partner. Later sold his dress-shop chain to Berkshire for $6M after his co-owner's widow started criticizing him — "'I hear you're the fastest draw in the West,' he says. 'Draw.'" *Munger:* "When Ben died, that ability left us." Character, not credentials, is the talent signal. See [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md).

**★ M21 · Property-cat reinsurance: $15 billion exposure, mostly Florida.** *Ajit Jain:* Property-cat prices disappointed at January 2023 renewal (capacity flooded back in December), but April renewals jumped sharply — Berkshire deployed heavily and now holds a portfolio "almost 50% more than what it was five, six months ago." At ~5% of $300B capital, the exposure cap is ~$15B. Portfolio is concentrated: "if there's a big hurricane in Florida, we will have a very substantial loss… as much as $15 billion." Without a Florida hurricane: "we'll make several billion dollars as profit." *Buffett:* approved a $2B increase in exposure in under 30 seconds when Ajit called. "I hope he calls me again." See [super-cat-insurance](../concepts/super-cat-insurance.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**M22 · "We don't get smarter, we get a little wiser."** *Buffett:* learning accelerates by observing consumer behavior across 80 auto dealerships, Garanimals, Apple, See's. The 1966 department-store purchase where "as the ink was drying on our purchase price, we realized we'd done something dumb." "We don't get smarter over time, we get a little wiser, though, following it over time. And you can do it while sitting in the office with a telephone, too, which we like." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ M23 · Japanese trading houses: patient reading of simple arithmetic.** *Munger* (prompted Buffett to tell the story). *Buffett:* "It was pretty simple." Five large, understandable companies, earning ~14% on cost, paying decent dividends, repurchasing shares — financed by yen bonds at ~0.5%. "If you get 14% on one side and a half a percent on the other side, and you've got money forever… we just started buying them." By the 2023 meeting: 7.4% in each, ~$4–5B ahead plus dividends, ¥164B of yen bonds (Berkshire now the largest non-domestic corporate yen borrower). First visit to Japan to introduce Abel to the CEOs. *Munger:* "It made $10 billion simply in that way. We would look like heroes. Now $10 billion just sort of disappears as if it's a little dot in Berkshire's reports." See [japanese-trading-houses](../entities/japanese-trading-houses.md).

**★ M24 · Apple: "a better business than any we own."** *Buffett:* Apple is ~5.6% of Apple (not 35% of Berkshire's portfolio) — comparing it to BNSF as a portfolio weight is the wrong frame; both are business stakes. The consumer grip: "these same people pay $35,000 for having a second car. And if they had to give up a second car or give up their iPhone, they'd give up their second car." Buybacks automatically grow Berkshire's stake: every tenth of a percent is "like adding $100 million to our share of the earnings." Admitted selling some shares in a prior year for tax reasons was "a dumb decision." *Munger:* "one of the inane things that's taught in modern university education is that a vast diversification is absolutely mandatory in investing in common stocks. That is an insane idea… if you've only got three, I'd rather be in my best ideas instead of my worst." The payoff coinage: if you know the edge of your own ability, "you should ignore most of the notions of our experts about what I call 'deworsification' of portfolios." *(Supersession: Berkshire sold approximately half its Apple stake in mid-2024; Buffett cited both tax considerations and portfolio rebalancing.)* See [apple](../entities/apple.md), [concentration-vs-diversification](../concepts/concentration-vs-diversification.md).

**★ M25 · US-China: "stupid, stupid, stupid" to increase tensions.** *Munger:* "I think the tension has been wrongly created on both sides. I think we're equally guilty of being stupid. If there's one thing we should do, it's get along with China… Everything that increases the tension between the two countries is stupid, stupid, stupid. It ought to be stopped on each side. And each side ought to respond to the other side's stupidity with reciprocal kindness." Apple–China partnership cited as the model: "good for Apple and good for China. That's the kind of business we ought to be doing with China. And more of it." *Buffett:* the two-superpower dynamic requires leaders who understand the other side faces the same constraint — "It's like playing chicken… driving toward a cliff." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ M26 · TSMC sold: geopolitical risk demands a fast reversal.** *Buffett:* Taiwan Semiconductor is "one of the best managed companies and important companies in the world." But "I don't like its location. And I've reevaluated that." Japan, with equivalent business quality and no Taiwan Strait risk, is preferable: "I feel better about the capital that we've got deployed in Japan than Taiwan. I wish it weren't so, but I think that's the reality." *Munger:* "My view is that Warren ought to feel comfortable if he wants to." One of the rarest Berkshire patterns: a position abandoned not because the business changed but because a geopolitical risk was initially underweighted. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**M27 · Ben Graham's book and GEICO as his one lollapalooza.** *Buffett:* *The Intelligent Investor* (1949) has sold 7.3 million copies and perennially ranks ~#300 on Amazon — no investment book sustains that position. *Munger:* "An interesting fact that he was sheepish about in his old age, was that more than half of all the investment return that Ben Graham made in his whole life came from one stock, one growth stock: GEICO, Berkshire subsidiary." Graham's own postscript acknowledged it: the prepared mind waits, doesn't lose along the way, and then "something comes along." Munger: "buying one undervalued great company is a very good thing, as Berkshire has found out again and again." See [ben-graham](../people/ben-graham.md), [geico](../entities/geico.md).

**M28 · GEICO and auto-manufacturer insurance at the point of sale.** *Ajit Jain:* GEICO is in talks with OEMs to offer insurance at point of sale. No success stories yet — driver data, not just vehicle data, complicates underwriting. Tesla and GM have projected big insurance volume but margins are only 4%: "once there are more people that are trying to take a bite of the apple, it just becomes very, very difficult." *Buffett:* General Motors Insurance tried for decades. Uber's insurance venture was laid off to James River, which "got their head handed to them." The matching-risk-to-rate model is genuinely hard to replicate via new distribution. See [geico](../entities/geico.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**M29 · Index funds "backed off" from ESG voting.** *Buffett:* index-fund managers discovered aggressive ESG voting threatens regulatory attention — "the last thing in the world they want to do is have Washington… decide that they're throwing around their weight too much." Their commercial interest is assets under management (at 2 bps), making ESG voting a free-rider option, not a core drive. "If you figure out where their self-interest is, you can judge where their behavior is going to go." *Munger:* "They've backed off a lot." See [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md).

**★ M30 · Greg Abel "understands capital allocation as well as I do."** *Buffett:* "Greg understands capital allocation as well as I do, and that's lucky for us. And he will make those decisions, I think, very much in the same framework as I would make them." *Abel:* "I don't see that framework changing. When the opportunity presents itself, we'll want to be an active repurchaser of Berkshire shares." *Buffett:* share repurchases "can be the dumbest thing you can do, or it can be the smartest thing you can do" — the rule is simple: buy when price is below intrinsic value; "no matter what investment banker comes in." See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md), [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md), [greg-abel](../people/greg-abel.md).

**★ M32 · "Hard to judge successor management in a really good business."** *Buffett:* "If they don't show up at the office, it'll keep working for a long time. And maybe that lack of useful input may show itself in five years." The board's challenge post-Buffett: "How are the shareholders, advised by a bunch of people that are concerned about whether you're meeting earnings projections… telling them whether the management's any good? It is very, very hard." He feels "100% comfortable" with Abel. *Munger:* nothing to add. The underlying lesson: the strength of a great business makes management quality hard to measure in real time — and that same strength is what makes the succession risk manageable. See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

## Q&A digest — Afternoon session

**★ A1 · Digital bank runs: deposits are no longer sticky — the run happens in seconds.** *Buffett:* "The situation in banking is very similar to what it's always been in banking, that fear is contagious, always." What changed: "You know, press a button, you don't have to get in a line and wait for days and have the teller counting out the money slowly in gold… you're going to have a run in a few seconds." SVB was the demonstration project: FDIC paid 100 cents on all demand deposits regardless of size, yet "the public is still confused." The messaging has been "very poor" by politicians, agencies, and the press. Buffett offered a $1 million bet (unreturned) that demand depositors at any U.S. bank will not lose. The core lesson: a lighted match can turn into a conflagration, and the time between them is now seconds, not days. See [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md), [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md).

**★ A2 · Bank CEO punishment: losses must fall on those who cause them.** *Buffett:* First Republic's fatal strategy was "in plain sight" in the 10-K — fixed-rate jumbo mortgages at low rates for long terms, giving the borrower the optionality. "You don't give options like that." Executives should face real consequences: "The CEO gets the bank in trouble, both the CEO and the director should suffer. The stockholders of the future shouldn't suffer. They didn't do anything." Current reality: "if you run a bank and you screw it up, you're still a rich guy and the clubs don't drop you." *Munger:* "I'm so old fashioned that I kind of liked it that when banks didn't do investment banking." And: "a banker should be more like an engineer. He's more, like, into avoiding trouble than he is getting rich." See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md), [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

**★ A3 · Berkshire cautious on banks; keeping Bank of America.** *Buffett:* Berkshire sold banks when the pandemic broke out and again in the six months before this meeting. "We don't know where the shareholders of the big banks, necessarily, or the regional banks or any bank, are heading now." The American public "is probably as confused about banking as ever," and that has unpredictable consequences. Exception: Bank of America — "I proposed the deal with them, so I stick with it." A sound bank remains an excellent investment in principle — of Berkshire's own Rockford bank, *Munger* interjected, "By the way, it never had a bad debt" — but the current regulatory and political environment makes the trajectory hard to project. *Munger:* "The gamier it gets and the more it looks like investment banking, the less I like it, as a citizen." See [wells-fargo](../entities/wells-fargo.md), [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

**★ A4 · De-dollarization: "madness to keep printing money."** *Buffett (responding to a 13-year-old shareholder):* No reserve-currency alternative exists; Powell understands the situation. But "nobody knows how far you can go with a paper currency before it gets out of control… you don't want to try and pick out the point at where it does become a problem, because then it's all over." America can do a lot, "but if we do too much it's very hard to see how you recover once you let the genie out of the bottle." Post-WWII parallel: price level roughly 10× since then. The best personal defense: your own earning power. "If you're the best doctor in town, if you're the best lawyer in town… you're going to make a good living… your value to the community… is sustained. And so the best investment is always in yourself." *Munger:* "At some point printing money to buy votes will be counterproductive. And we don't know exactly where that comes." Berkshire's defense: owning businesses producing real goods whose value persists across currency regimes. See [american-tailwind](../concepts/american-tailwind.md), [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md).

**A5 · Pilot acquisition: "the best way is just write the check."** *Buffett:* The three-stage Pilot structure (first tranche: attractive; second tranche: record 2022 diesel margins meant seller got a "very good price"; third tranche: seller's option) would have been better as a clean 100% purchase. "When you give the other person the option, they've got some advantage." The Nebraska Furniture Mart 80/20 structure is the prior precedent. Overall still very happy with Pilot; the Travel Centers of America comparison is "spurious" (Pilot owns its real estate). "We've got hundreds and hundreds of locations on the interstate that are zoned commercial… they're not going to move the interstate two miles to the right." *Munger:* "Warren, it wasn't for sale." See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**A6 · "Write your obituary and then try and figure out how to live up to it."** *Buffett:* the biggest mistakes — in both investing and life — are the ones that "take you out of the game." Always spend a bit less than you earn; never hold credit card debt. Tom Murphy's two rules: "You can always tell someone to go to hell tomorrow" (resist the digital impulse to damage relationships permanently); "Praise by name, criticize by category." *Munger:* "Spend less than you earn, invest shrewdly, avoid toxic people and toxic activities, try and keep learning all your life… if you do all those things, you are almost certain to succeed. And if you don't, you're going to need a lot of luck." See [margin-of-safety](../concepts/margin-of-safety.md).

**A8 · Brand moats: Garanimals and the gift-signal of See's.** *Buffett:* Garanimals is sold exclusively through Walmart because that channel serves the product's consumer niche — unlike Fruit of the Loom, which competes against many substitutes. The See's comparison: when buying See's as a gift, "they know if they give their girlfriend… a box of candy they don't say at the same time, 'Here, I got a wonderful deal on this candy.' I mean, that just kills the moment." Brand value flows from the emotional signal it sends the recipient, not the transaction economics for the buyer. Products demanded by name — not by category — create pricing power and distribution leverage that can sustain for a century. See [moat](../concepts/moat.md), [sees-candies](../entities/sees-candies.md).

**A9 · Streaming wars: "you need fewer companies, or you need higher prices."** *Buffett:* Paramount cutting its dividend 80% is "not good news." The streaming math: "you need higher prices, or it doesn't work. And you don't lock in people when you get them to join up for the streaming period when your serial runs." Recall the 1951 gas station: the one competitor set prices and there was no escape — "he determined our profit." *Munger:* "the movie business is one tough business. The talent will make the money; the agents will make the money." Eyeballs aren't going to increase; choice is. See [commodity-business-economics](../concepts/commodity-business-economics.md), [media-economics](../concepts/media-economics.md).

**A10 · BHE renewable energy record.** *Buffett:* Iowa has produced more wind energy than total customer consumption — but intermittency means storage is required. BHE spends ~$7B/year (depreciation plus $3B additional). "Our record in wind and solar has not been topped by any utility in the United States." Aided by not paying common dividends for 20 years, reinvesting everything. *Munger:* unconvinced about the scale of climate danger but in favor of conserving hydrocarbons regardless: "there're certain things hydrocarbons can do that nothing else can do. And there's only so much of them there. Why not be cautious in conserving them?" See [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md).

**★ A11 · Occidental: "We will not be making any offer for control."** *Buffett:* Berkshire loves the Permian Basin position and Vicki Hollub ("she knows what happens beneath the surface"). Has ~$10B preferred being retired at 110%, common stock, and warrants at ~$59. But: "we don't want to run an oil field." Shale economics: a well comes in at 12,000–19,000 barrels/day and declines sharply within 18 months. *Munger:* "If you like quick death in your oil wells, we have them for you." U.S. shale is now ~5M of 11M barrels/day — removing it would be an immediate supply shock. "There is no oil basin in the United States that compares to the Permian, in terms of promise." *(Supersession: By 2024–2025 Berkshire's OXY common stake grew to ~28%, with a regulatory allowance to hold up to 50% — contradicting "no intent for control" in spirit, if not legally.)* See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**A12 · Elon Musk: "brilliant, brilliant guy" who "likes taking on the impossible job."** *Munger:* "Elon Musk overestimates himself. But he is very talented. So, he's overestimating somebody who doesn't need to overestimate." "He would not have achieved what he has in life if he hadn't tried for unreasonably extreme objectives. He likes taking on the impossible job and doing it. We're different, Warren and I. Warren and I are looking for the easy job that we can identify." *Buffett:* "If we can do it playing tic-tac-toe, we'll do it." The contrast is clean: Musk's model requires repeated failure and fanaticism; Berkshire's selects for high-certainty, easily-compounding outcomes. Neither model is wrong — they require different temperaments. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ A13 · Cash as opportunity fund: waiting for the inevitable calls.** *Buffett:* "What we would really like to do is buy great businesses. If we could buy a company for $50 billion, or $75 billion, $100 billion, we could do it." The structural advantage of Berkshire's cash: when companies hit financial stress ("they just may need $5 billion, or $10 billion, or $20 billion"), "the number of phone calls that you can make at a time like that is very, very limited." Tiffany, Harley-Davidson, Goldman Sachs in 2008 — "that sort of thing will happen again." Meanwhile: "it isn't killing us to hold $130 billion of bills at 5% plus bond equivalent yields." *Munger:* "OK. Maybe. Fine." See [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md), [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

**A15 · 15% corporate minimum tax: "doesn't bother me in the least."** *Buffett:* the book-income minimum-tax regulations are still being written; whether unrealized gains feed the calculation is unresolved. "We were paying 52% tax as federal income taxes when I bought control… The 15% tax doesn't bother me in the least." If 1,000 Berkshire-equivalents existed, no other U.S. entity would need to pay taxes. The Berkshire implication: the company can manage the new standard and will "figure out a way to pay 15% every year, which generally, we've been paying anyway."

**A16 · "We are in the perfect sort of game" — and recognizing a bad deal mid-execution.** *Buffett:* the 1966 Hochschild-Kohn department-store purchase turned sour when the financing banker called it a "little old" business: "We immediately started thinking, 'Maybe this isn't the best deal we've ever seen in our lives.'" More broadly: "If you knew you were going to play golf and you were going to hit a hole in one on every hole… nobody would play golf." Berkshire is the perfect game — the surprise, the opponent, the uncertainty. Nobody is after anyone else's job. *Munger:* "Warren and I are naturally so ridiculous that we don't need very many funny stories." See [mistakes-of-omission](../concepts/mistakes-of-omission.md).

**A17 · Activision arbitrage: regulatory risk materialized.** *Buffett:* taken as a merger arb on the Microsoft-Activision deal. The UK CMA blocked it. "I think Microsoft has been remarkably willing to cooperate with governing bodies… They've met the opposition more than halfway. But that doesn't mean that it gets done if a given country wants to block it." Position readable from Berkshire's 10-Q filing that day. *Munger:* "You kissed that one off beautifully." Lesson: regulatory risk in merger arb is sovereign and cannot be bought off. *(Supersession: The CMA reversed course in late 2023 after a restructured deal; Microsoft closed the Activision acquisition October 2023, resolving the arb favorably.)* See [arbitrage](../concepts/arbitrage.md).

**★ A18 · Why sell a business to Berkshire: autonomy without the burdens of being public.** *Buffett:* "What we really hope to find is managers who love their business, but don't like a lot of what comes with it as a public company" — investor-relations calls, analyst courtship, banker dependency, trade-association conformity, quarterly earnings theater. "There's nothing like working for yourself. And if you can't own a big company, working at Berkshire Hathaway, from running a company, is the closest thing you will get." *Munger:* "most of the people who are going to end up the way we did, they almost already know how to do it." The National Indemnity case: Jack Ringwalt sold because he was irritated with the Nebraska insurance regulator "once a year" — once that irritation became Buffett's problem instead of his, Ringwalt was happy. The practical acquisition insight: Berkshire buys from owners who want to protect employees and preserve culture, not maximize exit price. See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**★ A19 · See's Candies: "a wonderful brand that doesn't travel."** *Buffett:* See's is 101 years old and has "magic" — but it is gravitational: strong in California and the adjacent West, inert in the East. "We always think we were right for the first week." East/West differences: dark vs. milk chocolate preferences, miniature-format sales patterns. "The economics were so good if we succeeded, so we tried various things." The lesson: knowing what consumers respond to — and where those preferences stop — is a skill built by decades of observation, not by analysis. A brand with built-in loyalty in a region can still be incapable of geographic expansion despite superior local economics. See [sees-candies](../entities/sees-candies.md), [franchise-vs-business](../concepts/franchise-vs-business.md).

**A20 · NetJets: "in a class by itself."** *Buffett:* NetJets is the "gold standard" in private aviation — 600 planes (heading to 650 with 100 new purchases this year), second-largest fleet after major commercial airlines, no real competitor. Wheels Up: stock from $10 to 48 cents, $1B+ of prepaid hours at risk. "There isn't any competitor." The cost framing: "Your heirs are paying for it." *Munger:* "NetJets has been remarkable. You can argue it's worth as much as any airline now." See [netjets](../entities/netjets.md).

**★ A21 · Auto industry: "fascinating but I can't tell you where it'll be in five or ten years."** *Buffett:* Henry Ford looked like he owned the world in Model-T years; 20 years later Ford was losing money. GM had 19,000 dealers in 1932 with a population of 120 million — now ~18,000 all-brand dealers with 330 million people. "You've got a lot of worldwide competitors, they're not going to go away." EV transition: *Munger:* "The electric vehicle is coming big time… it's imposing huge capital costs and huge risks." Contrast with Apple: "I think I know where Apple's going to be in five or ten years, and I don't know where the car companies are going to be." Ferrari is the exception — 11,000–12,000 cars/year, global prestige brand — but the narrowness of the moat proves the rule. See [moat](../concepts/moat.md), [commodity-business-economics](../concepts/commodity-business-economics.md).

**A22 · Fed balance sheet: "I don't worry about the Federal Reserve."** *Buffett:* the Fed is doing its job within the dual mandate; the fiscal problem belongs to Congress, not Powell. Observation: currency in circulation grew from $800B to $2.2T (mostly $100 bills, ~50 per American) — "I'd really like to know where all of that is." "Cash is not trash." *Munger:* "if you're going to just keep printing money and spending it, I think eventually it causes bad trouble" — the man who jumped off a tall building "is all right until he hits the ground." Overlaps A4 but adds the currency-in-circulation observation.

**★ A23 · Society's obligation to people displaced by capitalism.** *Buffett:* "a rich society should take care, one way or another, of people that worked in our shoe factories, people who worked in our textile companies." Citing Berkshire's own New Bedford mill workers in 1964 — half spoke only Portuguese, had no other market for their skills. "You can't talk about 'retraining' somebody that's 55 or 60… and really tell them they're going to have a great future." Free trade and globalization net-benefit society, but "net the country is better off" doesn't help the individual displaced. *Munger:* "Free market capitalism automatically creates GDP per capita that grows and helps everybody… But inherent in the process, there's a lot of pain… if you try and take all the pain out, you'll also take all the gains out of it." The balance: preserve the growth engine while building a safety net adequate to displacement. See [american-tailwind](../concepts/american-tailwind.md).

**★ A24 · Accounting: "We'll consistently do what is legal, and we'll consistently say what we think is right."** *Buffett:* On the apparent contradiction between criticizing banks for using hold-to-maturity accounting (hiding mark-to-market losses) and his own preference for not running stock price changes through Berkshire's income statement: "I believe, in both cases, in doing it on the balance sheet and not in the income statement." The right approach: mark-to-market on the balance sheet, with changes flowing to "other comprehensive income" — as the rule was before the FASB change. The banks abused hold-to-maturity by hiding balance-sheet losses from depositors; Berkshire's objection is to distorting the income statement with noise that "changes every five seconds." "We'll consistently do what is legal, and we'll consistently say what we think is right." *Munger:* "I don't know what the accountants were thinking when they made that change. It strikes me as bonkers, absolutely bonkers." See [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md), [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md).

**A25 · Munger philosophical about grandchildren — a closing note.** *Munger:* "I am quite philosophical about my grandchildren not thinking exactly the way I do. It seems to me that's almost the natural course of life… I have to pretend that I like some of the boyfriends and girlfriends I don't like… I just bite my tongue and keep silent." *Buffett:* "My three children have grown a lot smarter in the last 30 years, and I think I've grown smarter." *Munger:* "You needed a lot of help." Candid and human; Munger's final substantive exchange at his final meeting. *Munger (unprompted):* "I worked a year for US Steel… The thing was utterly doomed, and three years later it went back to greenfields. The whole thing was razed to the ground. I did not see it coming. Now, to be that ignorant as I was at that age, it was a sin."

## Connections to other sources

- The bank-run discussion (M5, A1, A2, A3) is the direct sequel to [berkshire-2008-meeting](berkshire-2008-meeting.md)'s mid-crisis observations on Fannie/Freddie conservatorship and deposit fragility — except now the mechanism is digital speed (seconds, not days) and the crisis is regional rather than systemic.
- Buffett's Apple-as-business-stake argument (M24) uses the same owner-vs.-portfolio logic first articulated at the [berkshire-2018-meeting](berkshire-2018-meeting.md) when he disclosed the stake. Here the position is ~3× larger and the framing upgrades to "better business than any we own."
- The TSMC reversal (M26) has no prior precedent in the meetings corpus. The closest inversion is the BNSF purchase (2008–2009 meetings) where Buffett committed to infrastructure despite macro uncertainty; TSMC is the mirror image — exit despite business quality, because the domain sits outside the circle.
- The "opportunities from dumb things" observation (M12) restates the [berkshire-1994-meeting](berkshire-1994-meeting.md) M47 theme that macro agnosticism is "a way more efficient way to behave" — now extended to institutional short-termism and the explosion of easily-available capital funding poor businesses.
- De-dollarization / fiscal concerns (A4) are the 2023 form of the inflation-and-purchasing-power argument Buffett has made since [berkshire-1977-letter](berkshire-1977-letter.md), now with a reserve-currency wrinkle not present in the early letters — and with Munger's "Latin America" warning adding the historical failure-mode.
- Munger's "old-fashioned intelligence works pretty well" on AI (M10) applies to a new domain the same skepticism of complex leverage he expressed about derivatives since [berkshire-1994-meeting](berkshire-1994-meeting.md) M11.
- The Japanese trading-house entry (M23) builds directly on [berkshire-2022-meeting](berkshire-2022-meeting.md), where the position was first discussed publicly; here the update confirms the yen-bond size, the 7.4% ownership, and the Japan visit — the chapter from discovery to ongoing partnership.
