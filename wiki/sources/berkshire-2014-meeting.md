# Berkshire Hathaway 2014 Annual Meeting

Held on May 3, 2014, at the CenturyLink Center in Omaha with record-breaking attendance — roughly 11,000 more tickets distributed than any prior year — the 2014 meeting carries the heft of Berkshire's near-50th anniversary under Buffett's management (the full 50-year retrospective appeared in the 2014 annual letter, published in early 2015). The defining controversy was Berkshire's abstention on Coca-Cola's equity compensation plan: Buffett and Munger had decided the plan was excessive but chose not to vote against it or publicly oppose it before the shareholder meeting, a stance that drew sharp criticism from activist David Winters and public scrutiny of Berkshire's governance role as the largest shareholder. Beyond the Coke drama, the 2014 meeting produces durable instruction in five areas: why picking your governance battle and your moment matters more than perpetual public opposition; how Berkshire's capital-redeployment without tax leakage is the conglomerate model's structural engine; how the $20 billion cash floor functions as an existential operating constraint rather than dry powder; how the circle of competence is identified in practice; and why corporate America's institutional forces reliably produce dumb acquisitions. This page keeps the wiki's two-layer convention: a distilled thematic top, then a comprehensive ★-marked Q&A digest as the home for detail.

## How to read the Q&A digest

- **★** marks a timeless, load-bearing exchange worth citing in the future; unmarked entries still carry investor value but are secondary or period-specific.
- Answers are attributed separately to **Buffett** and **Munger** — a Munger line appears only when he added a distinct lesson (he said "Nothing to add" to most).

## Key themes

### Governance timing: opposition without warfare

The Coca-Cola abstention is the meeting's most debated act, and Buffett's defense of it is a transferable lesson in governance execution. His framework: Berkshire made its view clear (the plan was excessive), notified management in advance (he told Muhtar Kent before abstaining), stated the position publicly immediately after the vote, and chose not to ally itself with the activist's erroneous dilution calculations. "I think the best result for the Coca-Cola Company was achieved by our abstention." Munger generalizes: "people accomplish more if they pick their spots for public disapproval. And knowing both Howie and Warren Buffett, I don't think you have to worry that they're going to go crazy or be soft and foolish just because they don't shout all the time about everything they disapprove of." And Buffett adds the social-organization insight: "if you're in any social organization and you keep belching at the dinner table, you'll be eating in the kitchen before very long. And people won't pay any attention to you." The same session applies this to board behavior generally — most boards never see a comp-committee report voted down because directors are selected for temperament ("they look for cocker spaniels… and make sure the tails are wagging"), and $200–$300K annual retainers are not independence by any meaningful definition. See [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md), [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md). *(M7, M13)*

### The permanent-home moat — a competitive position no one copies

The most complete explanation in the 2014 corpus of why Berkshire's acquisition franchise is structurally non-replicable: "We've kept our word to them. We've been doing it now for 49 years. And we've put ourselves in a class that is hard for other people to compete with." A private-equity firm "doesn't care" about the promises in the back of the annual report — and there is nothing wrong with that, it is just a different business. But the seller who has spent 40 years building something — who rules out competitors because of synergy layoffs, who rules out private equity because of debt-and-flip — reaches the same conclusion every time: "Our competitive advantage is we had no competitors." Munger attributes it to three things stated at the 2013 meeting and amplified here: staying sane when others go crazy; treating subsidiaries the way you'd want to be treated; and being a good partner to people who need capital. The mechanism that keeps copycats away is not strategic — it is *behavioral*, and it is slow. "It's very different," Munger says. "It looks just too hard to do. There's nothing in the American business school teaches people to be like Berkshire." And: "the difficulty with being slow is you're dead before it's finished." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md). *(M12, A16)*

### Capital allocation without tax leakage — the conglomerate's hidden engine

Asked to defend the conglomerate model against its history of failures, Buffett names the structural advantage most observers miss: "We have a system at Berkshire where we can allocate capital without tax consequences. So we can move businesses from See's Candy, to generate surplus capital, to other areas. It doesn't hurt See's in the process, and we can move it, as the textbooks say, to places where capital can be usefully employed, like wind farms or whatever it may be." The failed conglomerates — Litton, LTV, Gulf and Western — were built on serial stock issuance at high multiples to buy businesses at lower multiples, a chain-letter arithmetic with no operational rationale. Berkshire acquires for cash, builds earning power, redeploys cross-entity capital tax-free, and has the optionality of public securities when no acquisition targets appear. Munger names the closest historical analog: the Mellon brothers — "they were willing to own minority interest, they were willing to grow companies… and they were quite different. We're a lot more like the Mellon brothers than we are like Gulf and Western. And the Mellon brothers did very, very well for what, 50, 60, 70 years." The utility model (BHE, renamed from MidAmerican in 2014) makes the opportunity concrete: capital invested in Iowa wind earns 11.6% returns approved by regulators, and those earnings can flow to the next BHE acquisition without a tax payment at the Berkshire level. See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md), [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md). *(M35, A26)*

### The $20 billion floor — cash as oxygen

Berkshire's permanent minimum cash balance gets its most explicit articulation here. The logic is not about returns or opportunity cost — it is existential: "I've always said that cash is — available cash or credit — is a lot like oxygen: that you don't notice it — the lack of it — 99.9 percent of the time. But if it's absent, it's the only thing you notice. And we don't want to be in that position." The $20 billion is held specifically to ensure Berkshire can "never be dependent on the kindness of strangers," regardless of external credit conditions. The 2008-09 crisis lending to Harley-Davidson, Goldman Sachs, General Electric, and Tiffany's was possible precisely because Berkshire's balance sheet never depended on those external credit markets: "when you need cash, it's the only thing you need. And it's because other people aren't coming up with it." Any cash above $20 billion is deployed, but the floor is non-negotiable and structural, not tactical. Munger adds the cost-accounting case for sitting with excess: "in a world where short-term interest rates are half a percent, or lower, we love the opportunity to invest more capital intelligently in the businesses we control." See [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md), [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md). *(M23)*

### Identifying the circle of competence

A shareholder asks the deceptively simple question: how does one figure out what one's circle of competence actually is? Buffett's answer is self-knowledge — "being self-realistic, and that applies outside of business as well" — and concedes he most frequently violated his own circle in retail. "It's easy to sort of think you understand retail, and then subsequently find out you don't." Mrs. B's decision not to take Berkshire stock in 1983 — taking cash instead, because she knew real estate and retailing but not stocks — is named as the clearest example: "she knew exactly what she knew and what she didn't know, and that took her a long, long, long, long way in business life." Munger's method is comparative: size up whether the specific competitive arena requires qualities you genuinely lack, and withdraw. "If you're five-foot-two, you don't have much of a future in the National Basketball League." His operational insight is the most quotable: "what a lot of us need, including the one speaking, is — what I needed to get ahead was to compete against idiots, and luckily there's a large supply." The transferable principle: the circle is identified not by confidence in what you know but by honest accounting of where you have systematically been wrong. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md). *(A7)*

### Why companies make dumb acquisitions — institutional forces, not bad people

The most systematic diagnosis in the 2014 corpus of why the aggregate return on corporate acquisitions is poor. Munger: "I think the sum total of all acquisitions done by American industry will be lousy. It's in the nature of corporations that are prosperous to be talked into dumb deals, and bureaucracy tends to feed on itself and create unnecessary costs." Buffett traces the mechanism: CEOs promoted through operating skill have "animal spirits" and want to do things; they build strategy departments whose job is to generate deals; investment bankers call daily. "What do you think those people are going to do? Sit around and suck their thumbs? No, they're going to keep coming up with deals." The result: "there are all these forces that push toward deals, and if you try to push toward deals, you're going to get a lot of dumb deals." Buffett's governing discipline at Berkshire is to have none of those structures — no strategy department, no standing acquisition mandate — and to feel genuinely uneager. "We try very hard, Charlie and I, not to get eager to do a deal. We're just eager to do a deal that makes sense." The contrast: when he reads another company is making an acquisition he doesn't control, "I'm much more inclined to cry than to smile." See [institutional-imperative](../concepts/institutional-imperative.md), [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md). *(A18)*

### See's Candies and the ignorance-removal machine

Asked why See's profit growth stalled after 1999, Buffett is candid: the boxed-chocolate market is simply not growing and multiple attempts to expand geographically failed. But Munger's deeper answer is the session's best single formulation of how Berkshire learns: "Its main contribution to Berkshire was ignorance removal. And it's not the only big contributor to ignorance removal. If it weren't for the fact we were so good at removing our ignorance, step by step, Berkshire would be practically nothing today. What we knew originally wasn't enough. We were pretty damn stupid when we bought See's. We were just barely smart enough to buy it. And if there's any secret to Berkshire, it's the fact that we're pretty good at ignorance removal." Buffett makes the chain explicit: See's in 1972 taught him about brands, which led to Coca-Cola in 1988. "I wouldn't be at all surprised, if we had not owned See's, whether we would've owned Coca-Cola later on." The mechanism — buy a business just barely inside your circle, learn from operating it, enlarge the circle — is the growth model that looks slow and obvious in retrospect and is extremely hard to copy in advance. See [sees-candies](../entities/sees-candies.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md). *(M27)*

## Q&A digest — Morning session

**M5 · Dividend vote 45-to-1 against.** *Buffett:* shareholders — including B-share holders Berkshire has no ability to contact — voted 45-to-1 against paying a dividend. The result validates the 2012 letter's detailed case that a "sell-down" approach is arithmetically superior to dividends for shareholders who need cash. No proxy solicitation was used; the count reflects organic owner alignment. *Munger:* the Berkshire shareholder base is "understated, frugal people." See [dividend-policy](../concepts/dividend-policy.md).

**★ M7 · Coca-Cola compensation abstention.** *Buffett:* told Muhtar Kent in advance Berkshire would abstain; made the view public immediately after the vote; declined to endorse activist David Winters's calculation (which took dilution figures from the proxy but ignored Coke's systematic buyback of option shares). Net dilution under Buffett's own analysis: ~108 million shares on a 4.4 billion base, or ~2.5% — "far cry from the numbers that were being tossed around." The mechanism of opposing without declaring war: "I think the best result for the Coca-Cola Company was achieved by our abstention." *Munger:* agreed with the course of action before the vote. See [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md), [coca-cola](../entities/coca-cola.md).

**M8 · 3G and Berkshire styles don't blend — but will keep partnering.** *Buffett:* zero-based budgeting and the Berkshire "leave them alone" model are genuinely different; blending them would not work. But 3G are "as able as anybody I've seen in the management of businesses" and "more than fair in everything they do with us." Berkshire will likely partner with them again, possibly in very large transactions. "We've got a system, works very well for us." *Munger:* "I don't think we've ever had a policy that loved overstaffing." See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**M10 · Berkshire underperforms in strong up years — always will.** *Buffett:* Berkshire said explicitly in the 2012 report that if the market had five consecutive strong years, the five-year book-value comparison would be lost. 2013 was exceptional — "there were two years in the last 40 or so that the market was up more." The structural pattern: Berkshire underperforms in strong years, matches in moderate years, outperforms in flat or down years. "Over any cycle, I think we will overperform." *Munger:* Berkshire's book-value gain is after full corporate income taxes while the S&P pays none — "if this is failure, I want more of it." See [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md).

**★ M11 · "Eager" to repurchase at 120% of book — only below intrinsic value.** *Buffett:* Berkshire's buyback trigger is currently 120% of book (book value ~$230 billion), implying willingness to pay up to $276 billion for the company — "a loud shoutout as to a figure that we think is significantly below intrinsic value, or we wouldn't use it to repurchase shares." Critically: "If you buy a dollar bill for 90 cents, you're doing your shareholders a favor. And if you buy it for $1.10, you are doing them no favor at all." Coke's policy of buying back shares to cover options regardless of price violates this principle. *Munger:* "Some people buy their own stock back regardless of price. That's not our system." See [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md), [intrinsic-value-vs-book-value](../concepts/intrinsic-value-vs-book-value.md).

**★ M12 · Why sellers trust Berkshire — 49 years of keeping promises.** *Buffett:* "We've kept our word to them. And now we have to be very careful about what we promise." Berkshire can promise not to sell, can promise that managers keep running their businesses — and lists the explicit things it cannot promise (no layoffs under any circumstances, no closures ever). Private equity is "totally unimpressed" by what's in the back of the annual report — "that's their business, and there's nothing wrong with that." But for the owner who has built something over 40 years and cares where it goes: "We've put ourselves in a class that is hard for other people to compete with. And really, no one else will have much luck in competing with us." *Munger:* "we behave the way we do because we like doing it. And it's worked pretty well." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**★ M13 · Boards are social organisms — "cocker spaniels, not dobermans."** *Buffett:* in 55 years on corporate boards, 19 companies aside from Berkshire, he has never seen a comp committee report receive a dissenting vote. Reason: boards are "part business organizations and part social organizations," comp committees do their work in a smaller group, and the full board delegates to them — "it's almost unheard of to question that." Independent directors receiving $200–300K per year plus prestige "are not independent" by any meaningful standard. They are selected for social temperament: "they look for cocker spaniels. And then they make sure that the tails are wagging." The antidote: "if you're in any social organization and you keep belching at the dinner table, you'll be eating in the kitchen before very long. And people won't pay any attention to you." *Munger:* "people accomplish more if they pick their spots for public disapproval." See [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md), [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).

**★ M14 · Cost of capital — opportunity cost is the only rational definition.** *Buffett:* "Charlie and I always figure that our cost of capital is what could be produced by our second-best idea. And then our best idea has to exceed that." Every CFO "cost of capital" presentation is useless: "I've never seen a CEO who wanted to do a deal where the CFO didn't come in and say it exceeded the cost of capital. It's just a game." The only real test over time: "whether the capital we retain produces more than a dollar of market value as we go along." *Munger:* "I've never heard an intelligent one." And: "The answer's perfectly simple. We're right and they're wrong." See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md), [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md).

**M15 · Nebraska Furniture Mart was not a bargain purchase.** *Buffett:* corrects a shareholder's assumption that Berkshire paid ~85% of book value for Nebraska Furniture Mart. The actual terms: $60M purchase price for 100% value (Berkshire acquired 80%), which was "more than book at the time — not way more, but more than book," and at "probably 11 or 12 times after-tax earnings." "It was not a bargain purchase. It was a great business." The lesson: Buffett did not require a discount to book to buy NFM in 1983; he paid a fair price for a wonderful business — a principle that later formalised into the pay-up-for-quality framework seen in See's and Coca-Cola. See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), [nebraska-furniture-mart](../entities/nebraska-furniture-mart.md).

**M16 · Index fund for wife's trust — peace of mind is the goal, not optimization.** *Buffett:* the 90/10 S&P index / T-bill allocation for his wife's trust is not a statement about Berkshire's future; Berkshire shares from his estate go to five foundations, and trustees are instructed to hold every share through a 12-year distribution window — "my views on Berkshire at least through 12 years after my death are as bullish as anybody could possibly come up with." For the trust, the goal is different: "it is not designed to get even larger amounts of capital. It is just a question of total, 100 percent peace of mind on something that cannot get a bad result." *Munger:* "Warren is a little peculiar in the way he distributes money in the family. And I think he's entitled to do what he damn pleases." See [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md).

**M17 · BNSF service problems — weather and Bakken oil surge combined.** *Buffett:* BNSF handled 53% of all new industry units in 2013; the Bakken shale growth was faster than anticipated and the 2013-2014 winter brought "83 inches of snow in Chicago" and "30-plus days where it didn't get to zero in Minnesota." BNSF CEO Matt Rose: "we know this is an outdoor sport." Capex commitment: $5 billion in 2014 — "no railroad has ever spent that kind of money or even close to it." Recovery: the week of the meeting, BNSF handled 206,000 units — "no other railroad has ever handled 205,000 units." See [bnsf](../entities/bnsf.md).

**M22 · Publishing comp data perversely raises pay across corporate America.** *Buffett:* the SEC's proxy disclosure requirements may actually harm shareholders: "Corporate CEOs, as a group, would be being paid a lot less money if proxy statements hadn't revealed how much other people were getting paid." Every CEO reads the proxy statement of peers and negotiates upward. "There's no CEO that looks at other proxy statements and comes away thinking, 'I should get paid less.'" The Salomon lesson: making a "secret deal" with John Meriwether's group — even well-deserved pay — triggered massive envy and political turmoil across the firm. *Munger:* "envy is doing the country a lot of harm. And our practices are envy dampeners." See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).

**★ M23 · $20 billion minimum — cash is oxygen, not a return opportunity.** *Buffett:* "We will always have $20 billion around Berkshire. We will never be dependent on the kindness of strangers." The benchmark is not return on cash; it is survivability. "There will be some time in the next 100 years, and it may be tomorrow and it may be 100 years from now, and nobody knows, where we cannot depend on anybody else to keep our own strength." Cash availability is like oxygen — "you don't notice it — the lack of it — 99.9 percent of the time. But if it's absent, it's the only thing you notice." Above $20 billion, any excess is deployed intelligently — but the floor is absolute. *Munger:* BHE and BNSF as capital sinks are a "blessing" compared to the earlier Berkshire with no automatic intelligent redeployment. See [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md).

**M26 · "I'm slow to make personnel changes."** *Buffett:* "A clear weak point of mine would be I'm slow to make personnel changes. I mean, I like the managers we have." Acknowledges a case where he and Munger took a beloved manager "directly from an executive chair into an Alzheimer's home" rather than acting sooner. The counterfactual tradeoff: giving managers extraordinary freedom produces enormous gains across dozens of relationships; the occasional failure from under-supervision is the price. "Someone will come along someday and say, 'That wouldn't have happened if you'd followed the procedure that some other company followed.' And they'll be right." *Munger:* "By the standards of the rest of the world, we over trust. And so far, our results have been way better because we carefully selected people because they were going to be over trusted." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**★ M27 · See's Candies and the ignorance-removal machine.** *Buffett:* See's pre-tax profits grew from under $5M in 1972 to $74M in 1999 but have plateaued since; the boxed-chocolate market has declined against salted snacks and geographic expansion has repeatedly failed — "we tried it many times." But the real contribution was educational: See's taught Buffett about brand economics, which led to the Coca-Cola purchase in 1988. "I wouldn't be at all surprised, if we had not owned See's, whether we would've owned Coca-Cola later on." *Munger:* "Its main contribution to Berkshire was ignorance removal… And if there's any secret to Berkshire, it's the fact that we're pretty good at ignorance removal. And the nice thing about that is we have a lot of ignorance left to remove." See [sees-candies](../entities/sees-candies.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ M28 · Bank of America preferred exchange — Graham on non-cumulative preferreds.** *Buffett:* Brian Moynihan asked Berkshire to convert its $5B cumulative preferred to a non-cumulative preferred so BofA could count it as Tier 1 capital. Buffett invokes Graham: "Noncumulative preferreds — Ben Graham wrote about them in the 1934 edition of 'Security Analysis' — they're a terribly weak form of security." The weakness in practice: Freddie Mac and Fannie Mae preferred shareholders "are finding out" exactly how weak, as dividends can simply be skipped. But in a "five-basis-point money" environment Buffett was willing to accept the structurally weaker security in exchange for BofA making the preferred non-callable for five years — ensuring Berkshire is not locked in permanently and can always use it as payment for its warrants. The lesson: the weakness of a security instrument is a pricing variable, not a veto; at the right price and with the right covenants, even Graham's "terribly weak form" can be the right trade. See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [lending-discipline](../concepts/lending-discipline.md).

**M31 · Still reluctant to borrow despite historically cheap rates.** *Buffett:* acknowledges the shareholder's point — at historically low long-term rates, a leveraged conglomerate with strong businesses would mathematically benefit from more debt. "Another 30 or 40 billion of debt at Berkshire would be nothing and it would cost very little." The reasons for restraint: Berkshire already generates cheap leverage through the $77B insurance float; it does not want to re-rate existing bondholders from AA into lower territory; and the utility and railroad subsidiaries can hold more debt than they do. "We don't actually have great places to put it now, as evidenced by the fact that we've got 25 billion or so of excess cash." *Munger:* "Even though what you suggest is intelligent, we're probably not going to do it in advance." See [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md), [float](../concepts/float.md).

**M32 · Climate change is not a near-term investment factor.** *Buffett:* "I don't think in making an investment decision on Berkshire Hathaway, or most companies, virtually all of the companies I can think of, that climate change should be a factor in the decision-making process." On insurance pricing specifically, the year-to-year change in catastrophe probabilities is "extremely low" — too small to materially affect annual repricing. *Munger:* "a lot of the people who think they know how climate change is going to change weather patterns and hurricanes are overclaiming." Berkshire is well-positioned in renewables regardless of the trajectory — "we just stumbled into it." The transferable lesson: long-dated planetary risk and short-dated pricing signals operate on different timescales; conflating them produces both bad investment decisions and bad insurance pricing. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md).

**M33 · Combs and Weschler at $7B+ each — full-autonomy delegation.** *Buffett:* Todd Combs and Ted Weschler each manage "a little over 7 billion now," up from ~$3B each in early 2012, with roles expanded beyond investment management into deal execution and diligence at no additional compensation. "They don't ask for extra compensation, at all, because they do it." The lesson: genuine delegation to trusted talent means no second-guessing and no incremental pay for incremental scope — alignment comes from character selection at the outset, not ongoing incentive architecture. *Munger:* "Nothing to add." See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).

**★ M35 · The conglomerate's hidden engine: tax-free capital reallocation.** *Buffett:* the great conglomerate failures — Litton, LTV, Gulf and Western — were chain-letter schemes built on issuing overpriced stock to buy lower-multiple businesses. Berkshire is different in several ways: it acquires for cash; it has optionality in public securities when no acquisitions are compelling; it does not feel "hell bent to buy something quite regularly." And the core structural advantage: "We have a system at Berkshire where we can allocate capital without tax consequences. So we can move businesses from See's Candy, to generate surplus capital, to other areas." No standalone business can do this without triggering a tax. *Munger:* the historical parallel is the Mellon brothers, not Gulf and Western — "willing to own minority interest, willing to grow companies… and the Mellon brothers did very, very well for 50, 60, 70 years." See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md), [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [tax-deferral](../concepts/tax-deferral.md).

**M36 · Forest River / Pete Liegl — decentralization at full extension.** *Buffett:* Forest River will do over $4 billion in revenue in 2014, making it probably one of Berkshire's larger operating subsidiaries by revenue. Buffett has never visited; has had three or four phone calls in the ownership period. Liegl's IT department for a $4B business consists of six people. "It's his company." The initial comp arrangement — settled in one afternoon in Omaha when Liegl came to see Buffett — has never been changed by either side. "I wish we had 20 like it." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).

**M38 · Hedge fund bet, year 6 — S&P running away.** *Buffett:* six years into the 10-year bet, the S&P has led for five of the six years. The zero-coupon bonds bought to fund the $1M prize rose to near par as rates collapsed; the parties swapped into Berkshire stock (with Buffett guaranteeing a $1M floor), now worth ~$1.2M. The bet illustrates: "the investment world has been very good at extracting a very significant percentage of the returns that investors get for themselves." See [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md), [investment-friction](../concepts/investment-friction.md).

## Q&A digest — Afternoon session

**★ A2 · Energy Future Holdings — candid admission; change surveillance required.** *Buffett:* "That was just a mistake on my part. It was a big — it was a significant mistake, and we will make mistakes in the future." EFH's investment thesis rested on one assumption — gas prices staying high or rising — that simply proved wrong. "At that point the whole place toppled." The broader lesson: "All businesses should constantly be thinking about what can mess up their business model." GEICO's history is the counter-model — founded in 1936 on a low-cost direct-distribution model, it adapted from U.S. mail to telephone to internet to social media. "Change is going on all the time, and it's going on with all of our businesses." *Munger:* "There's another trick, which is scrambling out of your mistakes. And we've been quite good at both." See [mistakes-of-omission](../concepts/mistakes-of-omission.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**A4 · Phase 2 Berkshire — private businesses now dwarf the stock portfolio.** *Buffett:* "What really turns us on is finding a business that we want to buy, and that fits well for Berkshire, and that'll be earning money for Berkshire 10 and 20 and 50 years from now." Looking back at 2008-09: spent $16B too early (the March 2009 bottom was significantly lower than October 2008); buying Harley-Davidson bonds at 15% when the stock was the better bet. "Looking back, the most money would have been made just by buying a bundle of stocks." But the BNSF acquisition late in 2009 "will be an enormous part of our future." *Munger:* in the early years, stocks exceeded the private-business value — "like a big portfolio of common stocks and a lot of businesses thrown in as extras." "And now, of course, the private companies are worth way more than the stocks. And I would guess that will continue." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses](../concepts/cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses.md).

**★ A5 · Self-driving cars are a real GEICO threat — but change is slow and won't trigger a sale.** *Buffett:* usage-based pricing (Progressive's Snapshot) is a valuable input variable for assessing loss probability — no argument there. GEICO has its own system built over many decades; GEICO's claim: "there's nobody better at it, in terms of auto insurance, than the GEICO people." On self-driving cars: "that is a real threat to the auto insurance industry. If that proves successful and reduces accidents dramatically, it will be very good for society and it will be very bad for auto insurers." But: "it is hard to figure out how it could have a major impact in 10 years." Would Berkshire ever sell GEICO if analysis showed a challenging future? "No." *Munger:* "Some of these things happen a lot more slowly than you might think. I went to a program at Harvard, at least 30 years ago, describing how color movies were going to come to the house on demand, and they were just around the corner. Well, they've come, but it was 30 years later." See [geico](../entities/geico.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**★ A7 · Circle of competence — identifying the perimeter, not the center.** *Buffett:* "It is a question of being self-realistic, and that applies outside of business as well." Most often violated his own circle in retail — "it's easy to sort of think you understand retail." Mrs. B's decision to take cash rather than Berkshire stock in 1983 is the cleanest example: "She did not know anything about stock, but she knew a lot about what to do with cash. She knew real estate, she knew retailing, and she knew exactly what she knew and what she didn't know, and that took her a long, long, long, long way in business life." How to identify the circle: notice where you have systematically been wrong; ask friends who know you well. *Munger:* use the large-obvious-disqualifier method: "If you're five-foot-two, you don't have much of a future in the National Basketball League… If you're 95 years of age, you probably shouldn't try and act the romantic lead part in Hollywood." And his operational corollary: "what a lot of us need, including the one speaking, is — what I needed to get ahead was to compete against idiots, and luckily there's a large supply." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [rose-blumkin](../people/rose-blumkin.md).

**A8 · Book value vs. S&P comparison (Munger: "it's insane").** *Munger:* "The answer is you're totally right, and we do that because Warren wants to make it eccentrically difficult for himself. So if you don't understand people who like to wear hair shirts, you'll never figure out why anybody would do such a thing. It's a ridiculous way to make a comparison, but it makes it hard for Warren to look good. And he likes to climb mountains that are difficult. But it's insane, you're right." *Buffett:* "Normally when he goes all wishy washy like that, I like to clarify. But I don't think I'll try." See [intrinsic-value-vs-book-value](../concepts/intrinsic-value-vs-book-value.md).

**★ A12 · Will GEICO overtake State Farm? — "holy duty" to low price.** *Buffett:* GEICO passed Allstate in 2014 to become #2 in auto insurance with 10% market share; State Farm has ~19% and a 60–70B net worth plus a strong homeowners presence. Buffett's prediction: GEICO will gain share "month after month, year after year" as long as it keeps rates low and properly assesses risk. At the current trajectory: "if I live to be 100, we should be number one. I'm going to do my part." Under Tony Nicely (CEO since 1993), market share has risen from ~2% to 10%+. *Munger:* "GEICO to me is very much like Costco. And one of the reasons it's succeeded is that they really feel a holy duty to have a wonderful product at a very low price. A lot of people talk that game, but very few have it just right down under the body and soul of the company." *Supersession flag:* GEICO experienced severe underwriting losses and market-share decline in 2022–2023 as inflation and claims costs spiked before management responded with aggressive rate increases; the long-term market-share gain resumed by 2024 but the trajectory Buffett described proved non-linear. See [geico](../entities/geico.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**A14 · Berkshire doesn't begrudge paying taxes.** *Buffett:* Berkshire paid $8.9 billion in taxes in 2013 and will not engage in corporate inversions or other tax-minimization structures. "We could not have done Berkshire in any other country except the United States." The company does utilize legal tax advantages — low-income housing tax credits, wind and solar tax incentives — and those actually drive the economics of the renewable energy investments. "We follow the rules. But we don't begrudge the taxes we pay. We've earned a lot of money while paying U.S. taxes." *Munger:* "I think it would be crazy to be as prosperous as Berkshire and get our tax to zero while we remain this prosperous. That would not be a legitimate ideal." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**★ A16 · Intrinsic value — Aesop's formulation and why copycats don't emerge.** *Buffett:* intrinsic value is "what you might call private business value" — the present value of all cash distributed from now to judgment day. Aesop's "a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush" is the complete formulation: the investment question is always how certain the bush, how far away, what are interest rates. Graham emphasized the quantitative factors (two birds' worth of cash for one dollar now); Fisher and Munger emphasized the qualitative factors (how many birds will be in the bush). Neither disagreed with the underlying math. On why Berkshire has no real copycats: *Munger:* the Ed Davis analogy — a surgeon whose technique was so much better (2% death rate vs. 20%) that other surgeons watched and simply concluded "I don't think I'll try and copy that." "Nothing in the American business school teaches people to be like Berkshire." And: "the difficulty with being slow is you're dead before it's finished." See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**A17 · Inflation's asymmetric effect — equities survive Weimar; fixed-income doesn't.** *Buffett:* inflation hurts Berkshire as it hurts most businesses; the leveraged home-and-mortgage case is the exception (inflation wipes out the mortgage but you keep the home). *Munger:* "We had a test of hyperinflation in Weimar Germany, and the people who owned stocks in places like Berkshire got through. And they didn't prosper joyously, but they got through. And everybody else, practically, life insurance policies, bank deposits, you name it, got wiped out." The systematic takeaway: equity ownership in businesses with real earning power is the best long-run protection against inflation, but highly levered businesses benefit most from the inflation itself and suffer most from the reversal. See [bonds-and-inflation](../concepts/bonds-and-inflation.md).

**★ A18 · Why companies make dumb acquisitions — institutional forces, not bad people.** *Munger:* "I think the sum total of all acquisitions done by American industry will be lousy. It's in the nature of corporations that are prosperous to be talked into dumb deals, and bureaucracy tends to feed on itself and create unnecessary costs." *Buffett:* "When we read that a company we don't control is going to make an acquisition, I'm much more inclined to cry than to smile." The mechanism: CEOs with "animal spirits" build strategy departments whose entire job is to propose deals; investment bankers call daily; "there are all these forces that push toward deals, and if you try to push toward deals, you're going to get a lot of dumb deals." Berkshire's insulation: no strategy department, no standing acquisition staff at headquarters, and management that is "not eager to do a deal — we're just eager to do a deal that makes sense." The GEICO example: after recovery, GEICO made two acquisitions that "weren't disasters, but weren't successes," and the secondary cost — 12 years of foregone growth — dwarfed the accounting write-offs. *Munger:* "Some are mediocre." See [institutional-imperative](../concepts/institutional-imperative.md), [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**A19 · Prosecute individuals, not corporations.** *Buffett:* "I lean way more toward prosecution of individuals than corporations." At Salomon, a bad act by a couple of people came close to destroying thousands of lives. Prosecuting the corporation is easy — they write a check — while prosecuting individuals is hard and uncertain. The perverse result: corporations accept penalties with other people's money; no individual changes behavior. *Munger:* prosecuting Boy Scout leaders for steel price-fixing "changed behavior of American businessmen." "A few criminal prosecutions do change behavior a lot." The broader principle: fear of personal consequences is far more potent a behavioral modifier than fear of corporate fines. See [salomon](../entities/salomon.md), [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

**★ A23 · Activism — capital flows until the alpha disappears.** *Buffett:* activist funds "scare the hell out of a lot of managers"; they attract more and more money as long as the strategy looks successful — "anything in Wall Street that looks like it's successful will generate a funds flow that will, you know, go on until it's no longer successful." *Munger:* activism is "causing more of a stir in corporate management than anything has in years — practically nobody feels immune." When an activist enters a company, 20–30% of the stock can change hands rapidly and seemingly entrenched management suddenly finds itself threatened. Munger predicts the trend will be bigger in three years: "It's really serious." On bad activist-company pairings — an activist you wouldn't want in the family going after a company you'd never buy — Munger invokes Oscar Wilde's definition of fox hunting: "The pursuit of the uneatable by the unspeakable." The transferable lesson: institutional capital perpetuates any successful-looking strategy until its edge is competed away; the same mechanism that built the activist wave will eventually crowd it out. See [institutional-imperative](../concepts/institutional-imperative.md), [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

**A26 · MidAmerican/BHE utility returns — reinvestment model, not current yield.** *Buffett:* the correct metric for a regulated utility under heavy capital investment is *operating earnings after depreciation*, not cash flow minus capex — the latter produces misleadingly negative figures. The bet: Berkshire deploys growth capital into projects (e.g., Iowa wind at 11.6% approved return), earns on that capital, keeps rates below competitors, and builds the regulatory goodwill that enables future investments. *Greg Abel:* BHE's last Iowa rate increase before 2014 was in 1998; Google is building a 1,000-megawatt data-center footprint in Iowa partly because of BHE's low rates and renewable-energy generation. See [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md), [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**A29 · Housing finance — keep the government in, but don't let it own the portfolio risk.** *Buffett:* the 30-year fixed-rate mortgage is a significant social good; no private insurer can handle the $11 trillion market. Keep the government guarantee, but redesign Fannie/Freddie to avoid the temptation of portfolio activities that turned them into "the biggest hedge funds in the country." Berkshire is unlikely to participate in any government mortgage-risk arrangement. *Munger:* "When private industry was allowed to take over pretty much the whole field, we got the biggest bunch of thieves and idiots you can imagine screwing up the whole system." The existing structure — Fannie and Freddie being fairly conservative post-crisis — is "OK." See [lending-discipline](../concepts/lending-discipline.md).

**A30 · 3G and deserving a good partner.** *Buffett:* Berkshire is "very fortunate to be associated with" 3G (Jorge Paulo Lemann and associates): "they're very smart, they're very focused, they're very hardworking and determined. They're never satisfied." Post-Buffett, whatever advantage currently comes from his personal brand "will become the Berkshire brand. The first year or so people will wonder about it, but the person that follows me will bring the same qualities." *Munger:* "The way to get a good spouse is to deserve one… to get a good partner you deserve a good partner. It's an old-fashioned way of getting ahead. And the interesting about it is it still works in these modern times." See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**A32 · Tech disruption and circle of competence.** *Buffett:* on Uber, Airbnb, and the sharing economy — "we know there'll be change, and we don't know who the winners will be. And we try to stick with businesses where we know the winners." The State Farm/GEICO mousetrap analogy: State Farm came along with a better mousetrap than the agency model, then GEICO came along with a better mousetrap yet, and the incumbents fought back through legislation and competitive response — "in the end the better mouse trap usually wins." The implication: Berkshire avoids fields where it cannot identify the winners in advance. *Munger:* "the new technology is going to be quite disruptive to a lot of people. I think retailing, in particular, is facing some very significant threats." Asked where Berkshire is most vulnerable: "I don't think I want to name them." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

## Connections to other sources

- The Coca-Cola abstention (M7) is the 2014 meeting's defining event in the governance literature; it echoes the 1994 Salomon chapter ([berkshire-1994-meeting](berkshire-1994-meeting.md) M30) where Buffett argued that leverage "is tolerable only with the right operators" — here he applies the same selectivity to when and how to deploy his governance influence.
- The $20B liquidity floor (M23) is the 2014 articulation of the "cash is king at the right moment" principle that Buffett demonstrated at the 2008-09 nadir; [berkshire-2008-meeting](berkshire-2008-meeting.md) and [berkshire-2009-meeting](berkshire-2009-meeting.md) show the floor in deployment; this meeting is the clearest statement of *why* the floor is structural, not opportunistic.
- The conglomerate model defense (M35) directly continues the 2013 meeting's "reluctant-seller" and "800-number" arguments ([berkshire-2013-meeting](berkshire-2013-meeting.md) M27, M25): in 2013 Munger explains *what* Berkshire offers reluctant sellers; in 2014 Buffett explains the *financial mechanism* (tax-free reallocation) that makes the model structurally superior to the alternative.
- The "ignorance removal" formulation (M27) is Munger's clearest published summary of how the cigar-butt-to-wonderful-business evolution actually worked — complementing the detailed narrative in [berkshire-2013-meeting](berkshire-2013-meeting.md) A2 ("intensity is cumulative"), where the same learning arc was traced through American Express; here it is stated as a general operating principle.
- The dumb-acquisitions diagnosis (A18) extends the institutional-imperative theme from the 1994 meeting ([berkshire-1994-meeting](berkshire-1994-meeting.md) A24 — "the piano player handed a violin at Carnegie Hall") by identifying not just that most CEOs can't allocate capital but the *organizational structures* (strategy departments, investment bankers) that make bad deals structurally inevitable in publicly traded companies.
- The self-driving car exchange (A5) is the first meeting appearance of autonomous vehicles as a structural GEICO risk; it establishes Munger's "these things happen slower than you think" baseline that the 2023-2025 meetings will have to revisit as Waymo and Tesla's FSD expanded.
