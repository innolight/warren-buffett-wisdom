# Berkshire Hathaway 1982 Shareholder Letter

Operating earnings $31.5M, only 9.8% on beginning equity — the worst year in recent memory by that yardstick, "far below our recent high of 19.4% in 1978." Book value per share grew 40% (driven largely by GEICO's market gain) to $737.43; 18-year compound 22.0%. The letter is dense with three landmark essays: **abandoning beginning-ROE as the dominant yardstick**, the **commodity-business framework** (with insurance as the textbook case), and the **stock-as-acquisition-currency** essay. The first publication of Berkshire's six-point acquisition ad appears here.

## Key themes

- **Discarding the beginning-ROE yardstick.** Buffett's explicit reversal: with non-controlled ownership earnings now structurally exceeding reported operating earnings, single-year ROE on beginning equity has lost its utility *for Berkshire specifically.* "Yardsticks seldom are discarded while yielding favorable readings. But when results deteriorate, most managers favor disposition of the yardstick rather than disposition of the manager." Buffett pre-empts the suspicion by acknowledging it. See [return-on-equity-yardstick](../concepts/return-on-equity-yardstick.md).
- **"Economic" vs. "accounting" earnings.** A formalization of [look-through-earnings](../concepts/look-through-earnings.md). Examples: GEICO 1982 dividends $3.5M after tax vs. ~$23M attributable undistributed earnings; "if GEICO had earned less money in 1982 but had paid an additional $1 million in dividends, our reported earnings would have been larger despite the poorer business results." Accounting numbers are "the beginning, not the end, of business valuation."
- **Manic-depressive lemmings.** Public markets as auction venues whose mispricings are a feature, not a bug, for disciplined value-oriented buyers: "fractional-interest purchases can be made in an auction market where prices are set by participants with behavior patterns that sometimes resemble those of an army of manic-depressive lemmings."
- **Commodity-business economics.** A textbook framework: industries with substantial over-capacity + undifferentiated product + no administered pricing = poor profitability. Escape requires a cost advantage that is *both wide and sustainable.* Insurance is identified explicitly as a textbook case ("Many insureds, including the managers of large businesses, do not even know the names of their insurers"). GEICO is the canonical exception — a company with "extreme and improving operating efficiency" — what later wiki language calls a [moat](../concepts/moat.md). See [commodity-business-economics](../concepts/commodity-business-economics.md).
- **Insurance has structurally changed.** From 1950–1970 the industry combined ratio averaged 99.0 — but that was an artifact of quasi-administered pricing, where "Giant A charged the same price as Giant B." That regime is gone for good. "Future profitability of the industry will be determined by current competitive characteristics, not past ones. Many managers have been slow to recognize this. It's not only generals that prefer to fight the last war."
- **Stock-as-acquisition-currency.** A landmark essay. When a buyer's stock trades below intrinsic value, using it as currency means "giving up $2 of value to receive $1 of value." Sellers in negotiated transactions get full intrinsic business value; acquirers paying with shares get only what the market currently grants. Three remedies: (1) true business-value-for-business-value mergers (rare); (2) issue shares only when stock trades at or above intrinsic value; (3) commit to repurchasing an equivalent number of shares after the deal (damage-repair). Three favorite management rationalizations are dismantled. The linguistic reframe: "Part of A sold to acquire B," not "A acquires B." See [stock-as-acquisition-currency](../concepts/stock-as-acquisition-currency.md).
- **Dilution is misframed.** Standard EPS-based dilution math misses what counts — dilution *in intrinsic business value*. "There have been plenty of mergers, non-dilutive in this limited sense, that were instantly value destroying for the acquirer."
- **The six-point acquisition ad — first publication.** (1) Large purchases (≥$5M after-tax earnings). (2) Demonstrated consistent earning power; no projections, no turnarounds. (3) Good ROE while employing little or no debt. (4) Management in place. (5) Simple businesses. (6) An offering price. "We will not engage in unfriendly transactions… customarily within five minutes." See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).
- **GEICO is the high-profit exception in a commodity industry.** Berkshire's 35% interest represents ~$250M of premium volume, "an amount considerably greater than all of the direct volume we produce."

## Notable quotes

> Yardsticks seldom are discarded while yielding favorable readings. But when results deteriorate, most managers favor disposition of the yardstick rather than disposition of the manager.

> Like the Lord, [the market] helps those who help themselves. But, unlike the Lord, the market does not forgive those who know not what they do.

> A few producers in such industries may consistently do well if they have a cost advantage that is both wide and sustainable. By definition such exceptions are few, and, in many industries, are non-existent.

> It's not only generals that prefer to fight the last war. Most business and investment analysis also comes from the rear-view mirror.

> The acquirer who nevertheless barges ahead ends up using an undervalued (market value) currency to pay for a fully valued (negotiated value) property. In effect, the acquirer must give up $2 of value to receive $1 of value… For gold valued as gold cannot be purchased intelligently through the utilization of gold — or even silver — valued as lead.

> A cumulation of small managerial stupidities will produce a major stupidity — not a major triumph.

> If corporate pregnancy is going to be the consequence of corporate mating, the time to face that fact is before the moment of ecstasy.
