# Berkshire Hathaway 2022 Annual Letter

The 2022 letter is shorter and more distilled than most recent installments. Its center of gravity is the "Secret Sauce" case study — the 28-year Coca-Cola and American Express compounding arcs — which articulates a portfolio-level law: a few great holdings compound to dominate while mediocre alternatives shrink to insignificance. The letter delivers the richest single-letter collection of Munger aphorisms in the series, issues a new warning that even Berkshire's preferred operating earnings figure can be manipulated, and records float reaching $164 billion following the Alleghany acquisition.

## Key themes

- **"The flowers bloom, weeds wither" — portfolio compounding asymmetry.** From identical $1.3B starting points (both in the mid-1990s): Coke now pays $704M in annual dividends (up from $75M in 1994) and is worth $25B; AmEx now pays $302M (up from $41M in 1995) and is worth $22B. A flat $1.3B bond held over the same period would now represent a shrinking 0.3% of Berkshire's net worth, delivering unchanged income of ~$80M. "The lesson for investors: The weeds wither away in significance as the flowers bloom. Over time, it takes just a few winners to work wonders." The portfolio-weight argument for holding a few wonderful businesses for decades.
- **GAAP earnings "100% misleading" quarterly.** The 2022 quarterly table makes the case vividly: Q2 GAAP earnings of −$43.8B against operating earnings of $9.3B; Q4 GAAP of +$18.2B against operating earnings of $6.7B. The underlying businesses set a record ($30.8B operating earnings for the full year). The quarterly volatility is described as "in no way unusual."
- **Operating earnings manipulation — a new caveat.** A step beyond the prior GAAP distortion critique: even Berkshire's preferred operating earnings figure "can easily be manipulated by managers who wish to do so." "Bold imaginative accounting" requires no talent — only "a deep desire to deceive." Buffett adds: this extends to sophisticated CEOs, directors, advisors, reporters, and analysts who collectively embrace and celebrate the practice.
- **Float reaches $164B via Alleghany; 8,000-fold from the 1967 origin.** The Alleghany Corporation acquisition (a P&C insurer; Joe Brandon returns as CEO) drove float from $147B to $164B. "Since purchasing our first property-casualty insurer in 1967, Berkshire's float has increased 8,000-fold through acquisitions, operations and innovations." The GAAP treatment continues to understate value: float is recorded as a GAAP liability while its revolving, long-duration character is not.
- **Repurchases: 1.2% buyback; political defense.** Berkshire retired 1.2% of its outstanding shares in 2022. Apple and AmEx concurrently reduced their share counts, increasing Berkshire's ownership "without any cost to us" — the double-compounding mechanism first articulated in [[berkshire-2020-letter]] continues. The sharpest political statement in the series: "When you are told that all repurchases are harmful to shareholders or to the country, or to CEOs, you are listening to either an economic illiterate or a silver-tongued demagogue (characters that are not mutually exclusive)."
- **Munger's distilled investment wisdom — 15 aphorisms from a recent podcast.** The richest single collection of Munger principles in any letter. Key articulations: patience as a learnable skill with long attention span as "huge advantage"; leverage as a zero-product risk ("a string of wonderful numbers times zero will always equal zero — don't count on getting rich twice"); great companies keep working after you aren't; willingness to adapt when the world changes (the railroad admission). Buffett's addendum to the entire section: "Find a very smart high-grade partner – preferably slightly older than you – and then listen very carefully to what he says."
- **"Business-pickers, not stock-pickers."** The two-category ownership framework restated: "We own publicly-traded stocks based on our expectations about their long-term business performance, not because we view them as vehicles for adroit purchases and sales. That point is crucial: Charlie and I are not stock-pickers; we are business-pickers."
- **CEO as Chief Risk Officer.** A governance principle stated plainly: "Our CEO will always be the Chief Risk Officer — a task it is irresponsible to delegate." And on succession: "our future CEOs will have a significant part of their net worth in Berkshire shares, bought with their own money" — aligning successor interests with all shareholders.

## Notable quotes

> The lesson for investors: The weeds wither away in significance as the flowers bloom. Over time, it takes just a few winners to work wonders. And, yes, it helps to start early and live into your 90s as well. — Warren Buffett

> The GAAP earnings are 100% misleading when viewed quarterly or even annually. — Warren Buffett

> When you are told that all repurchases are harmful to shareholders or to the country, or to CEOs, you are listening to either an economic illiterate or a silver-tongued demagogue (characters that are not mutually exclusive). — Warren Buffett

> "Bold imaginative accounting," as a CEO once described his deception to me, has become one of the shames of capitalism. — Warren Buffett

> Even the operating earnings figure that we favor can easily be manipulated by managers who wish to do so. Such tampering is often thought of as sophisticated by CEOs, directors and their advisors. It requires no talent to manipulate numbers: Only a deep desire to deceive is required. — Warren Buffett

> Charlie and I are not stock-pickers; we are business-pickers. — Warren Buffett

> The world is full of foolish gamblers, and they will not do as well as the patient investor. — Charlie Munger

> Patience can be learned. Having a long attention span and the ability to concentrate on one thing for a long time is a huge advantage. — Charlie Munger

> There is no such thing as a 100% sure thing when investing. Thus, the use of leverage is dangerous. A string of wonderful numbers times zero will always equal zero. Don't count on getting rich twice. — Charlie Munger

> A great company keeps working after you are not; a mediocre company won't do that. — Charlie Munger

> You have to keep learning if you want to become a great investor. When the world changes, you must change. — Charlie Munger

> Warren and I hated railroad stocks for decades, but the world changed and finally the country had four huge railroads of vital importance to the American economy. We were slow to recognize the change, but better late than never. — Charlie Munger

> Warren, think more about it. You're smart and I'm right. — Charlie Munger (his decision-clinching formula for decades)

> Find a very smart high-grade partner – preferably slightly older than you – and then listen very carefully to what he says. — Warren Buffett (his Munger addendum)
