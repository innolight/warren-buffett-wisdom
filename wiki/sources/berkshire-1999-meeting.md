# Berkshire Hathaway 1999 Annual Meeting

The 1999 meeting sits at the very peak of the dot-com mania, and it reads like a long, patient argument against the spirit of its age. Berkshire's own stock had lagged badly, the businesses were "fine" but new money was piling up with nowhere cheap to go, and the audience kept handing Buffett and Munger the internet, technology, China, communications — every hot story of 1999 — and asking why they wouldn't play. Their answer is a unified theory of refusal: that **change is a threat to the investor, not an opportunity**; that **equities cannot, by arithmetic, keep compounding faster than the businesses underneath them**; that **leverage and derivatives are how brilliant people go broke** (LTCM is the meeting's morality tale); and that **a moat is just a measure of how certain the future cash is** — so they would rather trade a big payoff for a certain one. Underneath the macro caution, two specific 1999 facts quietly seed later trouble: General Re's float wasn't growing, its Unicover workers'-comp book had just blown a $275M hole, and it carried a $400M derivative book they pledged to "look at very carefully." Per the wiki's two-layer convention, a short distilled top precedes a **comprehensive Q&A digest** that captures every value-bearing exchange.

## How to read the Q&A digest

## Key themes

### Change is the investor's enemy, not its friend — buy the absence of change

In the year the world fell in love with disruption, Buffett inverts the whole premise: "We view change as more of a threat into the investment process than an opportunity. That's quite contrary to the way most people are looking at equities now" (A2). The job is "looking for the absence of change to protect ways that are already making a lot of money" — Coca-Cola sells a product "very, very similar to one that was sold 110-plus years ago," so an analysis from 50 years ago "can pretty well serve as an analysis now." This is why the pair decline the internet, software, communications, and tech wholesale: not from a negative view but because the future isn't legible to them. "We are perfectly willing to trade away a big payoff for a certain payoff. And that's the way we're put together" (A8). The corollary lesson, stated through the auto and airplane industries, is that **spotting a transformative industry tells you almost nothing about how to get rich from it**: "millions of people are flying around every day… the number of people who've made money carrying them around is very limited" (M21). See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [commodity-business-economics](../concepts/commodity-business-economics.md).

### The arithmetic of expectations: stocks can't outrun the businesses underneath them

The meeting's central valuation warning is delivered as plain math (A3). The Fortune 500 earned ~$334B on a ~$10.5T market cap; "the only money investors are going to make, in the long run, are what the businesses make… nobody's adding to the pot." If U.S. GDP grows 4–5% with low inflation, corporate profits — already high as a share of GDP — cannot grow faster ("in the end, they'd be greater than GDP"), so "it is nonsense" to expect equities to compound at 15%. Munger's coda: "If a thing can't go on forever, it will eventually stop," and his friend Fred Stanback's line, "People who expect perpetual growth in real wealth in a finite earth are either mad men or economists." Even the wonderful companies are caught: Coke at 35x and the rest of the great franchises "all strike us as being quite full," so "our enthusiasm for buying more… is less than it was" (M39). The instruction is to "dramatically reduce" return expectations. See [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md), [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md).

### Leverage and derivatives are how brilliant people go broke

LTCM is the year's parable (M8–M10). Sixteen of the highest-IQ, most-experienced people in finance, with their own net worth on the line, were "broke" on a day in September — and "whenever a really bright person goes broke… it's because of leverage." The gain/loss arithmetic is the lesson: "Why do very bright people risk losing something that's very important to them, to gain something that's totally unimportant? The added money has no utility whatsoever." It is "like playing Russian roulette" — and there is "no price" worth a single bullet when you're already rich ("You only have to get rich once"). Derivatives are the accelerant: they let traders evade margin rules and finance equity positions "a hundred percent," while accounting that "front-ends profits" pays people on marks that "don't come to fruition for a long time" (A34, M10 Munger). Berkshire "never used any real amount of borrowed money" — "What's two percentage points more… and run the risk of real failure?" See [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md), [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md).

### A moat measures the certainty of future cash — and a big enough one forgives the manager

Asked to "drill down" on moats and management, Buffett reframes both as inputs to one thing: "the degree of certainty that we attribute to the stream of cash… that we expect in the future and the amount of it" (A20). The discount rate is the Treasury rate, used only "to get comparability across time and across companies"; the real work is judging width and durability ("there's no formula that says the moat is 28 feet wide and 16 feet deep"). The Coca-Cola can is the exhibit — a "share of mind" so strong that people can identify it blindfolded by the container. And the punchline ties moat to management: "If you have a big enough moat, you don't need as much management" — Peter Lynch's business "so good that an idiot can run it, because sooner or later one will." See [moat](../concepts/moat.md), [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md).

### Investing is reporting: build the story yourself, think for yourself, start early

The richest how-to of the meeting (A31, A15, A16) recasts security analysis as journalism. "It's an investigative process. It's a journalistic process. And in the end, you want to write the story." The method: immerse in a handful of companies as if you were going to run or buy them, get years of reports and articles, and interrogate competitors with the "silver bullet" question — "If you had a silver bullet and could only put it through the head of one of your competitors, which one would it be and why?" Munger's deeper version is to keep asking "Why? Why? Why?" and relate the answers "to a structure of deep theory." None of it can be outsourced or borrowed: "You can't look around for people to agree with you… you have to think for yourself." On compounding, "we started building this little snowball on top of a very long hill" — start young (Munger: the first $100,000 is the hard part, helped by being "passionate about being rational" and steadily underspending your income). See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md).

### Berkshire's edge is structural, not magical: cheap float, non-interference, no outsourcing

Pressed repeatedly on how Berkshire adds value, the pair give an almost anti-heroic answer. They grow low-cost float "as fast as we can" while keeping its cost near zero (A12) — the engine they "think about all the time." They add value to wholly-owned businesses chiefly by *not interfering*: giving terrific managers "a very rational owner" so they can spend "a hundred percent of their time focused on what counts," where "the CEOs of most public companies waste a third of their time" on constituencies that "take the company backwards" (A32). And they refuse to delegate capital: "We will never turn our money over to somebody else… we're going to come back and look you in the eye and tell you how we lost it" (A28). Munger's gloss on the whole model: most of what they do "is just not interfere in a counterproductive way. And that non-interference has enormous value." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [float](../concepts/float.md).

### Honest accounting and candor are non-negotiable filters

Buffett spends real energy on accounting hygiene at the bubble's peak — because deception was epidemic. Companies are "hooked" on not expensing options ("corporate America's hooked on it," M18), and the new vogue is "big bath accounting, and the subsequent release back into earnings of taking an overly large bath" (M33, Munger), which Arthur Levitt's SEC was finally attacking. The investor's defense is the same as the issuer's duty: postmortems. "I think you're a better doctor if you drop by the pathology department, occasionally" — Buffett volunteers the McDonald's sale as a mistake that "cost you in the area of a billion dollars-plus," and contrasts every sale decision against every purchase (A29). The standing promise: "We don't sugarcoat things, and we don't put spin on things… if we fail there it's because we set out to fail" (A35). See [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md), [mistakes-of-omission](../concepts/mistakes-of-omission.md).

## Q&A digest — Morning session

**★ M6 · Buy businesses; don't predict the market.** *Buffett:* "Charlie and I don't think about the market," and Graham "made a mistake to occasionally try and place a value on it." They have "great trouble" finding businesses they like at attractive prices — "that is not a stock market forecast in any way." When they can't find things, "the money piles up"; nobody "made a lot of money predicting the actions of the market itself." *Munger:* "How could you say it any better?" See [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

**M7 · Gen Re's incremental float value is "zero" today — and float won't grow soon.** *Buffett:* Berkshire brings "nothing to the party that Gen Re's own investment people would not have brought"; the ~$24B of Gen Re/Cologne assets add no managerial value *yet*, though "over a long period of time" they should. At ~$6B of premium and ~$14B of float, "you won't have growth of float unless premium volume becomes significantly higher." Folds **M15** (*Buffett:* the best financing is cheap float; Berkshire would only borrow "moderate amounts" if it ran out of float and still saw opportunity — "we would never borrow huge amounts of money"). See [float](../concepts/float.md), [general-re](../entities/general-re.md).

**★ M8–M10 · LTCM, leverage, and the Russian-roulette arithmetic.** *Buffett:* Berkshire made "a firm bid for 100 billion-plus of balance sheet assets" for LTCM that the principals declined; the report's claim that hedge funds began in 1949 is wrong (Graham-Newman ran one in the 1920s). The deeper lesson: 16 of the brightest, most-experienced people, with their own money up, were "broke" in September — "whenever a really bright person goes broke… it's because of leverage." "Why do people risk losing something very important to gain something totally unimportant? The added money has no utility… it's like playing Russian roulette." Derivatives let them evade margin rules and finance equity "a hundred percent." *Munger:* "the general system of finance in America involving derivatives is irresponsible… way too much risk in all these trillions of notational value," with "no clearing system" and "very weak" accounting "favored by people who are sharing in the profits." See [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md), [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md), [arbitrage](../concepts/arbitrage.md).

**★ M11 · The "right approach" to intrinsic value.** *Buffett:* a shareholder lays out a discounted-look-through-earnings model; Buffett: "you've stated the approach — I couldn't state it better myself," declining to bless the specific growth numbers. They'd "use the long-term government rate to discount it back" only "to establish comparability," then "look for appropriate discounts from that figure." A second factor is unique to Berkshire: because all earnings are retained and float grows, intrinsic-value growth turns "to a big extent" on how well that ~$3B/yr of new money is deployed — "and your guess is as good as ours." *Munger:* for decades they had ">100% of book net worth in marketable securities" plus wonderful subsidiaries and a place to put new money — "Well, we still got the wonderful businesses. But we're having trouble with the new money. But it's not trouble, really, to have a pile of lovely money." See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [look-through-earnings](../concepts/look-through-earnings.md).

**★ M12 · The internet will revolutionize retailing — but brands and trust will win, and winners are hard to pick.** *Buffett:* the internet poses "such a threat" that they "simply wouldn't want to get into" many retailers; furniture retailing they think is safe, jewelry they think Borsheims and Tiffany win because "brand names are going to mean very, very much when you have thousands of choices… people have to trust somebody." GEICO will be "a big beneficiary." Note the real-estate hit: at 5% online substitution "where real estate is essentially free," dedicated retail space suffers. *Munger:* "it is tricky predicting technological change" — department stores lost three monopolistic advantages "and yet they've done well… at other times you get a change and you just get destroyed," like the trading-stamp and World Book businesses. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [borsheims](../entities/borsheims.md).

**M14 · Different compensation plans, one goal — and radical decentralization.** *Buffett:* comp arrangements "vary to an extraordinary degree"; Berkshire keeps each acquired culture, substituting performance-based plans for option plans at "equal expectable cost." GEICO's all-hands grid was Tony Nicely's work. "We do like the idea of paying for performance… but then [most companies] design systems that pay off no matter what happens." No HR, legal, IR, or PR department: "we ask them to mail the money to Omaha." *Munger:* "we are probably more decentralized… than any company of our size, or bigger, in America. We don't have a headquarters culture that's forced on the operating businesses." See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md), [tony-nicely](../people/tony-nicely.md).

**★ M16 · Big returns are easier with small sums.** *Buffett:* he could earn "50 percent a year on a million dollars" and names "a half a dozen people" who could too — but that edge "disappears very rapidly as the money compounds," falling off "dramatically" by $10M. The method is to "start with the As… find small securities in your area of competence" and "little arbitrage situations." "I'll leave to you the fun of finding them yourselves. Terrible to spoil the treasure hunt." *Munger:* what they did 40 years ago "was more simple… You have to know more now. Just sifting through the manuals until you find something selling at two times earnings, that won't work." *Buffett:* "It'll work. It's just you won't find any." See [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md).

**M17 · "Surprisingly high" ROE — and skepticism about 18–20%.** *Buffett:* his 1977 Fortune piece pegged corporate ROE at ~12–13% regardless of inflation; even after option costs and restructuring charges, recent ROE "has been surprisingly high." But "there's a real question whether, if long-term rates are 5½ percent, return on equity can be, across the board, 18 or 20 percent." Many companies "implicitly promise" 20%+; "I'm dubious about those claims." Notes the restructuring-charge game: "the other dollar a share doesn't count… we've never had a charge like that and we never will." See [return-on-equity-yardstick](../concepts/return-on-equity-yardstick.md), [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md).

**★ M18 + M33 · "Corrupt" option accounting and "deceptive" earnings games.** *Buffett:* the cost of an option is "about what could be received if they sold… those options as warrants" — a real compensation cost the income statement should carry; auditors once agreed, then "caved" under client pressure. "It's a scandal… it's too much in corporate America's interest." On deceptive accounting (M33), he won't "name the sinners" but names the sins: charges that dump future costs into one period or smooth/inflate later earnings, footnote option disclosures rigged "to minimize the income-account impact," and pooling-vs-purchase abuse — Arthur Levitt's SEC "making a concerted attempt" to stop it. *Munger:* "it's fundamentally wrong not to have rational, honest accounting… don't let little corruptions start, because they become big corruptions"; the abuse is "big bath accounting, and the subsequent release back into earnings." See [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md), [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md).

**★ M19 · Why he dissolved the partnership in 1969 — and is 1999 a rerun?** *Buffett:* in 1969 he couldn't find things *and* "the expectations of people had been so raised" that he "didn't want to face the internal pressure." He feels no such pressure running Berkshire and its wonderful businesses. "That does not mean we're sitting around with cash because we expect stocks to go down… We're looking for things right now." *Munger:* "there are some similarities between 1969-70 and the present time. But I don't think that means 1973-4 lies right ahead… I don't think we're likely to be quite that fortunate again." See [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

**★ M21 · Spotting a great industry doesn't make you money.** *Buffett:* AT&T's 15-year ROE "has been very, very poor" despite leading communications. "There's a lot of difference between making money and spotting a wonderful industry" — the auto and airplane were the two great industries of the half-century, "but very, very few people got rich by riding the back of that auto industry," and the airline industry "has been a terrible business… a marvelous industry." So "do not equate the prospects of growth for an industry with the prospects for growth in your own net worth by participating in it." See [commodity-business-economics](../concepts/commodity-business-economics.md).

**M24 · Goodwill should stay on the books, not be amortized.** *Buffett:* he'd treat all acquisitions as purchases, set up the economic goodwill "as reflective of the money you've laid out," and charge it off only when "permanently impaired" — not amortize it. Berkshire's economic goodwill "far exceeds" the original book figure. The purchase-vs-pooling gap drives "really stupid things": managers privately deplore the pooling maneuvers they perform to avoid amortization charges. *Munger:* Warren's system "would be the best," with exceptions where amortization is rational. See [economic-goodwill](../concepts/economic-goodwill.md), [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md).

**M25 · "We are not in the business of being white knights."** *Buffett:* Berkshire buys "wonderful businesses… with managements we like… at attractive prices," not rescues; when approached to top a takeover bid, "our answer, invariably, is no." *Munger:* "we're very good at saying no." See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**M26 · China: real opportunity, but outside their circle.** *Buffett:* businesses earning 20% on equity, reinvesting most of it, at "five times or less" earnings "would have to be an interesting field" — but "we would have trouble identifying those businesses ourselves," and the field may be too small for Berkshire's scale. "Look at your own area of expertise… you'll do much better." *Munger:* "I don't know much about China." (See A23.) See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ M27 · Prefer whole companies, but the stock market offers the real bargains.** *Buffett:* "we don't want to nibble" — they'd "take big gulps in the stock market" and have always preferred whole businesses, "but it's almost impossible to make a wonderful buy in a negotiated purchase. The person on the other side cares too much." In a 1973–74 market "you were dealing with the marginal seller" — he couldn't have bought the whole Washington Post for $80M, but could buy 10% from people "calculating betas." *Munger:* "over the next five years, we'll do some of both." See [washington-post](../entities/washington-post.md), [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md).

**M29 + M30 · "We blew it" on pharmaceuticals.** *Buffett:* offered a chance to buy leading drug companies "at a below-market multiple" in 1993, "we didn't do it. So we did blow it" — the industry's threats "should not cause the securities to sell at a depressed multiple." Today he'd "buy a group of the leading companies," not pick a winner, but not at current prices. *Munger:* the industry "has done more good for the customers than almost any other," and the country "has been very wise" to let drug companies "make almost obscene amounts of money." See [mistakes-of-omission](../concepts/mistakes-of-omission.md).

**M31 · Buying 100 shares to keep the information flowing.** *Buffett:* he holds "a hundred shares of a great many companies" (in his foundation) "so I keep reading about those companies" and their competitors — "It does pay to have a flow of information come in over the desk." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ M34 · Financial strength as a permanent competitive weapon.** *Buffett:* asked to update the 1994 letter's claim, he says Berkshire's reputation as "the reinsurer most certain to pay after any conceivable natural disaster… is stronger today than it's ever been." On a "very high-level cover," a buyer wouldn't "have a list of 10 people"; after a huge super-cat, "there will be a number of people whose checks will not clear. And Berkshire's check, undoubtedly, will clear." Think of Berkshire as "Fort Knox." See [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md), [super-cat-insurance](../concepts/super-cat-insurance.md).

**M35 · What drove the intrinsic-value gain.** *Buffett:* invested assets per share rose "very significantly"; GEICO "was worth far more at the end of the year," and Executive Jet (NetJets) "will be a very, very big company 10 or 15 years from now," reaching dominance "faster as part of Berkshire." "The problem is doing it year after year." See [geico](../entities/geico.md), [netjets](../entities/netjets.md).

**M36 · Successors are already in place — no grooming needed.** *Buffett:* "we have, today, the people to take over Berkshire… named in letters that the directors have," ready "to run Berkshire tomorrow morning." Exactly who depends on timing. *Munger:* "the prospects for continuity of corporate culture… is higher than at most other large public companies… the fresh cash would be allocated less well. But… that's too damn bad." See [berkshire-hathaway](../entities/berkshire-hathaway.md).

**M37 · Indexation is distorting the market.** *Buffett:* ~6–7% of equity money is indexed "and rising month by month"; adding Berkshire to the S&P 500 would create "a market order to buy 6 to 7 percent of the company" — a disruptive spike. Solutions: the company sells matching stock (they won't), or phase the weighting in over 12 months. As indexation grows toward 15%, "it will have more and more impact on the market" — shorting newly added stocks after the S&P-effect "might" pay as the "artificial buy order" wears off. See [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md).

**M38 · What it takes to buy abroad.** *Buffett:* market size first — "we think in terms of 500 million as being a minimum"; accounting differences are fine "as long as we understand" them; inferior corporate governance would require an adjustment. They "wouldn't rule out Japan, Germany, France, England." (53% of world market value is in the U.S., with 4½% of population.) See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ M39 · Bullish on Coke — but "we do not like the P/E ratios."** *Buffett:* he won't predict the dollar or short-term case growth; what matters is "share of market and share of mind," and Coke's "share of mind around the world" is unmatched. "I'll be giving that same answer 10, or 15, or 20 years from now." But Coke at 35x — "like virtually every other leading company" — strikes him as "quite full," so "our enthusiasm for buying more… is less than it was." *Munger:* on a 10–15-year view "you don't pay much attention to short-term economic developments… we have tuned out all this noise." *Hindsight flag:* Buffett's own caution proved the operative truth — Coke's stock stagnated for roughly a decade as that "full" multiple compressed, even as the business held; the "same answer forever" optimism was right about the franchise but the entry price mattered. See [coca-cola](../entities/coca-cola.md), [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md).

**★ M42 + M43 · Macro doesn't matter if you're right about the business.** *Buffett:* Japan's slump "is no factor in our thinking at all." Had someone handed him a 1972–82 roadmap (prime to 21½%, Dow to ~570), "that wasn't the important thing. The important thing was that this peanut brittle tastes like it does" — See's earned $4M pretax in 1972, $62M in 1998. "If we're right about the business, the macro factors aren't going to make any difference. And if we're wrong, macro factors are not going to bail us out." *Munger:* Japan's long contraction "defied… classical Keynesian theory"; the cause was "the extremeness of its booms" plus "corruption in its accounting practices," an "interesting lesson… not to let a lot of slop get into the accounting and regulatory systems." America's prosperity may owe more to "this so-called wealth effect" than people think. See [sees-candies](../entities/sees-candies.md), [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md).

**M44 · Analyst coverage, but no change in attitude.** *Buffett:* he wants the price "in a zone… not too wide around intrinsic value," in neither direction. The Gen Re deal brought institutional holders and one or two analysts — "a non-paid information office," which "goes along the grain of my nature." "What we want are informed owners who are in sync with our objectives… we don't want people who are concerned about quarterly earnings… or stock splits." See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**M45 · Flight-operations economics: FlightSafety vs. NetJets.** *Buffett:* FlightSafety is the "more capital intensive" — "every simulator costs real money" — with steady, unspectacular ROE tied to simulator cost. Executive Jet/NetJets is "in an earlier part of its development"; customers end up owning the planes, so "by its nature, it's not a capital-intensive business," and it "could be… a business with a very good return on capital" down the line. See [flightsafety](../entities/flightsafety.md), [netjets](../entities/netjets.md).

## Q&A digest — Afternoon session

**A1 · Gen Re's benefits are a 10-year, not 1–2-year, story.** *Buffett:* bought General Re "mostly because of the float" and the chance to invest it better; the float "actually declined very slightly in the first quarter" and won't grow without substantially higher premium. Berkshire "10 years from now will be worth more on a per-share basis with General Re included," but "not necessarily… on a one-year or two-year basis." *Munger:* "if we do one-third as well with the new float… as we've done on average in the past, it will work wonderfully." See [general-re](../entities/general-re.md), [float](../concepts/float.md).

**★ A2 · Change is a threat, not an opportunity.** *Buffett:* "We view change as more of a threat into the investment process than an opportunity… we're looking for the absence of change to protect ways that are already making a lot of money." Coca-Cola's product and distribution "really haven't changed at all"; "your analysis of Coca-Cola 50 years ago can pretty well serve as an analysis now." This means missing "a lot of very big winners — but we would not have picked those out anyway," in exchange for "very few big losers." *Munger:* "Berkshire's businesses, on average, are less likely to be obsoleted by new technology… New steel-toed work shoes? I do not anticipate a significant change in the technology." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ A3 · Stocks can't keep growing at the same rate — the arithmetic forbids it.** *Buffett:* "the only money investors are going to make, in the long run, are what the businesses make… nobody's adding to the pot." With GDP at 4–5% and profits already high as a share of it, profits can't grow faster ("in the end, they'd be greater than GDP"); so 15% equity returns are "nonsense" — the Fortune 500 earned $334B on ~$10.5T. "Any time you bump into absurdities… you better change expectations somewhat." *Munger:* "If a thing can't go on forever, it will eventually stop," and "People who expect perpetual growth in real wealth in a finite earth are either mad men or economists." See [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md).

**A4 · The "best contribution" is low-cost goods and services.** *Buffett:* Berkshire paid ~$2.6B in federal tax (perhaps more than GE or Microsoft); but the real societal contribution is efficiency — GEICO's ~15% saving on ~$4B of premium is ">$600M that consumers save." Berkshire won't give away owners' money to "the pet charities of the CEO," only shareholder-designated gifts. *Munger:* "you can't take it with you… it all has to go back one way or another." See [geico](../entities/geico.md), [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**A6 · Cable TV: more promise than capital returns — and TCA Cable is Lou Simpson's.** *Buffett:* cable has been "a good business" but over its 30-year life "has not made extraordinary returns on invested capital, at all"; current prices imply the market expects that to change. [Washington Post](../entities/washington-post.md) is a net *buyer* of cable, not a seller, so rising cable prices are bad news — "it's way better off if cable prices go down than up." TCA Cable is an entirely separate matter: it sits in [Lou Simpson](../people/lou-simpson.md)'s GEICO equity portfolio; Buffett "never read an annual report of TCA Cable. I know nothing about it" — a reminder to be careful about press reports attributing any subsidiary's holdings to Buffett personally. *Munger:* "we have demonstrated a signal lack of aptitude in correctly diagnosing the future of cable in a way that made us a lot of money."

**★ A8 · Avoiding tech: trading a big payoff for a certain payoff.** *Buffett:* "it's much easier to predict the relative strength that Coke will enjoy… than the strength Microsoft will possess in the software world. That's not to knock Microsoft… If I had to bet, I'd bet heavily on Microsoft. But I don't have to bet." Gates himself, asked which two stocks for a desert island, named "two very good stocks… but he'd rather buy Coca-Cola, because he'd feel sure." Of ~400 U.S. companies earning $200M+ after tax, "you could name 350"; five years out maybe 20 "come from nowhere," yet "dozens and dozens" of high-tech names are already priced as if they'll get there. "It's not that easy to make lots of money in a capitalistic society." *Munger:* even great tech fields may hit "a surfeit of anybody's interest." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [coca-cola](../entities/coca-cola.md).

**A9 · Don't dance in and out of the companies you love.** *Buffett:* Coke and Gillette fell 30–50% from highs "but neither got to the price that left us happy putting new money in" — "some evidence of where the market has been." Trying to trade around them means "you have to make two decisions right… and usually you have to pay some tax in-between." Disappointments happen, but "the long-term trend line" of the blade-and-razor "inevitable" is intact. *Munger:* "trying to dance in and out of the companies you really love, on a long-term basis, has not been a good idea for most investors." See [coca-cola](../entities/coca-cola.md), [gillette](../entities/gillette.md).

**A10 · Deflation is unlikely — but would reward long-bond holders.** *Buffett:* "very, very unlikely," though "my record is not great" on inflation forecasts. Under deflation "the buying power of money appreciates," so you need lower nominal returns. *Munger:* Japan's deflation "has been quite unpleasant… on the other hand, it hasn't been a catastrophe." *Buffett:* in Japan, owners of long bonds "would have had a tremendous bonanza." See [bonds-and-inflation](../concepts/bonds-and-inflation.md).

**★ A11 · Markets are "fairly" efficient — but efficient-market theory is "silly."** *Buffett:* "the market is generally fairly efficient," but that "does not suffice to support an efficient market theory approach." Believing it 20–30 years ago "would have been like learning the earth is flat." It became academic dogma because "you can't build anything around" roughly-efficient markets — "what people want are elegant theories." "What investment is about is valuing businesses… you try to figure out what a business is worth. And if it's selling below that figure you buy it." You can't find a course on valuing businesses, only on computing beta, "because that's something the instructor knows how to do." *Munger:* Max Planck — "the old guard really didn't accept the new ideas… because the old guard fades away, clinging to the asininities of the past." See [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md), [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md).

**A12 · Grow cheap float fast — but the old rate can't continue.** *Buffett:* float "grew at a much faster rate, since 1967, than I thought it would"; he can't forecast it now, "except to tell you it's very much a goal of Berkshire to grow that float as fast as it can, while maintaining a very low cost." The growth in intrinsic value over the next decade "will be determined, in a very significant way, by the rate at which we do grow it" and at what cost. *Munger:* "If we grow very low-cost float at the same rate… for another 30 years… if you look to the heavens there will be a star in the east." See [float](../concepts/float.md), [cost-of-float](../concepts/cost-of-float.md).

**A13 · Two reasons Berkshire avoids life insurance.** *Buffett:* the business "isn't very profitable," and much of it is "equity-related" — "we never wanted to get in the business of managing equities for other people… we want our sole interest on equities to be Berkshire Hathaway itself." They'd take a profitable life line (their annuity product is "very competitive… you are certain to get paid from Berkshire"), but term-life pricing makes no sense "even with the absence of commissions." See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**A14 + A21 · How the A and B shares relate.** *Buffett:* a B "can never be worth more than 1/30th of the A" (an A converts to 30 B's, capping the premium via arbitrage), but B "can sell at a discount" since it doesn't convert upward. His website guidance: above a ~2% discount he'd "rather buy B"; at parity, buying ≥30 shares, "I'd buy the A" for the optionality. Shorting "doesn't hurt us in any way… What counts is the intrinsic value of Berkshire." *Munger:* to a holder who bought the lower-priced B at parity, "When you made your original decision to buy the lower-priced of the two stocks, you made a mistake." See [berkshire-hathaway](../entities/berkshire-hathaway.md).

**★ A15 · Start early and think for yourself.** *Buffett:* "we started building this little snowball on top of a very long hill… the trick is to have a very long hill, which means starting very young or living very old." He'd "start with the As" and focus on small, overlooked companies. "You can't expect anybody else to do it for you… people will not tell you about wonderful little investments" — when he found GEICO in 1951, the insurance specialists "said I didn't know what I was talking about." "You have to think for yourself. And if you do, you'll find things." *Munger:* "the hard part… is the first $100,000," helped by being "passionate about being rational, very eager and opportunistic, and steadily underspend[ing] your income grossly." See [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**A16 · Learn insurance — and any business — by reading everything.** *Buffett:* he knows "no good book" on insurance; his head start was Lorimer Davidson's four hours in 1951, after which "I just started reading a lot of other reports." To learn the field today he'd "read the reports of every property-casualty company around," plus 20 years of Berkshire's insurance sections. See [lorimer-davidson](../people/lorimer-davidson.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ A17 · The Unicover loss — a "rare lapse" at General Re.** *Buffett:* Cologne set up a $275M reserve on the Unicover workers'-comp affair; "it may be the tip of the iceberg in terms of the loss to the industry… but I don't think it's the tip of the General Re or Berkshire iceberg." Insurance surprises "can come big and they can come late" — he cites the Omni affair at National Indemnity 20 years earlier, a CNA fiberboard loss (~$1.5B on thousands in premium), and a GEICO book that lost $60M on <$200K premium. "It is the nature of insurance that you get unpleasant surprises." *Munger:* "it was a dumb, amateurish error… a rare lapse," not a sign that General Re — "probably the best in the world" — is "suddenly full of amateurs"; "it does make you more careful." *Hindsight flag:* the "rare lapse / best in the world" framing badly understated what was coming — General Re's underwriting losses, the finite-reinsurance scandal, and the derivative-book unwind (see A34) would dog Berkshire for years; Unicover was an early symptom, not an isolated one. See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md), [general-re](../entities/general-re.md).

**A18 · Berkshire buyback unlikely.** *Buffett:* even when Berkshire looked cheap, "there were other securities that were even less efficiently priced" — in 1974 he bought the Washington Post (worth ~$400M, priced at $80M) rather than his own stock, "and like I said, many times I was wrong." A repurchase needs the stock "fairly dramatically underpriced" with "a big margin for error" — "we would not want to buy a dollar bill for 95 cents." *Munger:* "nothing to add." See [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md).

**A19 · Trust as the internet moat — and GEICO as the proof.** *Buffett:* "the internet reinforces the necessity for trust in dealing with people" you "are never going to see"; the Berkshire umbrella enhances brands like NetJets, Borsheims, and Berkshire Hathaway Life. GEICO already does ~$4.8B of business with 3.7M (soon 4.5M) policyholders "who have never met anyone from GEICO" — "based on the fact they trust this company." "That's a huge asset… It will be many times bigger 10 years from now." See [geico](../entities/geico.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**★ A20 · Moats, management, and the certainty of future cash.** *Buffett:* the discount rate is the Treasury rate, used "just to get comparability… a dollar earned from a horseshoe company is the same as a dollar earned from an internet company." The moat and management "enter into our thinking as to the degree of certainty… we attribute to the stream of cash… and the amount of it." Valuation "is an art"; with Wrigley you'd estimate unit growth, pricing flexibility, the danger of share loss, and "the likelihood of management being very bright with the cash… or very stupid." Coca-Cola has "a terrific moat" — even in the can (people identify it blindfolded) — and "share of mind" among billions. "If you have a big enough moat, you don't need as much management" — Peter Lynch's idiot-proof business. "There's no formula that says the moat is 28 feet wide and 16 feet deep." *Munger:* the academic approach "is a lot like Long-Term Capital Management. How can people so smart do such silly things?" See [moat](../concepts/moat.md), [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md).

**★ A22 · "The average insurance company is going to remain very average."** *Buffett:* consolidation "usually solves nothing — two lousy businesses put together is a big, lousy business." The winners have "some franchise based on specialized talents… distribution… even the ability to use the float effectively." GEICO has "the low-cost method of distributing personal auto insurance on an all-comers basis" (vs. USAA's specialized niche); it grew policyholders 20.8% last year, 22.5% in the year to March. The industry has "more capital… than there is opportunity to use it intelligently," which "actually hurts a General Re" as primaries retain more premium. "I look forward to" a tougher market — "it may offer us the opportunity to grow even faster." *Munger:* "nothing to add." See [geico](../entities/geico.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**A23 · China's unit-growth runway for Coke and Gillette.** *Buffett:* China is "the fastest growth market among countries of any size" for Coke — huge population, low per-capita consumption, building infrastructure. Gillette is different: people already shave, so growth comes as "disposable income grows and they trade up." "I do not sit and work out country by country… It would be a waste of time." *Munger:* "if I knew for sure the U.S. share of world market cap was going from 53% to 40%, I wouldn't know how to make money out of that insight." "Our game is to find a few intelligent things to do." See [coca-cola](../entities/coca-cola.md), [gillette](../entities/gillette.md).

**A24 + A26 · Reading recommendations.** *Munger:* Robert Hagstrom's *The Warren Buffett Portfolio* ("a considerable contribution to the synthesis of human thought on the investment process"); Ron Chernow's *Titan* (the John D. Rockefeller biography, "one of the best business biographies I have ever read"); David Landes's *The Wealth and Poverty of Nations*. *Buffett:* Katharine Graham's *Personal History* ("incredibly honest… a great read") and Jack Bogle's *Common Sense on Mutual Funds* ("if mutual fund investors listen to him, they would save billions"). See [charlie-munger](../people/charlie-munger.md), [kay-graham](../people/kay-graham.md).

**A25 · Tobacco is far more threatened than pharma was.** *Buffett:* "no comparison" — the 1993 pharma fears were about pricing and distribution; tobacco's legislative/liability threat is "of a far different order." *Munger:* "the legislative threat to tobacco is serious, and I haven't the faintest idea how to predict it." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ A27 + A28 · The internet is too hard to pick — and we'll never outsource your money.** *Buffett:* asked about joining a Kleiner Perkins-style venture fund, he agrees they understand "the process of early investment/promotion probably as well as anyone" but would not invest — early promoters "make a lot of money selling to the next stage… but in the end, they have to succeed as businesses." Ask the top names to name five internet companies that will earn $200M in 6–7 years, "I'm not so sure they would name a single one." Then the principle: "We will never turn our money over to somebody else… if we're going to lose your money, we're going to lose it ourselves and come back and look you in the eye and tell you how we lost it." Outsourcing usually lands you "in the hands of the promoters." *Munger:* "at least it's not an easy investment decision for us. And that's what we're looking for." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ A29 · Selling McDonald's was a mistake — and postmortems are how you improve.** *Buffett:* "I said it was a mistake to sell it, and it was… in the interest of candor." McDonald's didn't belong in his "eight or 10" must-own businesses, but the sale "has cost you in the area of a billion dollars-plus." The practice: contrast every sale against every purchase; "you're a better doctor if you drop by the pathology department, occasionally." Managers resist sticking real outcomes "up there against the projections… that's human nature." *Munger:* "we do rub our own noses in it. We don't even need the help of the Kenners." See [mistakes-of-omission](../concepts/mistakes-of-omission.md).

**A30 · A "little" look at health insurance.** *Munger:* "We've looked at everything in turmoil that's important… but so far it hasn't seemed to yield our particular mental approach." *Buffett:* "I don't know who I would want to get in with in that business." *Munger:* "a significant percentage of schlock operators in the field… makes it harder." See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**★ A31 · Security analysis is a journalistic process.** *Buffett:* immerse in 5–10 companies "as if you were going to work for the company, or they'd hired you as the CEO, or you're going to buy the whole business," then ask "What do I not know that I need to know?" Interrogate competitors with Andy Grove's "silver bullet" question — "If you could only put it through the head of one of your competitors, which one and why?" — and chase the answer down the chain. "It's an investigative process. It's a journalistic process. And in the end, you want to write the story." *Munger:* "the question you've got to keep asking is: Why? Why? Why?… relate the answers to a structure of deep theory"; for company histories "Value Line is in a class by itself." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ A32 · Berkshire adds value mostly by leaving managers alone.** *Buffett:* sometimes there's a specific edge (Gen Re's float/tax; the Berkshire brand putting Executive Jet "on the map"), but the general contribution is giving managers "a very rational owner" so they "spend a hundred percent of their time focused on what counts." "The CEOs of most public companies waste a third of their time… trying to please various constituencies… that take the company backwards." GEICO "has probably grown a fair amount faster as a subsidiary," adding "billions and billions of dollars" of value — "not because we've taught the management one thing." *Munger:* "most of what we do is just not interfere in a counterproductive way. And that non-interference has enormous value." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**A33 + A35 · Intrinsic value of the securities, and the candor promise.** *Buffett:* they "tend to use the market prices" for marketable securities, except rare moments (e.g., 1974) when "we really think these things are going to be worth a hell of a lot more than they're selling for." On reporting: "We may disappoint you in how the business performs… But we shouldn't ever disappoint you in either our accounting or in the candor of the reporting… if we fail there it's because we set out to fail. We don't sugarcoat things, and we don't put spin on things." See [intrinsic-value-vs-book-value](../concepts/intrinsic-value-vs-book-value.md), [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**★ A34 · Watch derivatives accounting "very carefully" — General Re's $400M book.** *Buffett:* General Re's derivative business has "big balance sheet numbers and big off-balance sheet numbers in relation to the amount of money made"; with credit guarantees and long-dated contracts, "it's a business that people can get in trouble in… while the accounting sails along merrily." At Salomon he and Munger found "mismarked derivative positions… in the 20-odd millions," because "the contracts got so complicated that the people valuing them didn't understand them" — and compensation depended on those marks. "When the numbers are big in relation to the amount of profits, you want to look very carefully." *Munger:* the broader Salomon books "just got so out of control… multimillion dollar errors" — explicitly NOT in the derivative department. *Hindsight flag:* this caution was prophetic and underweighted at once — General Re Securities became a multi-year, multi-hundred-million-dollar wind-down that prompted Buffett's later "financial weapons of mass destruction" verdict; the $400M "net asset" book they pledged merely to watch should, in hindsight, have been exited sooner. See [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md), [general-re](../entities/general-re.md).

## Connections to other sources

- The tech/change skepticism here is the dot-com-peak hardening of a stance present since [berkshire-1995-meeting](berkshire-1995-meeting.md) (declining Microsoft even with Gates as a friend) and [berkshire-1998-meeting](berkshire-1998-meeting.md); 1999 adds the explicit "change is a threat" framing and the "trade a big payoff for a certain payoff" formulation.
- The expectations-arithmetic of A3 is the spoken twin of the famous market-return warning in the [berkshire-1999-letter](berkshire-1999-letter.md) (and Buffett's 1999 *Fortune* article); the meeting states it as raw Fortune-500 math from the floor.
- The General Re strands (M7/A1 float, A17 Unicover, A34 derivatives) build directly on the optimistic Gen Re framing in [berkshire-1998-meeting](berkshire-1998-meeting.md) — and read, in hindsight, as the first audible cracks; later meetings will return to the underwriting losses and the derivative-book unwind.
- The LTCM/leverage parable extends the derivatives warnings first sounded at [berkshire-1994-meeting](berkshire-1994-meeting.md) (M11, "ignorance and borrowed money") and [berkshire-1995-meeting](berkshire-1995-meeting.md) (M13, "creation of risk, not the transfer of risk"); 1999 supplies the live case study.
- The moat-as-certainty discussion (A20) is the conversational successor to the moat-and-castle metaphor that got its fullest spoken form at [berkshire-1995-meeting](berkshire-1995-meeting.md) (M25), now fused explicitly to the discount-rate and valuation machinery.
- "Don't dance in and out of the companies you love" (A9) and the McDonald's post-mortem (A29) continue the behavioral spine of [berkshire-1995-meeting](berkshire-1995-meeting.md) ("the stock does not know that you own it"; "you don't have to make it back the way you lost it").
