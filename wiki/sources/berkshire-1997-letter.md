# Berkshire Hathaway 1997 Annual Letter

Dated February 27, 1998. Per-share book value up 34.1% to $25,488 — a year that "tempted" Buffett to declare victory but in fact "Quack": the S&P rose almost as fast. Headline events: a blow-out year at [geico](../entities/geico.md) (voluntary auto policies up 16%; new business up 48% to 913,176); an unusually lucky [super-cat](../concepts/super-cat-insurance.md) year; three "non-traditional" commitments (oil, silver, long-zeros); the Star Furniture and International Dairy Queen acquisitions; the death of Coca-Cola CEO [roberto-goizueta](../people/roberto-goizueta.md); the [salomon](../entities/salomon.md)-into-Travelers merger; and the announcement that "today's price levels … have materially eroded the 'margin of safety.'" The canonical essays of the year: the **Ted Williams strike-zone discipline**, the **"How We Think About Market Fluctuations" hamburger analogy**, the **catastrophe-bond dice essay**, and the **"Confession" on stock-as-acquisition-currency**.

## Key themes

- **Quack: the duck-after-a-rainstorm framing.** A 34.1% gain is no triumph when the rising tide lifts every boat. The Index bears no tax cost; Berkshire paid or accrued $4.2B of federal income tax (~18% of beginning net worth). The yardstick is "a modest advantage over the Index" over time, not annual victory laps.
- **Two-column intrinsic-value table updated through 1997.** Per-share investments $38,043 (up from $3,910 in 1987); pre-tax operating earnings ex-investments $717.82 per share (up from $108.14). Thirty-year (1967–97) compound: investments 25.6%, operating earnings 24.2%. Decade-by-decade growth slowing in earnings (27.6% → 24.1% → 20.8%) — "nothing recedes like success."
- **Ted Williams strike-zone discipline.** Carved zone into 77 cells; fat pitches mean .400, low-outside corner means .230. Today's "pitches" are catching the lower-outside corner. Berkshire would rather stand with the bat on its shoulder than swing at marginal prices. The discipline extended: Berkshire is not forced to swing — but standing still "day after day" is "not my idea of fun." See [concentration-vs-diversification](../concepts/concentration-vs-diversification.md).
- **Unconventional Commitments.** When the favorite commitment is unavailable, Berkshire occasionally ventures elsewhere — *thinking* rather than *knowing* it will make money. Three yearend positions: (1) 14.0M barrels of remaining oil derivative contracts (out of an original 45.7M position from 1994–95; closed contracts produced $61.9M pre-tax gain), (2) 111.2M ounces of silver bought in 1997 ($97.4M mark-to-market gain), (3) $4.6B amortized cost of long-term zero-coupon U.S. Treasuries ($598.8M unrealized pre-tax gain). Buffett: "Inflation expectations, it should be noted, play no part in our calculation of silver's value."
- **"How We Think About Market Fluctuations" — the hamburger analogy.** Net savers should *want* lower stock prices, just as hamburger-eaters want lower beef. "*Dis*investors lose as market falls — but investors gain." Roughly 97% of Berkshire shares are held year-to-year by the same investors; they are savers; they should rejoice when markets fall. See [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).
- **Float framework continued: 21.7% annual compound since 1967.** Average float now $7.09B; fifth consecutive year of underwriting profit (negative cost of float). "Therein lies an accounting irony: Though our float is shown on our balance sheet as a liability, it has had a value to Berkshire greater than an equal amount of net worth would have had." See [float](../concepts/float.md).
- **Super-cat: the catastrophe-bond dice essay.** Catastrophe bonds are an Orwellian misnomer: a true bond obliges the issuer to pay; a "cat bond" is a contract that lays a provisional promise to pay on the *purchaser*. The dice example: insure against a 12 (probability 1/36) for a $1M premium against $50M loss — severely underpriced — but 75.4% probability you go a decade without paying out, looking like a winner the whole time. The expert-with-no-skin-in-the-game problem: "Surprise: When the stakes are high, an expert can invariably be found who will affirm … that the chance of rolling a 12 is not 1 in 36, but more like 1 in 100." The influx of catastrophe-bond money has deteriorated super-cat prices; Berkshire will write less in 1998. Worst-case loss ~$600M after-tax (CEA earthquake policy). See [super-cat-insurance](../concepts/super-cat-insurance.md).
- **GEICO: blow-out year, but tempered.** Voluntary auto in-force up 16% (vs. 10% in 1996); new policies up 48% (617,669 → 913,176). 8.1% underwriting margin, "higher than we wish it to be" — target is ~4%, passing the rest to customers. Profit-sharing chart's bounds expanded again; 26.9% of base comp paid to 10,500 associates ($71M). Marketing budget up 50% to >$100M for 1998; market share only 3%. The Davy Davidson tribute at age 95.
- **"A Confession" on stock-as-acquisition-currency.** "If you aggregate all of our stock-only mergers … you will find that our shareholders are slightly worse off than they would have been had I not done the transactions. Though it hurts me to say it, when I've issued stock, I've cost you money." Not the sellers' fault — they performed. The problem: Berkshire's existing roster is full of ".380 hitters," and trading any of them away for a ".350 hitter" almost never makes sense. Public-company acquisitions require either overvalued acquirer stock or genuine synergies; Berkshire stock is "seldom … overvalued," and "synergy gains … is usually unrealistic." See [stock-as-acquisition-currency](../concepts/stock-as-acquisition-currency.md).
- **Look-through earnings $1.93B for 1997.** Operating earnings $1.292B plus $743M share of major investees' undistributed earnings, less $105M hypothetical 14% tax. See [look-through-earnings](../concepts/look-through-earnings.md).
- **Margin of safety eroded.** "Today's price levels, though, have materially eroded the 'margin of safety' that [ben-graham](../people/ben-graham.md) identified as the cornerstone of intelligent investing." Conditional valuation framework reaffirmed: not overvalued *if* interest rates hold or fall and *if* ROEs stay near recent levels — but ROEs are not a sure thing.
- **Coca-Cola succession: Roberto Goizueta's death.** Goizueta died October 1997. Buffett re-read 100+ letters Goizueta had written him over nine years; called them "a guidebook for success in both business and life." Quoted: "By the way, I have told Olguita that what she refers to as an obsession, you call focus. I like your term much better." Succession to Doug Ivester pre-arranged and seamless.
- **The convertible-preferred coda.** Salomon merged into Travelers — "long-suffering shareholders" rewarded; Deryck Maughan and Bob Denham credited with saving Salomon after 1991. USAir "resuscitation borders on the miraculous"; preferred called for redemption March 1998; "the gain could even prove indecent." The Amex PERC golf-course story with Frank Olson at Prouts Neck (1994) retold as "110% luck, the balance skill."
- **Star Furniture and International Dairy Queen acquired.** Star traces back through the Blumkin–Child referral chain ("Are there any more at home like you?"). Melvyn Wolff deal done in two hours in New York. IDQ patterned on FlightSafety: cash-or-stock option with a slight cash incentive; 45% took cash. "Charlie and I bring a modicum of product expertise to this transaction."

## Notable quotes

> Any investor can chalk up large returns when stocks soar, as they did in 1997. In a bull market, one must avoid the error of the preening duck that quacks boastfully after a torrential rainstorm, thinking that its paddling skills have caused it to rise in the world.

> Our appraisal of 1997's performance, then: Quack.

> We will not ask you to adopt the philosophy of the Chicago Cubs fan who reacted to a string of lackluster seasons by saying, "Why get upset? Everyone has a bad century now and then."

> For anyone deploying capital, nothing recedes like success.

> We weren't smarter then, just smaller.

> In his book *The Science of Hitting*, Ted explains that he carved the strike zone into 77 cells, each the size of a baseball. Swinging only at balls in his "best" cell, he knew, would allow him to bat .400; reaching for balls in his "worst" spot, the low outside corner of the strike zone, would reduce him to .230.

> If they are in the strike zone at all, the business "pitches" we now see are just catching the lower outside corner. If we swing, we will be locked into low returns.

> Unlike Ted, we can't be called out if we resist three pitches that are barely in the strike zone; nevertheless, just standing there, day after day, with my bat on my shoulder is not my idea of fun.

> Finding that kind of opportunity, we *know* that we are going to make money — the only question being when. With alternative investments, we *think* that we are going to make money. But we also recognize that we will sometimes realize losses, occasionally of substantial size.

> In purchasing zeros, rather than staying with cash-equivalents, we risk looking very foolish: A macro-based commitment such as this never has anything close to a 100% probability of being successful. However, you pay Charlie and me to use our best judgment — not to avoid embarrassment — and we will occasionally make an unconventional move when we believe the odds favor it.

> Along with President Clinton, we will be feeling your pain: The Munger family has more than 90% of its net worth in Berkshire and the Buffetts more than 99%.

> If you plan to eat hamburgers throughout your life and are not a cattle producer, should you wish for higher or lower prices for beef? Likewise, if you are going to buy a car from time to time but are not an auto manufacturer, should you prefer higher or lower car prices? These questions, of course, answer themselves.

> Only those who will be sellers of equities in the near future should be happy at seeing stocks rise. Prospective purchasers should much prefer sinking prices.

> So smile when you read a headline that says "Investors lose as market falls." Edit it in your mind to "*Dis*investors lose as market falls — but investors gain."

> As they say in golf matches: "Every putt makes *someone* happy."

> Markets that then were hostile to investment transients were friendly to those taking up permanent residence.

> Therein lies an accounting irony: Though our float is shown on our balance sheet as a liability, it has had a value to Berkshire greater than an equal amount of net worth would have had.

> What you must understand, however, is that a truly terrible year in the super-cat business is not a possibility — it's a certainty. The only question is when it will come.

> A true bond obliges the issuer to pay; these bonds, in effect, are contracts that lay a provisional promise to pay on the *purchaser*.

> Surprise: When the stakes are high, an expert can invariably be found who will affirm — to return to our example — that the chance of rolling a 12 is not 1 in 36, but more like 1 in 100. (In fairness, we should add that the expert will probably believe that his odds are correct, a fact that makes him less reprehensible — but more dangerous.)

> At GEICO, we are paying in a way that makes sense for both our owners and our managers. We distribute merit badges, not lottery tickets: In none of Berkshire's subsidiaries do we relate compensation to our stock price, which our associates cannot affect in any meaningful way.

> If you aggregate all of our stock-only mergers (excluding those we did with two affiliated companies, Diversified Retailing and Blue Chip Stamps), you will find that our shareholders are slightly worse off than they would have been had I not done the transactions. Though it hurts me to say it, when I've issued stock, I've cost you money.

> An example from sports will illustrate the difficulty we face: For a baseball team, acquiring a player who can be expected to bat .350 is almost always a wonderful event — *except* when the team must trade a .380 hitter to make the deal.

> We believe that it is almost impossible for us to "trade up" from our present businesses and managements.

> Only in fairy tales are emperors told that they are naked.

> Today's price levels, though, have materially eroded the "margin of safety" that Ben Graham identified as the cornerstone of intelligent investing.

> By the way, I have told Olguita that what she refers to as an obsession, you call focus. I like your term much better. [Roberto Goizueta in a handwritten note.]

> How can I miss you if you won't go away? [Buffett quoting a country song on USAir and Salomon two years earlier.]

> I would have enjoyed the play except that I had an unfortunate seat. It faced the stage. [Buffett quoting a drama critic on his 1991–92 Salomon experience.]

> Those who have watched my moves in this investment know that I have compiled a record that is unblemished by success. [On USAir.]

> Next time I make a big, dumb decision, Berkshire shareholders will know what to do: *Phone Mr. Wolf*. [On Stephen Wolf at US Airways.]

## Acquisitions of 1997

**Star Furniture.** Closed July 1, 1997. Twelve stores (ten Houston, one Austin, one Bryan; San Antonio coming). Wolff family ownership since 1924; Melvyn Wolff and sister Shirley Toomin took over in 1962. Originated through the 1983 Blumkin referral chain ("Are there any more at home like you?") and confirmed by Bill Child of R.C. Willey. Bob Denham of Salomon brokered the introduction at the 1997 annual-meeting week; deal closed in a single two-hour New York session. The Wolff–Toomin gesture: large special payments to all associates, funded by sellers' own proceeds.

**International Dairy Queen.** Announced 1997, closed early 1998. 5,792 Dairy Queen stores in 23 countries (almost all franchised); plus 409 Orange Julius and 43 Karmelkorn operations. John Mooty and Rudy Luther took control in 1970; rationalized franchising. Luther's death in summer 1997 forced the sale. Cash-or-stock structure mirrored FlightSafety: slight cash incentive; 45% took cash.

## "A Confession" — the stock-as-acquisition-currency essay

The full essay. The framework:
1. Paying a premium for an acquisition requires *either* acquirer overvaluation *or* genuine combination synergies.
2. Berkshire stock is "seldom … overvalued."
3. Synergy gains "usually unrealistic" — Berkshire expects acquirees to operate as they did before. "Joining with Berkshire does not normally raise their revenues nor cut their costs."
4. Corollary: stock-only mergers have, in aggregate, cost Berkshire shareholders money.

The forward policy: "very reluctant to issue shares." When forced (because acquiree sellers insist on stock), Berkshire will include a cash option to draw as many sellers as possible into cash.

The expense-of-options aside: when Berkshire acquires an option-issuing company, "we promptly substitute a cash compensation plan having an economic value equivalent to that of the previous option plan." The acquiree's true compensation cost is "brought out of the closet and charged, as it should be, against earnings" — an extension of the 1992 stock-options-as-expense essay.

See [stock-as-acquisition-currency](../concepts/stock-as-acquisition-currency.md).

## Convertible-preferred coda

The two laggards reverse:

- **Salomon → Travelers.** Merger closed; common-stock position now Travelers (23.7M shares; $1.28B market). Deryck Maughan and Bob Denham credited with the 1991 rescue and the multi-year vitality restoration.
- **USAir.** Preferred called for redemption March 15, 1998. Dividend arrearages paid in 1997 with delay compensation. Common stock $4 low → $73 high. Stephen Wolf the change agent. Conversion rights — once thought worthless — now valuable; "the gain could even prove indecent."

The Amex PERC origin story retold: August 1994; convertible into common; Buffett leaning toward selling; Prouts Neck golf round with Frank Olson, Hertz CEO, who explained the corporate-card franchise; Buffett turned buyer on the back nine; by year-end 1997, 10% of the company, $3B unrealized gain. "110% luck, the balance skill."
