# Berkshire Hathaway 1997 Annual Letter

The 1997 letter advances five durable investment lessons beneath the headline of a 34.1% per-share book-value gain. First, patience as capital discipline: the Ted Williams strike-zone essay establishes the canonical framework for selective deployment when prices are high enough that every pitch is catching the lower outside corner. Second, the correct attitude toward market fluctuations: the hamburger analogy formalizes why a net buyer should want lower prices, not higher. Third, "A Confession": stock-only acquisitions have in aggregate destroyed Berkshire shareholder value, empirically validating the warning issued 15 years earlier. Fourth, catastrophe bonds as a case study in the expert-with-no-skin-in-the-game problem — the dice essay is the most compact model of how mispriced tail risk hides behind lucky streaks. Fifth, a prescient early warning: "today's price levels have materially eroded the margin of safety" — the first such caution since 1994, and a harbinger of stronger warnings to come. Float compounded to $7.1B at 21.7% annually since 1967, with five consecutive years of negative cost.

## Key themes

### Waiting for the Fat Pitch

In 1997 Berkshire's 34.1% per-share book-value gain was no triumph: the S&P rose almost as fast, and passive index funds bore no tax cost while Berkshire accrued $4.2B in federal income tax. Any duck rises when the pond rises. The honest yardstick is relative return over time — "a modest advantage over the Index" — not year-by-year victory laps.

What follows from that recognition is discipline. When prices are high, the right move is to stand with the bat on your shoulder. Buffett's canonical framework: Ted Williams carved his strike zone into 77 cells and knew that swinging only at the best cell meant batting .400, while reaching for the low outside corner meant .230. In 1997, every business pitch was catching the lower outside corner. The structural asymmetry that makes this discipline possible — and rare — is that a capital allocator faces no strikeout on called balls. The honest cost: just standing there, day after day, with the bat on the shoulder "is not my idea of fun." Patience is active restraint under pressure, not passive contentment.

### Investors vs. Dis-investors

The letter's most teachable essay reframes how a long-term buyer should respond to a falling market. If you will eat hamburgers for life and are not a cattle producer, you want lower beef prices. The same logic applies to anyone deploying capital into equities: only sellers benefit from rising prices; buyers benefit from lower ones. At yearend, about 97% of Berkshire's shares were held by the same investors who owned them at the start — making them savers, not sellers. Investees (Coca-Cola, Washington Post, Wells Fargo) that repurchased their shares at low prices in prior years compounded Berkshire's ownership faster than today's repurchases at higher prices. Markets that were hostile to investment transients were friendly to those taking up permanent residence.

The corrective mental edit: "So smile when you read a headline that says 'Investors lose as market falls.' Edit it in your mind to '*Dis*investors lose as market falls — but investors gain.'"

### "A Confession": Why Stock-Financed Acquisitions Fail

Buffett audits his own record on stock-only deals and delivers the verdict: aggregating all Berkshire stock-only mergers, shareholders "are slightly worse off than they would have been had I not done the transactions. Though it hurts me to say it, when I've issued stock, I've cost you money."

The cause is arithmetic, not misfeasance. Issuing Berkshire stock to acquire a new business means reducing ownership in the existing roster. Berkshire's portfolio is full of ".380 hitters" — an acquirer with .380 hitters cannot trade to a .350 hitter without destroying value. Any stock-financed acquisition premium requires either that the acquirer's shares are overvalued (which Berkshire's seldom are) or that genuine synergies exist (which Buffett considers "usually unrealistic" — Berkshire expects acquirees to operate exactly as they did before). The forward policy: pay cash whenever possible; if sellers insist on stock, include an attractive cash option to draw as many as possible away from it. This empirical audit closes the loop on the principle first stated in 1982.

### Float: The Liability Worth More Than Equity

Five consecutive years of underwriting profit meant Berkshire had been paid to hold float — insurance premiums collected before claims are paid. By yearend 1997, average float stood at $7.09B, compounding at 21.7% annually since 1967. The accounting paradox stated most cleanly: "Though our float is shown on our balance sheet as a liability, it has had a value to Berkshire greater than an equal amount of net worth would have had." A zero-cost, compounding liability is not a liability in any economic sense — it is equity with a misleading label.

### Catastrophe Bonds and the Expert With No Skin in the Game

The letter offers a compact model of how mispriced tail risk sustains itself. The instrument deserves its Orwellian label: "A true bond obliges the issuer to pay; these bonds, in effect, are contracts that lay a provisional promise to pay on the *purchaser*." The dice essay quantifies the structural deception: a 1-in-36 event priced at $1M against a $50M loss is severely underpriced — yet there is a 75.4% probability of going a full decade without paying out, manufacturing a decade-long illusion of profitability. The error mechanism: promoters retain an expert paid up-front regardless of accuracy, creating structured incentives for motivated optimism. "Surprise: When the stakes are high, an expert can invariably be found who will affirm — to return to our example — that the chance of rolling a 12 is not 1 in 36, but more like 1 in 100." The lesson applies wherever expert compensation is decoupled from forecast accuracy — the incentive is to validate, not to discover. Consequence for Berkshire: super-cat prices deteriorated materially, so Berkshire wrote less business in 1998.

### The Margin of Safety Has Eroded

The letter's most quietly important passage: "Today's price levels, though, have materially eroded the 'margin of safety' that Ben Graham identified as the cornerstone of intelligent investing." Framed as conditional — not necessarily overvalued *if* interest rates remain near current levels *and* returns on equity hold near recent highs — but two conditions are required, and "returns on equity are not a sure thing to remain at, or even near, their present levels." This is the first explicit market-valuation caution since 1994, and a harbinger: the warnings intensify in the 1998 and 1999 letters.

## Notable quotes

> Any investor can chalk up large returns when stocks soar, as they did in 1997. In a bull market, one must avoid the error of the preening duck that quacks boastfully after a torrential rainstorm, thinking that its paddling skills have caused it to rise in the world.

> In his book *The Science of Hitting*, Ted explains that he carved the strike zone into 77 cells, each the size of a baseball. Swinging only at balls in his "best" cell, he knew, would allow him to bat .400; reaching for balls in his "worst" spot, the low outside corner of the strike zone, would reduce him to .230.

> If you plan to eat hamburgers throughout your life and are not a cattle producer, should you wish for higher or lower prices for beef? Likewise, if you are going to buy a car from time to time but are not an auto manufacturer, should you prefer higher or lower car prices? These questions, of course, answer themselves.

> So smile when you read a headline that says "Investors lose as market falls." Edit it in your mind to "*Dis*investors lose as market falls — but investors gain."

> If you aggregate all of our stock-only mergers (excluding those we did with two affiliated companies, Diversified Retailing and Blue Chip Stamps), you will find that our shareholders are slightly worse off than they would have been had I not done the transactions. Though it hurts me to say it, when I've issued stock, I've cost you money.

> A true bond obliges the issuer to pay; these bonds, in effect, are contracts that lay a provisional promise to pay on the *purchaser*.

> Surprise: When the stakes are high, an expert can invariably be found who will affirm — to return to our example — that the chance of rolling a 12 is not 1 in 36, but more like 1 in 100. (In fairness, we should add that the expert will probably believe that his odds are correct, a fact that makes him less reprehensible — but more dangerous.)

> Therein lies an accounting irony: Though our float is shown on our balance sheet as a liability, it has had a value to Berkshire greater than an equal amount of net worth would have had.

> Today's price levels, though, have materially eroded the "margin of safety" that Ben Graham identified as the cornerstone of intelligent investing.
