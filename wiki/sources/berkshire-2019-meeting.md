# Berkshire Hathaway 2019 Annual Meeting

Held May 4, 2019, at the CenturyLink Center in Omaha, the 2019 meeting is defined by candid post-mortems and structural disclosures that are unusual even by Berkshire standards. The Kraft Heinz write-down ($15.7B impairment announced in February 2019) and delayed 10-K filing shadow the morning session, with Buffett delivering his most direct public admission of an acquisition error: "we paid too much money for Kraft." Days before the meeting, Berkshire had committed $10 billion in 8% preferred financing to Occidental Petroleum's Anadarko bid — the largest capital deployment since the 2008 crisis transactions — and Buffett uses the occasion to articulate why Berkshire's "Saturday call, Sunday commitment" capacity is a genuine institutional competitive advantage that no other entity can replicate. The newly disclosed Amazon stake (purchased by one of the portfolio managers) triggers Buffett's clearest public statement that all investing is value investing — Aesop's bird-in-bush calculation applied equally to cheap banks and high-multiple tech. Most structurally significant: Greg Abel and Ajit Jain field substantive operational questions on stage for the first time, with Jain giving a candid competitive scorecard on GEICO vs. Progressive and Abel on BHE's utility capital program — a concrete succession signal beyond the 2018 introduction of their titles. This page follows the two-layer convention: a distilled thematic top, then a comprehensive ★-marked Q&A digest as the home for detail.

## How to read the Q&A digest

- **★** marks a timeless, load-bearing exchange worth citing in the future; unmarked entries carry investor value but are secondary or period-specific.
- Answers are attributed separately to **Buffett** and **Munger** — a Munger line appears only when he added a distinct lesson (he said nothing to add to most). **Jain:** and **Abel:** lines appear where they (not Buffett/Munger) gave the substantive operational answer — 2019 is the first year both fielded questions on stage.
- **M#/A#** map to the numbered items in the raw morning/afternoon transcript for traceability.

## Key themes

### Any price can turn a wonderful business into a terrible investment

Kraft Heinz earns roughly $6 billion pretax on $7 billion of tangible assets — a wonderful business by any financial measure. Berkshire still overpaid for the Kraft side of the combination, and the write-down proves the point. "The business does not know how much you paid for it. It's going to earn based on its fundamentals." The corollary is equally important: "You can turn any investment into a bad deal by paying too much. What you can't do is turn any investment into a good deal by paying little." Buffett frames the Kraft error not as a thesis failure on branded food but as a price failure — "we paid too much" — while the Heinz piece (bought at an appropriate price with 3G in 2013) worked. The lesson is paired with the Kirkland observation: Costco's private label now does $39B vs. all of Kraft Heinz at $26B, through 775 warehouses vs. millions of distribution points. Brands vs. retailers is a genuine and intensifying contest, but it is a separate question from whether the price paid was right. See [cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses](../concepts/cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses.md), [franchise-vs-business](../concepts/franchise-vs-business.md).

### All investing is value investing — Aesop was the first analyst

The Amazon purchase by one of Berkshire's portfolio managers prompted a question about whether Berkshire was abandoning value investing. Buffett's answer is the clearest statement of the principle in the corpus: value investing is not buying cheap on book or P/E; it is putting money out today to get more later. "In the end, it all goes back to Aesop … that a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush"; the whole discipline reduces to estimating "how certain that bush is, how far away it is." A manager buying Amazon at a high multiple is doing exactly the same calculation as Buffett buying a bank at 70% of book — both are estimating the size, certainty, and timing of future cash flows. The circle-of-competence constraint is not about multiples; it is about whether you can estimate the bush at all. Munger's addendum: "But I feel like a horse's ass for not identifying Google better. … We could see in our own operations how well that Google advertising was working. And we just sat there sucking our thumbs." See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [mistakes-of-omission](../concepts/mistakes-of-omission.md).

### The emergency-capital franchise is institutional, not personal

The Occidental $10 billion preferred commitment — 8% coupon plus warrants, negotiated from a Friday phone call to a Sunday signed commitment, announced Tuesday — illustrates the unique structural position Berkshire occupies in the capital markets. No other institution can write a $10 billion check in 48 hours with no MAC clauses, no committees, no conditions beyond Berkshire's word. This capacity, Buffett argues, is no longer dependent on him personally: "Berkshire's reputation for being a good home… is institutional, not personal." Ted Weschler and Todd Combs have expanded the deal pipeline. "If there are any $10 billion, or $20 billion, or maybe even $50 billion two-day transactions in the world, they will think of Berkshire Hathaway." The $112 billion cash position is not idle capital awaiting a better allocation — it is optionality held for the moments when "it'll be raining gold" and Berkshire needs to move at institutional scale in days. See [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md), [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

### Float: Berkshire's insurance as a structure no competitor can replicate

Berkshire's $124 billion in insurance float is described here with more structural precision than in any previous meeting. The key insight: three of the world's largest reinsurers nearly went extinct in the past 30 years despite no worst-case catastrophe materializing. A standalone insurer that maintains enough capital to survive any scenario has so much tied up in that capital that the business loses its economics. Berkshire avoids this trap because its vastly diversified, largely non-correlated asset base means it effectively never needs to buy reinsurance from anyone else — and those assets can be deployed in ways impossible for a pure insurer. "We manage to earn money on money that was given to us for nothing and have earnings from underwriting and then have these large earnings from investing." Ajit Jain has personally created more than $50 billion of value at Berkshire since walking into Buffett's office on a Saturday in the mid-1980s having never worked in insurance. Tony Nicely's GEICO has created a similar sum. See [float](../concepts/float.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md), [national-indemnity](../entities/national-indemnity.md).

### Competitive intelligence stated plainly: GEICO needs to close the loss-ratio gap

The most candid competitive disclosure in the 2019 meeting comes from Ajit Jain, answering a question on GEICO vs. Progressive for the first time in public. The scorecard: GEICO has a roughly 7-point expense ratio advantage over Progressive; Progressive has a roughly 12-point loss ratio advantage over GEICO; net, Progressive is ahead by about 5 points. "GEICO is very aware of this disadvantage on the loss ratio that they are suffering, and they're very focused on trying to bridge that gap as quickly as they can." Buffett's parallel admission on BNSF: Union Pacific has a 4-point+ operating ratio advantage despite BNSF's longer average haul length and higher ton-mile volumes — "they have been operating more efficiently than we have during the last few years." Both disclosures follow the same discipline: identify the gap, measure it honestly, and state it without excuses. *Supersession flag:* the GEICO-Progressive loss-ratio gap widened significantly in 2021–2022, requiring Jain to take direct management control of GEICO and implement structural cost and underwriting reforms.

### Abel and Jain field operations for the first time: succession becomes visible

Buffett addresses the succession question directly: "You could not have two better operating managers than Greg and Ajit. I mean, they are — it is just fantastic what they accomplished. They know the businesses better. They work harder, by far." For the first time, both Jain and Abel answer substantive operational questions on stage — Jain on unconventional insurance pricing and the GEICO/Progressive competitive position; Abel on BHE's capital program and NV Energy. The format change signals that the audience should begin calibrating its assessment of the two successors through their public answers, not only through Buffett's endorsements. Buffett on the proprietary value of Berkshire's institutional capacity: "There are no three owners more interested in pouring money into sensible deals within the utility industry, or better situated in terms of the people we have to maximize any opportunities."

### The discipline of doing nothing: price, not access, is the constraint

Berkshire's $112 billion cash balance and modest deployment rate draws repeated questions. Buffett's framework is consistent: the cash is not an asset-allocation failure; it is held for moments of genuine dislocation. In the meantime, it earns more than T-bills would have in prior years (rising short rates), and it is available within 48 hours for the right transaction. When asked why Berkshire doesn't deploy more capital internationally, Munger's answer ends the discussion: "Our problem is not a lack of boots on the ground, our problem is the people on the ground are paying prices that we don't want to pay. That problem is not going to be cured by boots." Private equity's structural advantage — "somebody else's money, with the upside but not the downside" — makes the competitive market for acquisitions particularly irrational today. Berkshire is not going to match that. See [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md), [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md).

## Q&A digest — Morning session

**★ M3 · GAAP earnings are harmful when they include mark-to-market swings.** *Buffett:* opens by flagging that Q1 2019 operating earnings were "up a bit" but the headline GAAP number will show a $21.6 billion gain — the opposite distortion from Q1 2018's GAAP loss despite record operating earnings. Second consecutive year emphasizing this point. Also notes: Kraft Heinz had not yet filed its 2018 10-K (SEC accounting investigation pending), so Berkshire's Q1 2019 results include no KHC equity earnings — a subtraction vs. prior year comparisons. "I really regard these bottom line figures, particularly if they're emphasized in the press, as potentially being harmful to our shareholders." See [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md), [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md).

**★ M7/M11 · Share repurchase arithmetic: the partnership test.** *Buffett:* the only test for a buyback is whether remaining shareholders are worth more the moment after the purchase than before. "If you had three partners in a business worth $3 million and one said he'd sell for $900,000, you'd take it — the remaining two would each go from $1M to $1.05M." Bought "something over a billion" in Q1 2019 at prices he considers modestly below intrinsic value — "nothing like my ambitions." Would spend far more at 25–30% below intrinsic value. Cited FT interview where he said $100B was possible: "I probably arrived at that in about three seconds — it was a nice round figure and we could do it." *Munger:* "When it gets really obvious, we'll be very good at it." See [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md), [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**M8/M9 · BNSF: precision-scheduled railroading and energy efficiency.** *Buffett:* "not above copying anything that is successful" from CN, CP, CSX, and Union Pacific's precision-scheduled railroading experiments; BNSF waiting to see whether it can serve customers better and improve efficiency simultaneously. BNSF moves more than 15% of all U.S. ton miles; achieves 500+ ton miles per gallon of diesel — far more efficient than trucks. Post-WWII railroad workforce: 1.5M employees; now <200,000 while carrying far more freight. *Munger:* "over the long term, our questioner is on the side of the angels. Sooner or later, we'll have it more electrified. I think Greg Abel will decide when it happens." See [bnsf](../entities/bnsf.md).

**★ M10 · Bank CEO accountability and the pyromaniac test.** *Buffett:* Wells Fargo's core error was not the cross-selling incentive itself but the failure to act when evidence surfaced. At Salomon in 1991: "John Gutfreund heard about a pyromaniac, and he let him keep the box of matches" — a four-month delay between hearing about Paul Mozer's violation and the government learning of it destroyed the franchise. Berkshire's response: hotlines, anonymous letter review by audit committee, outside investigators. "I will guarantee you that we will have some people who do things that are wrong at Berkshire in the next year or five years. It's the one thing that always worries me about my job." Proposed: any bank that needs government assistance should result in the CEO losing his entire net worth, plus recovery of five years of director compensation. *Munger:* "I don't think people ought to go to jail for honest errors of judgment. It's bad enough to lose your job." See [institutional-imperative](../concepts/institutional-imperative.md), [wells-fargo](../entities/wells-fargo.md), [salomon](../entities/salomon.md).

**M15 · Stock pricing: no desire to push shares up or down.** *Buffett:* prefers ideally to have Berkshire trade "in a range that more or less is its intrinsic business value"; "we have no desire to hype it in any way" and no desire to depress it for cheap repurchases either. A-vs-B repurchases: more B will be bought because daily dollar volume is much higher; occasional A-share blocks available in estate sales or corporate transactions. The ideal: "once a year we'd have a price and people who want to get out could get out, like a private company." See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**M16 · Welcome change, but we won't always adapt to it.** *Buffett:* textiles, shoes, department stores, trading stamps — all founding Berkshire businesses that were "destroyed" by changes, but capital was redeployed to better uses. "We had 80 percent of the people working on farms in 1800. And if there hadn't been a lot of changes, you would have a whole different society." Managers of subsidiaries, not headquarters, are responsible for anticipating change in their own businesses. Berkshire's advantage: capital flows to the winners; losing businesses don't get reinvestment.

**★ M17 · Kraft Heinz: paid too much for Kraft; brands still matter, but retailers have gained.** *Buffett:* precise diagnosis: Heinz acquisition (2013, with 3G) was priced appropriately; Kraft acquisition (2015 merger) was overpaid. "You can pay too much for a wonderful business. See's Candy — there's some price at which we could've bought it and it wouldn't have worked." Current Kraft Heinz earns ≈$6B pretax on ≈$7B tangible assets — still a wonderful business. Kirkland (Costco) is now a $39B brand vs. all of Kraft Heinz at $26B, operating through 775 warehouses vs. millions of distribution points. Amazon is a brand. Retailers have structurally gained leverage over brands over the past decade. "You can turn any investment into a bad deal by paying too much. What you can't do is turn any investment into a good deal by paying little." *Munger:* "it's not a tragedy that, out of two transactions, one worked wonderfully, and the other didn't work so well. That happens." *Supersession flag:* KHC's SEC accounting investigation was eventually settled in September 2021 for $62M without admitting wrongdoing; Berkshire has maintained its ≈26% stake rather than exiting. See [franchise-vs-business](../concepts/franchise-vs-business.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md), [coca-cola](../entities/coca-cola.md).

**M18 · Nebraska Furniture Mart vs. Wayfair: jury still out.** *Buffett:* NFM already does significant online sales volume, but a large share of online customers choose in-store pickup — suggesting the model is hybrid, not pure e-commerce. Q1 2019 was weak at all four furniture operations partly because of soft single-family housing construction (ownership rate fell from 69% to 63% post-2008). "We don't want to become a showroom for the online operations." NFM's $2,500 paid-in capital has produced $9.3M in one day of meeting-week sales. *Munger:* "I think we'll do better than most furniture retailers." See [nebraska-furniture-mart](../entities/nebraska-furniture-mart.md).

**★ M19 · Alternative investments: pension funds should be skeptical.** *Buffett:* private equity has performed well because of leverage in a bull market with loose covenants, not because of superior business-selection skill. "My personal opinion is, if you take unleveraged returns against unleveraged common stocks, I do not think what is being purchased today and marketed today would work well." Supply of buying power has exploded: ≈$1T committed capital plus leverage = ≈$3T chasing businesses in a $30T+ public market. Returns are "not calculated in a manner that I would regard as honest" — IRR math excludes committed-but-uncalled capital held in T-bills while those T-bills earn a fee. LTCM cited: "extraordinarily high-IQ people, working with their own money, years of experience — and you know, it all turned to pumpkins and mice in 1998." *Munger:* "all they're doing is lying a little bit to make the money come in." See [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md), [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md).

**★ M20 · All investing is value investing — Aesop's bird in the bush.** *Buffett:* the portfolio manager who bought Amazon is "as much a value investor as I was when I was looking around for things selling below working capital." All investment is an estimate of cash flows: how certain is the bush, how far away, what's the worst case, how many birds could be there? "And it really, despite a lot of equations you learn in business school, the basic equation is that of Aesop. And your success in investing depends on how well you were able to figure out how certain that bush is, how far away it is." Amazon vs. bank at 0.7x book: same calculation, different inputs. *Munger:* "But I feel like a horse's ass for not identifying Google better. … We could see in our own operations how well that Google advertising was working. And we just sat there sucking our thumbs. So, we're ashamed. … Maybe Apple was atonement." See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [mistakes-of-omission](../concepts/mistakes-of-omission.md).

**★ M21 · Berkshire's insurance franchise is worth more inside than standalone.** *Buffett:* $124B in float is "somewhat like having a bank where people come in and deposit $124 billion and promise not to withdraw it forever." The structural advantage: Berkshire doesn't buy reinsurance from anyone else because its non-correlated asset base serves as its own reinsurer — something no standalone insurer can replicate. Three of the world's largest reinsurers (including Lloyd's) nearly went extinct in the past 30 years despite no worst-case catastrophe. "If you keep enough capital to really be sure you can pay anything that comes along, you have so much capital in the business that it isn't worthwhile." Float shown as a balance-sheet liability — "it's extraordinary." Ajit Jain: "somebody would have to give me more than $50 billion to undo everything he has produced for Berkshire." Tony Nicely similarly. *Munger:* "Is there anybody in the world who has a big casualty insurance business that you'd trade our business for theirs?" See [float](../concepts/float.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md), [national-indemnity](../entities/national-indemnity.md), [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md).

**M22 · Don't go overboard on delayed gratification.** *Buffett:* Munger: gratification-delay is innate, not trained. Buffett's gloss: in today's world — 30-year Treasury at 3%, Fed targeting 2% inflation — holding fixed income means you "get to go to Disneyland and ride the same number of rides 30 years from now that you would if you did it now." Delayed gratification is not an unqualified virtue; loads of money beyond a point doesn't produce happiness. "I always believed in spending two or three cents out of every dollar I earn and saving the rest. But I've always had everything I wanted."

**★ M23 · Succession: Abel and Jain on stage for the first time.** *Buffett:* "You could not have two better operating managers than Greg and Ajit. They know the businesses better. They work harder, by far. And you are absolutely invited to ask questions directed to them at this meeting." Format not permanent; the two-person Q&A won't last forever. Ted and Todd won't answer investment questions — investment decisions are proprietary to Berkshire and advertising holdings hurts execution. On the institutional value of "Saturday call, Sunday commitment": "nobody in the world doubts whether that commitment will be upheld — it's got nothing more than Berkshire's word, and that's an asset that every now and then will be worth a lot of money, and I don't think it will be subject to competition." *Munger:* "Berkshire is so very peculiar. There's only one thing like it... You're just going to have to endure us." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [greg-abel](../people/greg-abel.md), [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md).

**M24 · Bitcoin: Judas Iscariot and the Flamingo.** *Munger:* announced he was invited to a bitcoin-people happy hour during the meeting: "I finally figured out what the bitcoin people do in their happy hour. They celebrate the life and work of Judas Iscariot." *Buffett:* recalled standing in the Flamingo Hotel on his 1952 honeymoon watching "well-dressed people who had come from thousands of miles away to do something that every damn one of them knew was mathematically dumb. I told Susie, we are going to make a lot of money. Bitcoin has rejuvenated that feeling in me."

**M25 · 10% ownership threshold: two reasons to stay below — for now.** *Buffett:* Federal Reserve bank-holding rules require staying below 10% to avoid bank holding company classification. Separately: Section 16 short-swing profit recovery — any profit on a sale within six months by a >10% holder must be returned to the company. "It restricts significantly your ability to reverse a position or change your mind." Going above 10% also triggers two-to-three-day purchase reporting, which signals the market and increases execution costs. If the Fed loosens rules, Berkshire will likely drift above 10% in more holdings through investee share repurchases. "If we like 9.5 percent of a company, we'd like 15 percent better."

**M26 · Starting an investment fund: alignment before capital.** *Buffett:* in May 1956, formed the Buffett Partnership by sitting seven people down for dinner and setting ground rules: "here is how I want to be judged, and if you're not in sync with me on this, I don't want to manage your money, because you may be disappointed when I think things are even better." Refused institutional partners entirely (committees = panic potential). The prerequisite: "I would not have done that if I thought there was any chance I would lose the money — not about how I would behave, but how they would behave." *Munger:* the Mozart story — a young man asked how to compose symphonies; Mozart said he was too young at 22. "But you were writing them at 10." "Yes, but I wasn't running around asking other people how to do it."

**M28 · Precision Castparts: margins below projections but improvement expected.** *Buffett:* earnings are below what was projected at acquisition; improvement expected "fairly significantly" within a year or two as aircraft program learning curves mature and volume scales. $400M/year in non-economic purchase amortization understates true earnings power. "You'll have that question for me next year, and I think I'll be giving you a different answer." *Supersession flag:* COVID-19 devastated aviation demand in 2020; Berkshire took a $9.8 billion goodwill impairment on Precision Castparts in Q2 2020 — one of its largest single write-downs — reversing this optimistic projection. See [economic-goodwill](../concepts/economic-goodwill.md).

**M29 · Older = better understanding of human behavior.** *Buffett:* most cognitive abilities decline with age, but understanding of human behavior improves with experience — "you just have more experience with it. And I don't think you can get to be an expert on human behavior at all by reading books, no matter what your I.Q." *Munger:* Lee Kuan Yew's mantra: "Figure out what works, and do it. If you just go at life with that simple philosophy from your own national group, you will find it works wonderfully well."

**★ M30 · Ajit Jain on pricing unconventional insurance contracts.** *Jain:* first, apply all available historical data science; second, impose a strict cap on maximum exposure so you know exactly how much can be lost if you're wrong; third, if a price can be found that embeds a significant margin of safety in the subjective probability of the event, it's worth taking. "More often than not, it's impossible to have a point of view, and we end up passing." The acid test: pick up the phone and call Warren. *Buffett:* "We do occasionally get inquiries about doing things that really nobody else in the world can do. It's a little like our investment situation, only transferred over to insurance." *Munger:* "We're not holding anything back. It's hard. And it's not very teachable." *Note: 2019 is the first year Jain fields operational questions on stage.* See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md), [super-cat-insurance](../concepts/super-cat-insurance.md), [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md).

**M31/M32 · 3G relationship and Kraft Heinz brand defense.** *Buffett:* 3G remains a genuine partner; Jorge Paulo Lemann is "a good friend." Possible to partner again, though 3G has a greater appetite for leverage and paying up than Berkshire. The brand-vs.-retailer fight is real: "at See's Candy we sell directly to the consumer, but at Kraft Heinz they're intermediaries." Direct brands (GEICO) are structurally protected; channel-dependent brands must fight for margin against retailers whose power is growing. *Munger:* "a long series of transactions that worked very well, and finally there was one transaction at the end that didn't work so well. That is a very normal outcome of success in a big place with a lot of young men who want to get rich quick. It's so much easier to take the good ideas and push them to wretched excess." See [sees-candies](../entities/sees-candies.md), [geico](../entities/geico.md), [direct-distribution-moat](../concepts/direct-distribution-moat.md).

**★ M33 · Apple: biggest problem is the stock keeps going up.** *Buffett:* "what hurts, in the case of Apple, is that the stock has gone up." The buyback arithmetic: Apple authorized another $75B in repurchases; at $200/share that retires 500M of 4.6B shares outstanding; at $150 it retires 667M — Berkshire's percentage ownership rises automatically. "We'd much rather have the stock at a lower price so we could buy more stock." Regulatory and antitrust risks (Spotify complaint, Elizabeth Warren's App Store proposals) acknowledged but not dispositive to the thesis. *Munger:* "in my family, the people who have Apple phones, it's the last thing they'll give up." See [apple](../entities/apple.md), [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md).

## Q&A digest — Afternoon session

**A1 · BNSF vs. Union Pacific: the operating ratio gap.** *Buffett:* BNSF receives the lowest ton-mile revenue of any of the six major North American railroads, partly explained by longer average haul lengths. But Union Pacific has achieved a 4+-point operating ratio advantage — "they have been operating more efficiently than we have during the last few years." BNSF has a wonderful franchise; "there's no fundamental reason why the BNSF franchise couldn't have margins comparable to other railroads." UP cut significantly here in Omaha and Buffett is "measuring ourselves very carefully against what they do." *Munger:* "I don't know much about it." See [bnsf](../entities/bnsf.md), [commodity-business-economics](../concepts/commodity-business-economics.md).

**A4 · FlightSafety and the 737 MAX.** *Buffett:* FlightSafety specializes in corporate and fractional-jet pilots, not airline training — the 737 MAX issues won't produce meaningful incremental demand. Al Ueltschi (founder) built it around a genuine mission of saving lives: "that spirit still continues." NetJets pilots fly only one aircraft model and receive maximum annual simulator training. *Munger:* FlightSafety has never killed a passenger. See [flightsafety](../entities/flightsafety.md), [netjets](../entities/netjets.md).

**★ A5 · Tech moats are real but must be within Berkshire's own circle.** *Buffett:* responds to 9-year-old Daphne's question about whether Berkshire needs to embrace tech platforms as the new moat holders. "You're exactly right that tech can build incredibly valuable moats. I've not felt the confidence that I was the best one to judge that in many cases." Won't expand circle by subcontracting judgment to specialists: "We won't go into something because somebody else tells us it's a good thing to do. We are not going to subcontract your money to somebody else's judgment." Ted and Todd can cover territory Buffett and Munger cannot. *Munger:* "maybe" [the answer to whether Berkshire needs more tech exposure]. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**★ A7 · GEICO vs. Progressive: Jain's honest scorecard.** *Jain:* GEICO has approximately a 7-point expense ratio advantage over Progressive. Progressive has approximately a 12-point loss ratio advantage over GEICO. Net: Progressive is ahead by about 5 points overall. GEICO is aware of the loss ratio gap and has projects underway to close it; telematics and data-driven pricing are the focus. *Buffett:* "It's to some extent a two-horse race, and we've got a very good horse." Would bet significant money GEICO increases market share in five years. *Munger:* "every once in a while, somebody's a little better at something than we are." *Supersession flag:* the loss-ratio gap widened in 2021–2022 before Jain took direct management control of GEICO and instituted structural reforms including workforce reduction and disciplined underwriting exit. See [geico](../entities/geico.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md), [direct-distribution-moat](../concepts/direct-distribution-moat.md), [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md).

**★ A8 · Building circle of competence: read broadly, stay realistic about the perimeter.** *Buffett:* "I would just do a whole lot of reading… try to learn as much as I could about as many businesses, and figure out which ones I really had some important knowledge and understanding that was probably different, overwhelmingly, from most of my competitors. And I would also try and figure out which ones I didn't understand." Knowing even one area deeply gives a durable edge; "you just wait till the odds are strongly in your favor." Cites discovery of GEICO via Lorimer Davidson on a 1951 Saturday: "what he said made so much sense to me in the three or four hours I spent with him." *Munger:* "the great strategy, for the great mass of humanity, is to specialize. Nobody wants to go to a doctor that's half proctologist and half dentist. Warren and I really didn't do that. And I don't think we could recommend it to other people." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [lorimer-davidson](../people/lorimer-davidson.md).

**A9 · ESG: performance good, expensive reports unnecessary.** *Buffett:* Berkshire performs well on environmental metrics — Iowa heading to 100% wind generation, NV Energy coal fleet being retired — but will not prepare elaborate ESG reports for rating agencies. "We don't want to be preparing a lot of reports and asking 60 subsidiaries each to do something, where they'll set up a team, and they'll mail things to headquarters, and then we'll supply them to somebody who, if our stock goes up some, is probably going to sell it anyway." Trust managers; performance over paperwork. *Munger:* "the people that talk about best corporate practices don't really know what the best practices are. They just know what they think are the best practices. And they determine that based on what will sell, not what will work." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md).

**★ A10 · "Independent" directors aren't really independent.** *Buffett:* after sitting on 20 public company boards, the observation is structural: if $250K/year in director fees is meaningful income, the "independent" director has a powerful financial incentive never to rock the boat. "They put them on the comp committee" — the director whose income depends on CEO goodwill will not oppose compensation proposals. "I can't recall any independent director where their income from the board was important to them ever doing anything in board meetings that actually was counter to the interest…" *Munger:* "I think it works a little worse than Warren's telling you. It's really awful." See [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md), [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).

**★ A11 · "We will put a lot of money into energy" (Abel).** *Abel:* Iowa is at or near 100% renewable output relative to customer consumption — leading the nation. PacifiCorp transmission expansion begun in 2008 is now entering its second and third build phases. NV Energy rates are down 5–7% since Berkshire acquired it (prior management raised rates nearly every two years). NV Energy coal fleet will be fully retired by approximately 2023. Capital spending guidance of $4B in 2021 understates what is coming in later phases. "We do have the planning in place for the second phase and the third phase." *Munger:* "Are our rates about half that of our leading competitor in Iowa?" *Abel:* "Exactly. We're right in that range." *Note: 2019 is the first year Abel gives operational answers on stage.* See [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md), [greg-abel](../people/greg-abel.md).

**★ A12 · Why Berkshire doesn't put excess cash in an index fund.** *Buffett:* a "perfectly rational observation" — he'd rather own an index fund than T-bills, and that may be a policy his successor uses. The constraint is scale and timing: $100B+ deployed into equities cannot be rapidly converted back to purchasing power when a crisis creates genuinely extraordinary opportunities. "We committed $10 billion a week ago [Occidental]. There are conditions under which we could spend $100 billion very, very quickly." Cash is held for the two or three periods in the next 20-30 years when "it'll be raining gold and all you have to do is go outside." *Munger:* "I watched Harvard use the last ounce of their cash, including all their prepaid tuition from the parents, and plunge it into the market at exactly the wrong moment. We don't want to be like Harvard." See [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md), [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

**A13 · Clayton Homes / manufactured housing: better Fannie/Freddie access is good.** *Buffett:* expanded Fannie/Freddie financing for manufactured homes is good for America even if it reduces Clayton's loan income. "It may not help [Clayton's] profits, but it definitely is good." During 2008–09, Clayton's low-FICO borrowers kept making payments because "the home was an enormously important item to them" — loan experience far better than anticipated. Now nine site-built home operations (none existed a few years ago). *Munger:* Clayton "will get bigger and bigger and bigger as far ahead as you can see." See [clayton-homes](../entities/clayton-homes.md).

**A14 · Automation and jobs: capitalism's record is reassuring.** *Buffett:* since 1776, capitalism has been "figuring out ways to get rid of jobs" — that is what it does — while employment keeps growing. From 80% of the population on farms to less than 2% today, yet 160M jobs exist for 330M people when 4M people had to farm to feed everyone in 1776. "Our economy and our people, our system, has been remarkably ingenious in achieving whatever we have now… We never know exactly where they're going to come from. I do know there will be a next big thing." *Munger:* "We want to shift the scut work to the robots to the extent we can. That's what we were doing, as Warren said, for 200 years." See [american-tailwind](../concepts/american-tailwind.md).

**A15 · Regulation: irritating but necessary for insurance and banking.** *Buffett:* insurance has been regulated since Berkshire entered the business; GEICO went from $7M to $30B+ in premiums under regulation throughout. "Anytime you can take other people's money, and they go home with a promise and you go home with the money, I don't mind a certain amount of regulation in those businesses." FDIC has never cost the government a dollar and has built $100B in reserves entirely from bank fees. *Munger:* "I absolutely believe we should have a regulation system that involves supervision of risk-taking by banks."

**A16 · Less subsidiary detail in annual reports — by design.** *Buffett:* writes the annual report for "Doris and Birdie" — his sisters — people who have a significant proportion of their net worth in Berkshire but don't know what a "combined ratio" or "operating ratio" means. "You can write a 300-page report that gives a whole lot less information than a 50-page report." Detailed segment data would help competitors more than shareholders. *Munger:* "competitors would like [more detail], and it wouldn't do our shareholders any good."

**★ A19 · International acquisitions: price is the constraint, not market access.** *Buffett:* Berkshire hears about international deals; Eitan Wertheimer arranged European introductions after ISCAR. Problem is not awareness. *Munger:* "Our problem is not a lack of boots on the ground, our problem is the people on the ground are paying prices that we don't want to pay. And that problem is not going to be cured by boots." Private equity competitors: "somebody else's money, upside but not the downside — it is hard to compete with people like that." *Buffett:* "We can spend $100 billion in the next year. That is not a problem. Spending it intelligently is a huge problem." If family sellers want an auction, Berkshire won't participate. See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md).

**★ A20/A21 · Berkshire as a long-term compounding machine.** *Buffett:* "It is the primary motivation." Culture is stronger than 10 years ago and was stronger then than 10 years before that. Berkshire "will be one of the safest ways to make decent money over time" — not the highest compounder, but distinctively reliable. "If there were to be a very strong bull market from this point forward, we would probably underperform during that period. If the market five years from now or 10 years from now is at this level or below, we will probably overperform." A structural caveat he adds is easy to miss: a C-corp carries a tax drag an index fund does not — "if we just owned stocks, and we owned the S&P, our performance would be significantly worse than the S&P because we would be incurring a corporate tax" (21% on capital gains) that a pass-through index fund's holders never pay. *Munger:* "the fact that it slows down a little when it becomes monstrous is not my idea of a huge tragedy." Munger's tax gloss: even if Berkshire "just matched the S&P," its taxable holders would be "way ahead after taxes." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**A22 · NV Energy: casino defections and the recovery path (Abel).** *Abel:* five large casino customers left NV Energy in year 7 of ownership to avoid bearing societal costs (renewables mandates, efficiency programs). NV Energy inherited strained relationships from prior management's years of rate increases. Under Berkshire: rates down 5–7% since acquisition; coal fleet on track for full retirement by 2023; load has grown back through the customer exits. Legislation is leveling the regulatory playing field. "Our team has the right model." See [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md), [greg-abel](../people/greg-abel.md).

**A23 · Combs and Weschler: roughly matching S&P, with ancillary value beyond portfolios.** *Buffett:* as of March 31, 2019, one manager modestly ahead of S&P from inception, one modestly behind. Both "doing better than I am anyway." Beyond portfolio returns: Todd Combs has led the Haven healthcare JV work; Ted Weschler stabilized Home Capital Group in Canada ("heard about it Monday, put an offer before the company on Wednesday"). "They are very, very smart… they've made us a lot of money, but they made it during a market where you'd have made a lot of money in the S&P as well." See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md).

**★ A24 · American Express: the brand that outlasted traveler's checks.** *Buffett:* AmEx was founded in 1850 by Wells and Fargo; in 1950, the full company earned $3M in a record travel year — entirely from traveler's checks. Then entered the credit card business after Diners Club, later than Carte Blanche, but came to dominate the luxury segment because of status signaling ("you looked like J.P. Morgan" pulling out the Centurion vs. a Diners Club card). Platinum Card raised fees from $450 to $550 during a JPMorgan Sapphire offensive — and retention improved, new customer acquisition improved, 68% of new accounts were millennials. Berkshire's cost basis will be fully earned back by AmEx dividends and retained earnings "by next year." Ownership growing automatically via AmEx buybacks. *Munger:* "We own the world as long as the technology stays the same." WeChat noted as the cloud he can't assess. See [american-express](../entities/american-express.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**★ A25 · Occidental $10B: Berkshire's 48-hour commitment capacity.** *Buffett:* received the call from Occidental management on a Friday afternoon (or Saturday morning, per M21 context); met with them Sunday; deal announced Tuesday. $10 billion preferred at 8% coupon plus warrants to acquire 80M shares of Oxy at $62.50. Fully committed with no MAC clause, no committee review, no conditions. "If somebody wants a lot of certain money for a deal, they've seen that I can get a call on Friday afternoon, make a date Saturday, and on Sunday it's done. And they absolutely know they have $10 billion and we're not going to tell them how to structure their transaction." This won't be the last such transaction; could be $20B or $50B next time. *Munger:* "Well, I like it." *Note: Berkshire subsequently exercised warrants and accumulated OXY common stock to become a ≈25% common shareholder by 2022-2023, deepening rather than exiting the relationship.* See [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md), [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**★ A26 · No formula for risk — and no formula-based decision-making at Berkshire.** *Buffett:* "We don't have any formula that evaluates risk, but we certainly make our own calculation of risk versus reward in every transaction." The process: verify you know what you're doing (the hardest hurdle); then calculate the math of gain vs. loss and how much loss can be absorbed. Won't use committees and spreadsheets because "if I had a group under me, they would try and figure out what I wanted the answer to be, and they would tell me what I wanted to hear." "We have no formulas around Berkshire. We don't sit down and write a bunch." The hurdle-rate spreadsheet culture: "if you have a 15% hurdle, they'll all come out at 15.1 or 15.2. You're going to get the numbers you want to hear." See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md), [risk-vs-volatility](../concepts/risk-vs-volatility.md).

**★ A27 · Dishonest business propositions from sellers and bankers.** *Buffett:* two recent examples. First: private company presenting $100M in earnings but asking Berkshire to value on $110M because as a private company they expensed cash compensation, while Berkshire could replace it with stock options — "by essentially lying about our accounting, we could add $10M in earnings, and they wanted us to pay them for that." Second: a private equity firm accidentally forwarded an internal email to Berkshire, instructing management to add 15% to projections: "Buffett will discount it by 15–20% anyway, so just add 15% to offset his conservatism." *Munger:* "Why do we want our leading citizens lying and cheating?" See [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md), [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md).

**A28 · Tesla insurance: won't threaten GEICO.** *Buffett:* General Motors ran Motors Insurance Company for decades — auto companies have tried insurance before. "I would say the success of auto companies getting insurance business are about as likely as the success of the insurance companies getting into the auto business." Telematics (driving behavior data) is genuinely valuable and is spreading widely, but auto companies have no advantage in underwriting or claims management that would allow them to price more accurately than GEICO or Progressive. "I worry much more about Progressive than all of the auto company possibilities." See [geico](../entities/geico.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**★ A29 · Betting the entire estate on Berkshire's culture.** *Buffett:* "My estate will have basically nothing but Berkshire in it for some time as it gets disbursed to philanthropies." Will instruction to trustees: you are exempt from the normal trustee obligation to diversify; keep it all in Berkshire as you distribute it. "I have a lot of confidence in the ability of the Berkshire culture to endure. I'm betting my entire net worth on that. And that doesn't give me pause at all." *Munger:* "I don't own any indexes. I have always been willing to own just two or three stocks. I don't think you need a portfolio of 50 stocks if you know what you're doing. And I hope my heirs will just sit." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [concentration-vs-diversification](../concepts/concentration-vs-diversification.md), [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**A30 · Having the right partners in life.** *Buffett:* in 60 years with Munger, "we've never had an argument" — disagreements happened, but not arguments involving emotion or anger. "Having the right partners in life, particularly the right spouse, but having the right partners in life is enormously important. It's more fun with a partner, both in personal life and in business life." *Munger:* "There's always a new rock to be turned over. And it's interesting. It's a very interesting thing. We've been collecting all our lives."

**A31 · Expand circle of competence if you can, but don't force it.** *Buffett:* "You can't force it. You can expand it a little bit over time." Learned about the energy business from Walter Scott and Greg Abel — "but I'm not close to their level of competence." *Munger:* "If you can't — I'd be pretty cautious." Some people have a "money mind" that works well in certain situations; it isn't about IQ. Tom Watson Sr.: "I'm no genius, but I'm smart in spots and I stay around those spots." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ A32 · 50% on a million — size destroys fringe advantages.** *Buffett:* "if Charlie or I was working with a million dollars, we would find a way to make [50%] with essentially no risk." Little-noticed fringe inefficiencies, small arbitrages, obscure situations. "You change the one million to a hundred million and that 50 goes down like a rock." At Berkshire's scale, those inefficiencies are unreachable. *Munger:* "I've just seen genius after genius with a great record and pretty soon they got 30 billion and two floors of young men and away goes the good record. That's just the way it works." See [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md).

**A33 · See's Candies: does not travel.** *Buffett:* tried 10–12 geographic expansion ideas over decades; none of them worked at the unit economics that make See's such a spectacular business. West coast: milk chocolate, chunky pieces; east coast: dark chocolate, miniatures. "When we open a store in the east, we get enormous traffic for a while… then we end up with a store that does X pounds per year when we need 1.5X in the same square footage." $25M invested → $2B+ pretax income; far better to redeploy the earnings elsewhere than force geographic expansion. *Munger:* "We failed in turning our little candy company into Mars or Hershey's for the same reason that you fail to get the Nobel Prize in physics and achieve immortality. It's too tough for us." See [sees-candies](../entities/sees-candies.md), [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md), [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md).

## Concepts introduced or discussed

- [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md) — second consecutive year emphasizing that mark-to-market equity swings through income make GAAP net income "potentially harmful"; KHC equity earnings gap adds another layer of distortion in Q1 2019
- [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md) — most complete Aesop formulation: "how certain is the bush, how far away, what's the worst case, how many birds could there be"; applies identically to Amazon at high multiple and bank at 0.7x book
- [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md) — partnership arithmetic stated most cleanly here; "$900K for $1M partner interest → remaining two jump to $1.05M each"; willingness to spend $100B at 25-30% discount; Apple's buyback accretion as passive ownership increase
- [cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses](../concepts/cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses.md) — Kraft Heinz provides the negative case: even a wonderful business can destroy investment value if acquired at the wrong price; Buffett explicitly rejects cigar-butt approach as current Berkshire philosophy
- [float](../concepts/float.md) — $124B at 2019; structural advantage over standalone insurers (no external reinsurance needed); three of world's largest reinsurers nearly went extinct; worth more inside Berkshire than standalone
- [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md) — Jain's unconventional insurance framework: science + strict exposure cap + margin of safety in subjective probability; GEICO/Progressive loss-ratio gap quantified for first time publicly
- [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md) — Occidental deal as proof of 48-hour $10B deployment; cash held for "raining gold" moments, not index-fund substitute; Harvard's crisis as the counter-example
- [moat](../concepts/moat.md) — brands vs. retailers (Kirkland > all Kraft Heinz); direct-to-consumer brands (GEICO) structurally protected vs. channel-dependent brands; tech moats are real but beyond current circle; "no fundamental reason BNSF can't have margins comparable to peers"
- [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md) — no formulas; no committees; the process is identical for securities, private deals, and insurance: confirm you know what you're doing, cap the downside, assess the math; size destroys fringe advantages
- [institutional-imperative](../concepts/institutional-imperative.md) — Wells Fargo/Salomon both trace to the same failure: finding a pyromaniac and leaving the matches; CEO accountability when fraud surfaces is the only corrective
- [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md) — independent directors are often least independent because $250K fees create financial dependency on CEO approval; comp committee placement compounds the conflict
- [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md) — expand only genuinely, not by subcontracting to specialists; "money mind" as a natural aptitude, not IQ; Tom Watson Sr.'s "smart in spots" as the operational goal
- [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md) — $1M → 50% possible; $100M → "50 goes down like a rock"; fringe inefficiencies are unreachable at Berkshire scale; same principle applies to international acquisitions (constraint is price, not access)
- [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md) — no auctions, no MACs, no committees; price is the real constraint internationally, not market access; PE's structural advantage (other people's money, upside without downside) makes competition irrational
- [direct-distribution-moat](../concepts/direct-distribution-moat.md) — GEICO's direct model structurally protects margin (no channel to fight); Kraft Heinz's channel dependency is its moat vulnerability; "our gross margin is their [retailer's] opportunity"
- [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md) — no desire to push stock up or down; write for "Doris and Birdie," not analysts; Buffett's estate entirely in Berkshire as ultimate owner signal
- [mistakes-of-omission](../concepts/mistakes-of-omission.md) — Google: "we could see in our own operations how well the advertising was working and just sat there sucking our thumbs"; Amazon called a miracle in 2018 (M28 2018), still treated with deference here

## Connections to other sources

- The Kraft Heinz admission (M17) is the live companion to the write-down discussed in the 2019 annual letter; Buffett's "we paid too much for Kraft" here is sharper and more unhedged than any written formulation. The error mirrors the USAir overpayment anatomy from [berkshire-1994-meeting](berkshire-1994-meeting.md) M40 — in both cases the business itself was identifiable as challenged, but the price paid sealed the outcome.
- The Aesop value-investing framework (M20) is the most complete version of an argument that appears implicitly across two decades of Berkshire letters, crystallized here by the Amazon question. It connects directly to the intrinsic-value DCF framework in [berkshire-1994-meeting](berkshire-1994-meeting.md) A22 ("present value of all future cash at the appropriate rate") but strips out the technical language entirely.
- The Occidental deal (A25) echoes the 2008-crisis preferred transactions with Goldman Sachs, GE, and Wrigley discussed in [berkshire-2009-meeting](berkshire-2009-meeting.md) — but happens in a non-crisis environment, demonstrating that the "Saturday call, Sunday commitment" capacity is permanent, not cyclical.
- The GEICO vs. Progressive scorecard (A7) extends the competitive discussion begun in [berkshire-2017-meeting](berkshire-2017-meeting.md) and [berkshire-2018-meeting](berkshire-2018-meeting.md) with the first public quantification of the loss-ratio gap from Jain personally — a disclosure that 2018's M17 on GEICO's post-cat recovery did not include.
- The independent-director critique (A10) extends Buffett's governance observations in the 2003 annual letter about director independence and the structural forces that undermine it, here made more personal from his own board experience at 20 companies.
- Munger's "boots on the ground" price diagnosis (A19) directly echoes the [berkshire-2018-meeting](berkshire-2018-meeting.md) A22 international acquisitions discussion — "not on the radar screen the way the U.S. is" — but sharpens the diagnosis: the problem isn't visibility, it's that LBO buyers with other people's money set prices Berkshire won't rationally meet.
- The succession signal (M23/A11/A7/A30) makes concrete what the 2018 meeting's formal Abel/Jain VC elevation announced structurally — for the first time, the audience can begin assessing successors through their public reasoning, not only through Buffett's endorsements.
