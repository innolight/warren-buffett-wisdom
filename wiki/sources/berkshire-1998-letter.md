# Berkshire Hathaway 1998 Annual Letter

The 48.3% book-value gain is the first thing Buffett deflates — most of it came from issuing shares in acquisitions, not from earning a dime. The year's durable contributions are conceptual: a canonical essay on how to read insurance economics through float's cost, a demonstration that permanent capital dissolves the structural paradox facing every publicly-traded reinsurer, the fullest expression to date of the owner-operating mandate Berkshire extends to its CEOs, and paired essays on why stock options are a hidden expense and why earnings management is institutional corruption wearing respectable clothes. The General Re and Executive Jet acquisitions are the year's vehicles; these five ideas are the cargo.

## Key themes

### Permanent capital dissolves the reinsurance paradox

Reinsurers are paid to absorb the earnings volatility that primary insurers want to shed. The paradox: a publicly-traded reinsurer is then penalized — in credit ratings and P/E multiples — for exactly the volatility it is being paid to hold. The rational response is to lay off business via retrocessions or reject sound but lumpy risk, which destroys value. Berkshire dissolves the paradox by structure, not skill. "We are a Fort Knox of capital, and that means volatile earnings can't impair our premier credit ratings." The acquisition mandate to General Re: stop offloading what you should be retaining, expand internationally, exercise underwriting discipline without the public-company constraint. "As we've long said, we prefer a lumpy 15% return to a smooth 12%."

The lesson generalizes beyond reinsurance: wherever a business produces volatile-but-positive expected returns, permanent-capital ownership creates value by removing the market's tax on variance. Berkshire asked only that [General Re](../entities/general-re.md)'s management team do what it already knew how to do — unfettered from the constraints that had been forcing value-destroying retrocessions.

### Float: three determinants that decide whether insurance is a business or a lemon

"The key determinants are: (1) the amount of float that the business generates; (2) its cost; and (3) most important of all, the long-term outlook for both of these factors." Float is the pool of premiums collected before claims are paid — money the insurer holds and invests for its own account. Its cost is the underwriting result: if premiums cover losses plus expenses, float is free; if not, the shortfall is the price of holding it.

"An insurance business has value if its cost of float over time is less than the cost the company would otherwise incur to obtain funds. But the business is a lemon if its cost of float is higher than market rates for money."

Berkshire's 32-year record puts numbers behind the claim: float compounded at 25.4% annually from $17 million in 1967 to $22.8 billion at yearend 1998 (the General Re acquisition drove most of the jump from $7.1 billion the prior year), with average cost over the period well under zero — Berkshire was paid to hold and invest a growing capital pool. The balance-sheet accounting inverts the economic reality: "though our net float is recorded on our balance sheet as a liability, it has had more economic value to us than an equal amount of net worth would have had. As long as we can continue to achieve an underwriting profit, float will continue to outrank net worth in value." Costless float, persistently maintained, is better than equity.

### The owner-operator mandate: what Berkshire actually offers a first-class CEO

1998 gives the clearest articulation to date of the operating framework Berkshire extends to its managers. The three-part mandate is precise: "Just run your business as if: 1) you own 100% of it; 2) it is the only asset in the world that you and your family have or will ever have; and 3) you can't sell or merge it for at least a century. As a corollary, we tell them they should not let any of their decisions be affected even slightly by accounting considerations. We want our managers to think about what counts, not how it will be counted."

This mandate is made credible by structure: Berkshire's shareholder base has "the longest investment horizon to be found in the public-company universe. Indeed, a majority of our shares are held by investors who expect to die still holding them." The mandate is also cost-free in a way no public company can match — Berkshire removes every ritualistic demand on a CEO's time: no quarterly earnings guidance, no investor-relations grind, no credit-rating management, no banker presentations.

GEICO's 1998 results are the proof of concept. Tony Nicely grew market share from 3% to 3.5%, spent $143 million on marketing (up from $33 million in 1995 at Berkshire's acquisition), paid $103 million in profit-sharing — 32.3% of salary for all 9,313 eligible associates — and generated a 6.7% underwriting margin. None of that is possible under conventional quarterly-earnings pressure, where first-year acquisition costs ($143 million in marketing to build a book that renews profitably) would be treated as a drag to be avoided. Berkshire's compensation design matches the mandate: 50% of bonuses tied to seasoned-book (>1 year) earnings, 50% to policyholder growth. First-year costs excluded from the formula so growth is never penalized.

### Options are compensation; compensation is an expense; stop pretending otherwise

The General Re transaction made the contradiction unavoidable. Replacing General Re's stock-option plan with an economically identical cash plan increased the combined 1997 pro-forma compensation expense by $63 million — not because anything changed economically, but because accounting rules require cash pay to appear in earnings while options disappear. "Accounting principles offer management a choice: Pay employees in one form and count the cost, or pay them in another form and ignore the cost."

The practical correction for a shareholder: when evaluating any option-issuing company, subtract from reported earnings what the company could have realized by publicly selling options of like quantity and structure. "The earning revisions that Charlie and I have made for options in recent years have frequently cut the reported per-share figures by 5%, with 10% not all that uncommon." The exercise has caused outright sales and passed purchases. The logical foundation: "If options aren't a form of compensation, what are they? If compensation isn't an expense, what is it? And, if expenses shouldn't go into the calculation of earnings, where in the world should they go?"

### Bad accounting drives out good: the earnings-management epidemic

The options essay opens into a broader indictment. By 1998, a "significant and growing number of otherwise high-grade managers" had concluded that earnings manipulation was not merely acceptable but their duty. The mechanism: take a restructuring charge ("the distortion du jour") in an already-bad quarter, seeding future "earnings" from which bad holes are excluded. Wall Street rewards the sequence 140, 80, 80, 80, 80 — calling the 140 a "discontinued swing" — and punishes the honest sequence 91, 94, 89, 94, 92. "Call this behavior Son of Gresham: Bad accounting drives out good."

In P-C insurance acquisitions the variant is sharper: acquirers routinely boost target-company loss reserves at deal close, creating a store of future income to be released as reserves are drawn down. "Berkshire has kept entirely clear of these practices: In all of our acquisitions, we have left the loss reserve figures exactly as we found them." The scale of the epidemic in 1998: 1,369 special charges were recorded totaling $72.1 billion — against the $324 billion of 1997 Fortune 500 earnings. The addictiveness is the key insight: "it's easier to fiddle with the scorecard than to spend hours on the practice tee." Each manipulation makes the next one necessary.

The Berkshire principle applied to its own reporting: "If we are to disappoint you, we would rather it be with our earnings than with our accounting."

### Scale forces discipline: activity does not correlate with achievement

At any capital base, frenetic deal-making is counterproductive — the insight sharpens arithmetically as the base grows. "In allocating capital, activity does not correlate with achievement. Indeed, in the fields of investments and acquisitions, frenetic behavior is often counterproductive." Waiting for the right idea is not idleness; it is discipline. At $57.4 billion of net worth, a 15% compounding target requires adding $58 billion over five years: "Earning this daunting 15% will require us to come up with big ideas: Popcorn stands just won't do." The year's own portfolio actions confirmed the point from the other direction: selling McDonald's was "a very big mistake," and "you would have been better off last year if I had regularly snuck off to the movies during market hours."
