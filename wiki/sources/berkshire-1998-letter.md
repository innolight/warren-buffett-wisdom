# Berkshire Hathaway 1998 Annual Letter

Dated March 1, 1999. Per-share book value up 48.3% to $37,801 — but Buffett opens by warning that "most of that 48.3% gain came from our issuing shares in acquisitions" and that the per-share gain in intrinsic value fell well short of the book-value figure. The defining 1998 event is the **$22 billion acquisition of General Re**, closed December 21 — the largest deal in Berkshire's history and a transformative addition of $22.7 billion of yearend float. The year also brings the **$725 million acquisition of Executive Jet (NetJets)** (cash + stock), the canonical **economics-of-property-casualty-insurance essay** (the three determinants: amount, cost, long-term outlook of float), the **stock-options-as-expense essay** (Accounting Part 1) reopened around the General Re cash-comp swap, and the **earnings-management essay** (Accounting Part 2) on restructuring charges and "Son of Gresham" accounting. Buffett's portfolio confession: "my decision to sell McDonald's was a very big mistake."

## Key themes

- **General Re — $22B reinsurance acquisition.** Closed December 21. Largest property-casualty reinsurer in the U.S.; with 82%-owned Cologne Re, operates in 124 countries. Strategic rationale: Berkshire's "Fort Knox of capital" and tolerance for lumpy earnings let General Re retain more of what it writes and escape the public-reinsurer's penalty for volatility. Ron Ferguson keeps running it; Berkshire takes over the General Re investment portfolio (not Cologne Re's). 1998 yearend float jumps to $22.762B from $7.093B a year earlier.
- **The reinsurance paradox.** "A reinsurer gets paid for absorbing the volatility that the client insurer wants to shed" — yet a publicly-held reinsurer is itself penalized by markets for that same volatility, forcing it to lay off business via retrocessions or reject good business. Berkshire's structure dissolves the paradox: "we prefer a lumpy 15% return to a smooth 12%."
- **Executive Jet (NetJets) — $725M acquisition.** Cash + stock; closed August 7, 1998. Rich Santulli, the industry's creator (1986). 1,000+ customers, 163 aircraft. The fractional-ownership economics: a client owning 1/8 of a jet gets 100 flying hours annually, plane-on-call at 5,500 U.S. airports. EJA accounted for 31% of all corporate jets ordered globally in 1997.
- **Economics of P-C insurance — canonical essay.** "The key determinants are: (1) the amount of float that the business generates; (2) its cost; and (3) most important of all, the long-term outlook for both of these factors." A business has value if cost of float is less than the cost of obtaining funds otherwise; it is a lemon if cost of float exceeds market rates. Berkshire's 32-year average cost of float: well under zero. Float compounded 25.4% annually 1967–1998 (from $17M to $22.762B).
- **Float-outranks-net-worth claim.** "Indeed, though our net float is recorded on our balance sheet as a liability, it has had more economic value to us than an equal amount of net worth would have had. As long as we can continue to achieve an underwriting profit, float will continue to outrank net worth in value." Continues the [[float]]-as-equity essay of [[berkshire-1995-letter]].
- **GEICO — Tony Nicely's record year.** 1998 new auto policies: 1,317,761; in-force: 3,562,644 (vs. 2,949,439 in 1997). Marketing spend up from $33M in 1995 to $143M in 1998; budgeted $190M for 1999. 1998 profit margin 6.7%; rates cut 3.3% and will be cut further in 1999, targeting 4% margins. Profit-sharing payment of $103M was 32.3% of salary to all 9,313 eligible associates. Market share moved from 3% to 3.5%. Counselor count grew from 652 (1995) to 2,162.
- **The "GEICO environment" essay — pitch to other CEOs.** Berkshire's structure liberates first-class operators: no backseat driving, no quarterly-earnings pressure, no investor-relations grind. "Just run your business as if: 1) you own 100% of it; 2) it is the only asset in the world that you and your family have or will ever have; and 3) you can't sell or merge it for at least a century." Extends the [[acquisition-criteria]] / "selling to Berkshire" pitch.
- **Stock-options accounting — Part 1.** Reopened by the General Re cash-comp swap (replacing options with a cash plan that pays the same economically would increase 1997 pro-forma compensation expense by $63M). "If options aren't a form of compensation, what are they? If compensation isn't an expense, what is it? And, if expenses shouldn't go into the calculation of earnings, where in the world should they go?" Buffett's portfolio adjustment: in recent years, valuing options properly has cut 5% (sometimes 10%) off reported per-share earnings, and "has affected our portfolio decisions, causing us either to make a sale or to pass on a stock purchase we might otherwise have made."
- **Earnings management — Part 2.** "Son of Gresham: Bad accounting drives out good." Restructuring charges ("distortion du jour"), big baths, deal-time loss-reserve boosts. The golf-scorecard analogy: turn in a 140 once, then four 80s. Anecdote: bank CEO asked if a deal meant a $1B charge — "We'll make it bigger than that — that's why we're doing the deal." R.G. Associates count: 1,369 special charges in 1998, totaling $72.1B (vs. $324B in 1997 Fortune 500 earnings). Praise for SEC chairman Arthur Levitt's September 1998 speech.
- **Portfolio confession.** "The portfolio actions I took in 1998 actually decreased our gain for the year. In particular, my decision to sell McDonald's was a very big mistake. Overall, you would have been better off last year if I had regularly snuck off to the movies during market hours." Adds to [[mistakes-of-omission]] catalog (with Cap Cities, Disney 1967, Fannie Mae).
- **General Re portfolio liquidation.** Once the merger was certain, Berkshire instructed General Re to sell its 250 common-stock positions, incurring $935M of taxes. "We don't back into decisions."
- **Operating-earnings paradox.** Per-share operating earnings fell 33.9% in 1998 — but only because General Re's pro-forma underwriting loss was included. Without General Re, per-share operating earnings would have shown a modest gain. Investments per share grew 25.2% to $47,647.
- **15% intrinsic-value aspiration.** "Charlie and I will do our best to increase intrinsic value in the future at an average rate of 15%, a result we consider to be at the very peak of possible outcomes." Earning 15% on $57.4B of net worth requires adding $58B over five years — "Popcorn stands just won't do."
- **Internet reporting.** First letter mentioning berkshirehathaway.com as a primary disclosure venue; quarterlies to be posted online. Frames Berkshire's "equal-access" disclosure philosophy against the "selective disclosure" critique of Levitt's speech. GEICO, Borsheim's, See's, and Buffalo News now selling via the Internet; Ajit Jain launches an annuity product online.

Well, Berkshire's progress in 1998 — though more than satisfactory — was not as good as it looks. That's because most of that 48.3% gain came from our issuing shares in acquisitions.

> Our future rates of gain will fall far short of those achieved in the past. Berkshire's capital base is now simply too large to allow us to earn truly outsized returns. If you believe otherwise, you should consider a career in sales but avoid one in mathematics (bearing in mind that there are really only three kinds of people in the world: those who can count and those who can't).

> Earning this daunting 15% will require us to come up with big ideas: Popcorn stands just won't do. Today's markets are not friendly to our search for "elephants," but you can be sure that we will stay focused on the hunt.

> Whatever the future holds, I make you one promise: I'll keep at least 99% of my net worth in Berkshire for as long as I am around. How long will that be? My model is the loyal Democrat in Fort Wayne who asked to be buried in Chicago so that he could stay active in the party. To that end, I've already selected a "power spot" at the office for my urn.

> Combine a great idea with a great manager and you're certain to obtain a great result. That mix is alive and well at GEICO.

> Because of the first-year costs, companies that are concerned about quarterly or annual earnings would shy from similar investments, no matter how intelligent these might be in terms of building long-term value. Our calculus is different: We simply measure whether we are creating more than a dollar of value per dollar spent — and if that calculation is favorable, the more dollars we spend the happier I am.

> At Berkshire we feel that telling outstanding CEOs, such as Tony, how to run their companies would be the height of foolishness. … Besides, they are the Mark McGwires of the business world and need no advice from us as to how to hold the bat or when to swing.

> We give each a simple mission: Just run your business as if: 1) you own 100% of it; 2) it is the only asset in the world that you and your family have or will ever have; and 3) you can't sell or merge it for at least a century. As a corollary, we tell them they should not let any of their decisions be affected even slightly by accounting considerations. We want our managers to think about what counts, not how it will be counted.

That's an equation I explained to my wonderful Aunt Alice 40 years ago when she asked me whether she could afford a fur coat. My reply settled the issue: "Alice, you aren't buying it; your heirs are."

> Errors of estimation, usually innocent but sometimes not, can be huge. … As for Berkshire, Charlie and I attempt to be conservative in presenting its underwriting results to you, because we have found that virtually all surprises in insurance are unpleasant ones.

We have one excuse, though: In allocating capital, activity does not correlate with achievement. Indeed, in the fields of investments and acquisitions, frenetic behavior is often counterproductive. Therefore, Charlie and I mainly just wait for the phone to ring.

## The General Re acquisition — strategic logic

- **Size and structure.** $22B, closed December 21. General Reinsurance Corporation (100%) is the largest U.S. P-C reinsurer; Cologne Re (82% owned, including stock under arrangement to buy) is the oldest reinsurer in the world. Combined: all lines, 124 countries.
- **The reinsurance paradox.** A reinsurer is paid to absorb client insurers' volatility. But a publicly-held reinsurer is itself penalized for that volatility (credit ratings, P/E multiples). This forces it to lay off business via retrocessions or to reject good business. Berkshire's permanent capital and credit standing dissolve the penalty.
- **What Berkshire brings.** "Fort Knox of capital"; tolerance for lumpy earnings; tax and investment benefits; freedom to retain more business and expand internationally.
- **What General Re brings.** Distribution force, technical facilities, management, international footprint. "Berkshire can add absolutely nothing to the skills of General Re's and Cologne Re's managers. On the contrary, there is a lot that they can teach us."
- **Operating mandate.** Berkshire will NOT involve itself in underwriting. Berkshire WILL manage General Re's portfolio (not Cologne Re's). Ron Ferguson continues as CEO. Mandate: exercise the discipline of the past, increase retention proportion, expand product line, widen geography.
- **Portfolio clean sweep.** Once merger was certain, General Re sold ~250 common-stock positions, incurring $935M of taxes. "We don't back into decisions."
- **Historical thread.** "General Re played a key role in the resuscitation of GEICO from its near-death status in 1976."
- **SEC fee.** $30M check to the Treasury for SEC fee tied to the new shares — believed to set an SEC record.

## Executive Jet — fractional ownership

- **Deal:** $725M; equal cash and stock; closed August 7, 1998.
- **Founder/CEO:** Rich Santulli created the fractional-ownership industry in 1986.
- **Product:** 1/8th interest = 100 hours/year on a chosen jet (or another at least as good), available at 5,500 U.S. airports on a few hours' notice. Dead-head hours don't count. Five-year averaging of hours allowed.
- **Scale:** 1,000+ customers, 163 aircraft (including 23 core aircraft owned/leased by EJA itself for peak demand), ~650 pilots. EJA accounted for 31% of corporate jets ordered globally in 1997.
- **Competitive position.** EJA offers Boeing, Gulfstream, Falcon, Cessna, Raytheon — versus competitors owned by single manufacturers. "NetJets is like a physician who can recommend whatever medicine best fits the needs of each patient; our competitors, in contrast, are producers of a 'house' brand."
- **Buffett's personal usage:** Bought a 1/4 share of a Hawker 1000 (200 hours/year) four years earlier (introduced by Frank Rooney of H.H. Brown). Family flew 900 hours on 300 trips by 1998. Sold the Berkshire plane and switched all flying to NetJets.
- **Cross-Berkshire integration.** EJA's ~650 pilots are trained twice yearly at FlightSafety International, another Berkshire subsidiary.

## Stock-options accounting — Part 1

Reopened by the General Re cash-comp swap. Pro-forma 1997 compensation expense would have been $63M higher had General Re's stock-option plan been replaced with the equivalent cash plan a year earlier. The two plans are an economic wash but produce vastly different reported earnings.

Key claims:
- Options are a "huge and increasing expense at a great many corporations."
- Accounting principles "offer management a choice: Pay employees in one form and count the cost, or pay them in another form and ignore the cost." Choice is "lop-sided" for shareholders.
- The advertising analogy: if Berkshire paid the media for $190M of GEICO ads with ten-year at-the-market Berkshire options, no one would argue Berkshire bore no cost.
- Repricing makes employee options often more costly than public options, despite forfeiture and tax-deduction offsets.
- "Giving an employee a company car that can only be used for certain purposes diminishes its value to the employee, but does not in the least diminish its cost to the employer."
- Buffett's portfolio practice: subtract the cost of an option-issuing company's option grants from reported earnings; "frequently cut the reported per-share figures by 5%, with 10% not all that uncommon." This has caused outright sales and pass decisions.

Continues the canonical 1992 stock-options-as-expense essay (see [[berkshire-1992-letter]]); reasserted in the context of FASB's defeat by congressional lobbying.

## Earnings management — Part 2

The shift from accounting-as-fiction to manipulation-as-strategy:

- **Restructuring charges as "distortion du jour."** Lumping multi-year costs into one already-bad quarter to clean up past misrepresentations or seed future ones.
- **Golf-scorecard analogy.** Variant 1: open with a 140, then turn in four 80s, charging bad holes to the "discontinued" 140. Variant 2: defer bad holes into a fifth-round 140 "big bath."
- **Deal-time loss-reserve boost in P-C insurance.** Often coincides "uncanny" with deal inking — sets up future "earnings" as reserves are released. "Berkshire has kept entirely clear of these practices: … In all of our acquisitions, we have left the loss reserve figures exactly as we found them."
- **The CEO anecdote.** Friendly bank-merger discussion: "But won't that mean a huge charge, perhaps as much as $1 billion?" Reply: "We'll make it bigger than that — that's why we're doing the deal."
- **R.G. Associates tally.** 1,369 special charges in 1998, totaling $72.1B — measured against 1997 Fortune 500 earnings of $324B.
- **SEC and Arthur Levitt.** "Landmark speech last September" calling for an end to "earnings management." Buffett endorses Levitt's effort against "selective disclosure" and earnings manipulation.
- **The Berkshire principle.** "If we are to disappoint you, we would rather it be with our earnings than with our accounting."

Extends the [[institutional-imperative]] framework — the same systemic forces that produce "lemmings as a class" produce, in the late 1990s, accounting deception by otherwise high-grade managers.

## The "GEICO environment" pitch and owner-orientation

The 1998 letter formalizes Berkshire's value proposition to first-class CEOs of acquirable businesses. The three-part operating mandate — own 100%, sole family asset, no sale for a century — is the cleanest expression to date of the [[owner-orientation]] framework as applied to operating-business management (not just shareholder communication). Berkshire's permanent-capital shareholder base ("a majority of our shares are held by investors who expect to die still holding them") is the precondition that allows the structure to be credible. See [[acquisition-criteria]].

## Tax discussion

Berkshire and General Re combined paid (or will soon pay) $2.7B in 1998 federal income taxes — "all of the U.S. Government's expenses for more than a half-day." A 35% corporate rate applies to capital gains as to ordinary income (Berkshire's tax on a long-term capital gain is 75% higher than what an individual would pay). The 70% dividend-received deduction applies to most corporations; for insurance subsidiaries, the exclusion is 59.5%.
