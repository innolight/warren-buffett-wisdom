# Berkshire Hathaway 2006 Annual Letter

Record net-worth gain of $16.9 billion (18.4% per-share book-value growth) — a figure Buffett describes as the largest one-year gain in net worth in American corporate history, excluding merger-driven boosts. The year was materially aided by an exceptional insurance result: after hurricane seasons in 2004-05 inflicted heavy super-cat losses, 2006 saw no major catastrophes. Float reached $50.9 billion; underwriting profit was $3.838 billion — the best in Berkshire's 40-year insurance history.

## Key themes

- **GEICO's productivity miracle:** Between 2003 and 2006, policies grew 42% (5.7M to 8.1M) while employees fell 3.5% — a 47% productivity gain. Advertising spend rose to $631M (vs. $238M in 2003, $31M in 1995). The clearest articulation yet of the virtuous-circle low-cost compounding engine.
- **Float milestone and Equitas deal:** Float reached $50.9B and will grow by another $7B via the Equitas retroactive reinsurance contract. Detailed accounting of DCRA mechanics — how retroactive contracts produce intentional annual underwriting losses offset by investment income.
- **Derivatives nuance:** Buffett explicitly reconciles his "weapons of mass destruction" critique with his own selective use of 62 derivative contracts (managed personally, counterparty-free), arguing derivatives are sometimes mispriced like any security.
- **2-and-20 arithmetic:** Extends the 2005 Gotrocks essay with specific arithmetic: at 10% gross return, the manager retains 3.6 percentage points, leaving 6.4 for investors — while an index fund might return 15%.
- **Walter Schloss as EMT refutation:** 47-year partnership built on simple Graham methods, dramatically outperforming the S&P 500, cited as decisive evidence against efficient-market theory.
- **Newspaper moat erosion:** Extended essay on why newspaper monopoly economics — which made dominant papers "economic heaven" for a century — are now structurally collapsing. Buffalo News explicitly named as a declining-margin operation.
- **CEO/CIO succession structure:** Berkshire has three named CEO candidates but Buffett publicly declares it "not as well-prepared on the investment side." He announces a search for a younger chief investment officer — explicitly separate from CEO succession — and articulates the required traits: genetic risk-recognition, emotional stability, independence from institutional behavior. The Lou Simpson precedent (hired 1979, now only six years younger than Buffett) frames both what Berkshire needs and why the candidate must be substantially younger.
- **Berkshire as international "buyer of choice":** First non-US acquisition (ISCAR, $4B for 80%), sourced by a 1¼-page letter from Eitan Wertheimer. The pattern: sellers reject strategic buyers (dismantlement risk) and PE (debt and flip), leaving Berkshire.

## Notable quotes

> Between yearend 2003 and yearend 2006, the number of GEICO policies increased from 5.7 million to 8.1 million, a jump of 42%. Yet during that same period, the company's employees (measured on a full-time-equivalent basis) fell 3.5%. So productivity grew 47%. And GEICO didn't start fat.

> When we were due to close the purchase at Charlie's office, Jack was late. Finally arriving, he explained that he had been driving around looking for a parking meter with some unexpired time. That was a magic moment for me. I knew then that Jack was going to be my kind of manager.

> At the end of 2006, our float had grown to $50.9 billion, and we have since written a huge retroactive reinsurance contract with Equitas — which I will describe in the next section — that boosts float by another $7 billion.

> Derivatives, just like stocks and bonds, are sometimes wildly mispriced. For many years, accordingly, we have selectively written derivative contracts — few in number but sometimes for large dollar amounts. We currently have 62 contracts outstanding. I manage them personally, and they are free of counterparty credit risk.

> Following a strategy that involved no real risk — defined as permanent loss of capital — Walter [Schloss] produced results over his 47 partnership years that dramatically surpassed those of the S&P 500.

> Simply put, if cable and satellite broadcasting, as well as the internet, had come along first, newspapers as we know them probably would never have existed.

> A manager who achieves a gross return of 10% in a year will keep 3.6 percentage points — two points off the top plus 20% of the residual 8 points — leaving only 6.4 percentage points for his investors.
