# Berkshire Hathaway 2012 Annual Meeting

Held on May 5, 2012, at the CenturyLink Center in Omaha with roughly 18,000 attendees, the meeting opened under two shadows: Buffett had disclosed a prostate-cancer diagnosis days earlier (per contemporaneous press reports; he called it "a nonevent"), and the question of succession — whose name the board had agreed on but refused to disclose — was more urgent than ever. The broader backdrop was a recovering-but-still-fragile economy: IBM was Berkshire's newest disclosed mega-stake (bought quietly in the market in 2011), the Bank of America warrant deal had just been struck, the eurozone was in acute crisis, housing was still depressed, and the 1.1× book repurchase limit was brand new. The durable teaching across the day concentrates in four clusters: why risk management cannot be delegated or expressed in sigma; why Mr. Market's manic-depressive mispricing is the investor's structural advantage; why declining businesses are almost never worth their apparent cheapness; and why Berkshire's culture — decentralized autonomy plus centralized capital allocation plus a patient controlling shareholder — is genuinely hard to replicate and designed to outlast any single manager. This page keeps the wiki's two-layer convention: a distilled thematic top, then a comprehensive ★-marked Q&A digest as the home for detail.

## How to read the Q&A digest

- **★** marks a timeless, load-bearing exchange worth citing in the future; unmarked entries still carry investor value but are secondary or period-specific.
- Answers are attributed separately to **Buffett** and **Munger** — a Munger line appears only when he added a distinct lesson (he said "Nothing to add" to most).
- **M#/A#** map to the numbered items in the raw morning/afternoon transcript for traceability.

## Key themes

### The CEO must be the chief risk officer — sigma is a dangerous substitute for judgment

Buffett's clearest post-2008 position: the CEO must own risk personally, never delegate it to a committee armed with sigma charts. The principle found its sharpest expression in Munger's dismissal at M6: "The so-called 'Value at Risk' and the theories that outcomes in financial markets followed a Gaussian curve, invariably … was one of the dumbest ideas ever put forward." The problem is dispositional, not intellectual — the technicians who built these models were often brilliant, but they twisted every problem to fit the mathematical tool they'd learned. The corrective is not a better formula but a different disposition: think about worst cases constantly, add a large margin of safety, and never accept a situation where a tail event forces you off the field. Buffett's $20 billion cash floor is deliberately not derived from a model — "I would get very worried about somebody that walked in every morning and told us precisely how many dollars of cash we needed to be … secured at three sigma" — it is a structural guarantee of survivability. The lesson transfers: organizations that express risk in sigmas tend to accumulate the risk they cannot measure, while the practice of asking "what is the honest worst case" and then overdoing the cushion is cheaper than it sounds. See [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md), [model-risk](../concepts/model-risk.md). *(M6, A15)*

### Mr. Market is a "psychotic drunk" — use his mispricing, don't join it

The most extended Graham lesson in the meeting corpus: "in the market you're going to have a partner named 'Mr. Market,' and the beauty of him as your partner is that he's kind of a psychotic drunk … and your job is to remember that he's there to serve you and not to advise you." The stock market is "the most obliging, money-making place in the world" precisely because it offers the same price to buyer and seller every day across thousands of businesses, makes all kinds of mistakes, and you never have to do anything. The anti-pattern is to "start behaving like the drunken psychotic instead of the guy that's there to take advantage of it." Berkshire's stock has been cut roughly in half at least four times in its history — each time an occasion to buy, never a signal of a worse business. Buffett cites Graham Chapters 8 and 20 of *The Intelligent Investor* as "all you need to do to get rich in this world." See [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md), [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md). *(M24, M9)*

### Ignore macro headlines; the decision is always about business value

"Charlie and I, to my memory, in 53 years, I don't think we've ever had a discussion about buying a stock or a business … where we've talked about macro affairs." This is not bravado — it is a system: if you find a business you understand and like the price, you buy it, regardless of headlines, Fed policy, or Europe. The qualification Munger adds is the structural one: Berkshire kept liquid reserves at the 2008 bottom precisely because they didn't know whether it could get worse, and you can't play tomorrow if you go broke today. But within that survivability constraint, macro commentary is noise to filter, not a signal to trade on. "There's always going to be good and bad news out there, and which gets emphasized the most … depends on the moods of people or newspaper editors or whomever." See [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md). *(M25)*

### Declining businesses are almost never worth the apparent price

Buffett's most explicit statement on the cigar-butt approach: "generally speaking, it pays to stay away from declining businesses … the same amount of energy and intelligence brought to other types of businesses is just going to work out better." The meeting's own examples — textiles, trading stamps, department stores — illustrate both the danger and the irony: those three failures eventually compounded into billions because the capital was eventually redeployed, but the starting-position was a handicap, not a feature. Munger's addition sharpens it: "They're not worth nearly as much as growing businesses. But they can still be quite valuable if a lot of cash is going to come out of them." The practical rule for newspapers (which Berkshire was actively buying) is the exception that proves the rule: enter only when the price is low enough to absorb secular decline AND the local-community moat gives plausible longevity. See [cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses](../concepts/cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses.md), [media-economics](../concepts/media-economics.md). *(A5, M33)*

### Succession is the board's number-one obligation — the culture is the real continuity

Buffett's succession answer in 2012 is unusually detailed: the successor is identified (not named), "totally imbued with the culture," and will "in many ways … be better than I am." The more durable point is what the culture consists of: 260,000 people run with a short code and a decentralized design, managers keep their paint brushes and are never told to coordinate with siblings, Todd Combs and Ted Weschler are hired on character and intellectual integrity as much as track record. Munger's forecast: "the first 200 billion was hard, and the second 200 billion, with the momentum in place, is likely to be pretty easy compared to what's been accomplished in the past." The companies built to take Berkshire to $400 billion are already in the portfolio. See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md). *(A2, M6, A21)*

### Own your stock only when it is dramatically cheap — and cash is a guarantee, not an asset class

The 1.1× book repurchase limit is clarified here: it is a floor of certainty ("we know we're making significant money for remaining shareholders"), not a precise estimate of intrinsic value ("we know it's significantly above 110"). Berkshire would "buy very aggressively" at that price as long as cash doesn't fall below $20 billion. The operating principle is absolute: "we are never going to risk what we have and need for what we don't have and don't need." The $20 billion is not a model output — it is the amount at which Buffett can sleep soundly if a nuclear bomb hits the Federal Reserve tomorrow. Companies buying back stock at any price, for ego, are doing "idiotic" things; repurchasing only makes sense when it increases per-share value the day it is done. See [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md), [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md). *(M9, M10, A15)*

### Newspapers survive only if they own local primacy — every other reason to subscribe is gone

Buffett's most systematic diagnosis of the newspaper business: papers used to be primary across "dozens and dozens" of areas of reader interest (apartments, jobs, sports scores, stock prices, classifieds). The internet stripped every one of those advantages, leaving only one viable moat — hyper-local coverage of things that only the local paper knows: "what's going on in my city, local sports, my neighbors … things about my local institutions." The business will survive only in communities where residents have genuine attachment to place, where the paper covers those things better than anyone else, and where the paper does not give its product away free online. Berkshire's purchase of the Omaha World-Herald, Buffalo News, and a string of Nebraska papers was a conscious, discounted bet on local-primacy businesses purchased at secular-decline prices — explicitly not a return to the franchise model. *Supersession flag:* Berkshire sold its entire newspaper portfolio to Lee Enterprises in January 2020 for a nominal $140 million plus assumption of debt, confirming the secular decline thesis. See [media-economics](../concepts/media-economics.md). *(M33)*

## Q&A digest — Morning session

**M3 · Q1 earnings: GEICO accounting change, non-residential construction strong.** *Buffett:* all five largest non-reinsurance subsidiaries had record earnings last year, aggregating over $9 billion pretax; expects over $10 billion this year. GEICO had a "terrific first quarter" — nearly 9-point underwriting margin — but a mandated accounting change (deferred policy acquisition cost) reduced reported earnings by $250 million pretax; underlying figures are better. Housing-related businesses remain weak. See [geico](../entities/geico.md).

**★ M6 · The CEO must be the chief risk officer — and sigma-based models are dangerous.** *Buffett:* "I do believe that the CEO of any large, particularly financial-related, company should … be the chief risk officer. It's not something to be delegated." He has watched institutions delegate it to risk committees that reported with charts and "how many sigmas were involved … and the place was just ripe for real trouble." His named successor already "can do a lot of things much, much better that I can do." On the Bank of America warrant: he had never spoken to Brian Moynihan before but called with a fully-formed proposal; Berkshire's ability to "commit, and have the other person know your commitment is good for very large sums" is a structural advantage that will survive him. *Munger:* "Value at Risk and the theories that outcomes in financial markets followed a Gaussian curve, invariably … was one of the dumbest ideas ever put forward." See [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md), [model-risk](../concepts/model-risk.md), [berkshire-hathaway](../entities/berkshire-hathaway.md).

**M7 · Reserve conservatively — and accept that some contracts will be worse than expected.** *Buffett:* the Swiss Re mortality book (American business written pre-2004) came in far above expectations; Berkshire set reserves to worst-case present value. The overriding principle across GEICO, Gen Re, and reinsurance: "we hope, and our plan is, to reserve conservatively." *Munger:* "There's always going to be some contract where the results are worse than we expected. Why would anybody buy our insurance if that weren't the case?" See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md), [general-re](../entities/general-re.md).

**M8 · "Amazing how little influence we've had" — decentralized ownership means pushing on a noodle.** *Munger:* "We have found that we have a lot of control — it's kind of like controlling affairs by pushing on a noodle. It's amazing how little influence we've had when we had 20 percent of the stock." Berkshire talks to the CEOs of its four largest investees (worth ~$50 billion in total) at most twice a year and gives no advice. "If we thought that the success of our investment depended upon them following our advice, we'd go onto something else." See [passive-ownership-advantage](../concepts/passive-ownership-advantage.md).

**★ M9 · No warning when stock is overvalued — but do nothing to imply it's cheap.** *Buffett:* shareholders want a warning when Berkshire is overpriced; "I don't see how we would do it" — announcing it simultaneously to all shareholders would be unprecedented, and the reaction unpredictable. The one time Berkshire sold stock (mid-1990s B-share offering), they put on the cover of the prospectus that "neither Charlie nor I would buy the stock at the price, nor would we recommend that our family did it." The current 1.1× book limit means: "we know it's significantly above 110." "We are not selling it. We are not saying that 111 percent or 112 percent is intrinsic business value." See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md), [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md).

**★ M10 · Buy back only at a "dramatically" undervalued price — and keep $20 billion available always.** *Buffett:* the 1.1× book threshold is where Berkshire is "very comfortable" — "we wanted to be dramatically — or very significantly — undervalued to do buybacks." At 110% of book, "we know we're making significant money for remaining shareholders." Would buy "very aggressively" if the chance arose, as long as cash does not fall below $20 billion. *Munger:* "Some people buy their own stock back regardless of price. That's not our system." *Buffett:* "We think a lot of the share repurchases are idiotic … it's for ego … we will only do it for one reason, and that's to increase the per-share value the day after we've done it." See [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md), [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md).

**M11 · U.S. banks "in far, far, far better shape" than European.** *Buffett:* American banks have taken most abnormal losses, recapitalized substantially, have "liquidity coming out their ears." European banks relied more on wholesale funding that "can run pretty fast" — the ECB's trillion-euro LTRO (three years at 1 percent) replaced funding that was fleeing. European banks still need more capital. The structural difference: U.S. has a federal union with a central bank that can say "we'll do whatever it takes" and be believed; "17 countries that have surrendered their sovereignty, as far as their currency is concerned" cannot. See [wells-fargo](../entities/wells-fargo.md), [american-tailwind](../concepts/american-tailwind.md).

**M12 · "Idiotic" to consume scarce natural gas reserves while cheaper coal goes unburned.** *Munger:* the 50-to-1 oil-to-gas price ratio is extraordinary and the market response — burning gas instead of coal — is wrong: "We are using up a precious resource, which we need to create fertilizer and so forth, and sparing a resource which is precious but not as precious." Gas reserves just discovered are "the most precious things we could leave our descendants." The conventional free-market defense — if it happens in a free market it must be OK — is not his view "with 100 percent accuracy." See [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md).

**M13 · GEICO telematics: "I do not see anything that threatens GEICO."** *Buffett:* Progressive leads in driver-tracking technology but GEICO has 51 predictor variables; if a better predictive tool emerges, they will adopt it. GEICO's first quarter: nearly 300,000 new policies in February alone. GEICO is carried at roughly $1 billion over tangible book value but is worth perhaps $15 billion more than that. "We wouldn't sell it at all, but that would not tempt us in the least." See [geico](../entities/geico.md).

**★ M14 · Business schools teach investment "nonsense" — only two courses needed.** *Buffett:* "I have a course on how to value a business, and I have a course on how to think about markets … if people grasped the basic principles in those two courses that would be far better off than if they were exposed to a lot of things like modern portfolio theory or option pricing." The fads (VaR, Black-Scholes for long-term options) spread because going against "the revealed wisdom of your elders can be very dangerous to your career path at major business schools." "The high priests in any particular arena … have to convince the laity that the priests have to be listened to." *Munger:* Black-Scholes for long-dated options "is not the optimal way to price it … they want some kind of a standardized solution that requires them not to think too hard." See [model-risk](../concepts/model-risk.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**M16 · Cat pricing: write if rate passes a "fairly negative hypothesis."** *Buffett:* it is very hard to separate random from trend in earthquakes and hurricanes. Berkshire's post-Japan/NZ/Thailand approach: assume the data from the recent loss years reflects something real even if you can't extrapolate; "if we think we're getting a rate that … if a fairly negative hypothesis would indicate — then we move ahead." Berkshire has propositions out for as much as $10 billion of coverage in the Pacific; will only write it at the right price and if it does not correlate with other exposures. See [super-cat-insurance](../concepts/super-cat-insurance.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**M17 · Wind projects "would not make sense" without the federal subsidy — but Berkshire has a tax-capacity edge.** *Buffett and Greg Abel:* the 2.2-cent-per-kilowatt-hour subsidy makes Iowa wind work; absent it, none of MidAmerican's 3,000 megawatts of wind capacity would have been built. Solar requires a 30% construction cost recovery and a long-term PPA with Pacific Gas & Electric. The structural Berkshire advantage: roughly 80% of U.S. utilities cannot use tax credits because they have already wiped out taxable income via bonus depreciation; Berkshire, "perhaps 2 percent of all U.S. corporate taxes," can deploy those credits at full dollar-for-dollar value. See [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md).

**M19 · Above $20 billion, acquisitions become a stretch — especially without stock.** *Buffett:* recently looked at a $22–23 billion deal and would have done it — "there was probably about 22 billion" — but wouldn't push cash below $20 billion; would have sold securities he didn't want to sell. "If that had been 40 billion, I don't think I would have wanted to peel off 25 billion." Won't use stock at all; used it for Burlington at ~30% and "we felt that it was a mistake." *Munger:* "It's hard to imagine, but it could conceivably happen." See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), [stock-as-acquisition-currency](../concepts/stock-as-acquisition-currency.md).

**M21 · Prostate cancer: "a nonevent."** *Buffett:* stage-1 diagnosis; no hospitalization required, no days off work, treatment at a center two minutes from the office for two months. Survival rate "99½ percent for 10 years." *Munger:* "I rather resent all this attention and sympathy Warren is getting … I probably have more prostate cancer than he does. I don't know because I don't let them test for it." Context: the cancer announcement amplified the succession question that dominates the meeting's governance section.

**M22 · Annuity runoff books at near risk-free cost only.** *Buffett:* Berkshire would take on annuity runoff books "but not like we're going to assume anything much better than the risk-free rate in making a bid." Won't accept 150bp above Treasuries for long-term liabilities. The most attractive float is property-casualty at an underwriting profit — "if we can write property-casualty business at an underwriting profit and get it for nothing." Has done small UK annuity books and would do more at the right terms. See [float](../concepts/float.md).

**★ M23 · Career advice: get into buying-to-keep businesses as fast as possible.** *Buffett:* "I would do very much what I have done in life, except I'd try to do it a little earlier." Would build an audited partnership record, attract capital, then "get into something much more interesting, which would be buying businesses to keep." Explicitly contrasts private equity — "buying businesses to sell" — with Berkshire's model of wanting relationships "for keeps." "That's been enormously satisfying. But it takes some capital to get into that business." *Munger:* "Nothing to add."

**★ M24 · Mr. Market is a "psychotic drunk" — take advantage of his mistakes.** *Buffett:* "the beauty of him as your partner is that he's kind of a psychotic drunk … and he will do very weird things over time and your job is to remember that he's there to serve you and not to advise you." Thousands of businesses priced every day at the same buy and sell price with lots of information available — "you don't have to do anything." Berkshire stock has been cut roughly in half at least four times in its history; "stocks get mispriced and Berkshire has been no exception." "In the next 20 years, Berkshire will someday be significantly overvalued, and at some points significantly undervalued." The correct response: make buy and sell decisions on business value, not on market sentiment. "Chapters 8 and 20 [of *The Intelligent Investor*] … you'll get rich in this world." *Munger:* "It's enormously more constructive … we got rich enough so we bought businesses and stocks to hold instead of to resell." See [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md), [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

**★ M25 · Macro and headlines: "we don't have any discussions about macro factors."** *Buffett:* "Charlie and I … in 53 years … I don't think we've ever had a discussion about buying a stock or a business … where we've talked about macro affairs … If we find a business that we think we understand and we like the price at which it's being offered, we buy it. And it doesn't make any difference what the headlines are, it doesn't make any difference what the Federal Reserve is doing … We look to value, and we don't look to headlines at all." *Munger:* "we did keep liquid reserves at the bottom of the panic that, if we'd known it was not going to get any worse, we would have spent, but we didn't know that." *Buffett:* "We know what we don't know. We know we don't want to go broke." See [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md), [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md).

**M26 · Big subsidiaries have done well — mistakes were in the purchasing, not the managing.** *Buffett:* BNSF, GEICO (2% → ~10% market share), MidAmerican (~$34 → ~$250/share appraisal), ISCAR — all dramatically more valuable than at purchase. Gen Re took years but is now "a terrific asset." Ajit Jain "created something from nothing that's worth tens of billions of dollars." *Munger:* "We have 80 percent or so of our businesses, by value, at least, increased their market strength." *Buffett:* "The mistakes have been made in the purchasing … where I misgauged the competitive position of the business. It isn't because of the faults of management." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md).

**★ M27 · Derivatives book will be small — collateral exposure is the dealbreaker.** *Buffett:* "I don't think we'll ever have much of a derivatives book … not going to be a huge factor at Berkshire." Todd Combs and Ted Weschler are unlikely to use derivatives much, though not restricted. The operative rule: "we don't know what tomorrow morning will bring," so any obligation requiring cash on short notice is categorically off-limits. "A requirement to collateralize things means that you are putting yourself in a position where you may have to come up with some cash tomorrow morning, and we're never going to do that on any significant scale." *Munger:* "We wouldn't have entered if we'd had to sign normal contracts … by the time that has all run off, we will have made at least $10 billion, maybe a lot more." See [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md), [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md).

**★ M28 · Valuation: 9–10× pretax for operating businesses; EBITDA is "nonsense."** *Buffett:* "Under today's conditions, I would love to buy those [operating businesses with good characteristics] at certainly nine times pretax earnings, maybe 10 times pretax earnings. I'm not talking about EBITDA … which is nonsense. I'm talking about regular pretax earnings." *Munger:* "When you used the word EBITDA, I thought to myself, I don't even like hearing the word. There's so much nutcase thinking involving EBITDA. Earnings before what really counts in costs." *Buffett:* "We prefer EBE, which is earnings before everything." They also confirm differential insurance valuation: GEICO is worth significantly more than net worth plus float (growth + underwriting margin expected); not true of all insurers. See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md).

**★ M29 · Gold "arouses passions" but productive assets will win over any long period.** *Buffett:* since Berkshire was bought ($15/share), gold has gone from $20 to $1,600 — a fine return, but Berkshire went from $15 to $120,000. "The one thing I would bet my life on, essentially, is over a 50-year period, not only will Berkshire do considerably better than gold, but common stocks as a group will do better than gold, and probably farmland will do better than gold." An ounce of gold in 100 years is still an ounce; 100 acres of farmland in 100 years has also produced 100 crop years. *Munger:* "I have never had the slightest interest in owning gold. It's a much better life to work with businesses and people engaged in business." See [investment-categories](../concepts/investment-categories.md).

**M30 · "My best ideas are all in Berkshire."** *Buffett:* his personal J.P. Morgan purchase is a "second choice" — Berkshire is buying Wells Fargo, which he likes better, so he bought JPM personally with money that "I've got some money around." "My best ideas are all in Berkshire. That I can promise you." *Munger:* "the Munger family is in two or three things only. Diversification is … not something I have practically no interest in, except as it happens automatically in a big place like Berkshire." See [concentration-vs-diversification](../concepts/concentration-vs-diversification.md).

**M31 · BNSF inside National Indemnity: capital flexibility at the holding-company level.** *Buffett:* money in life companies is less useful than in property-casualty because of regulatory constraints; "the ultimate flexibility" is at the holding company with its current ~$10 billion. Placing BNSF inside National Indemnity gives a huge non-insurance asset to one of the most strongly capitalized insurers in the world — "a property-casualty company that has an asset in it that, unrelated to insurance, will probably make $5 billion pretax or more … like having a royalty." *Munger:* "nobody else has it … They wouldn't let us do it if we weren't so strong." See [national-indemnity](../entities/national-indemnity.md), [bnsf](../entities/bnsf.md).

**M32 · Stock price goal: as close to intrinsic business value as possible, not maximized.** *Buffett:* "we would obviously prefer to have our stock sell at exactly intrinsic business value … if it's over intrinsic business value, and we could use it as part of a consideration for buying something else at intrinsic business value … we would like that situation." A dividend would be paying out money worth $1.10 inside Berkshire at $1 outside — "not very attractive." The only goal is running the business well and communicating honestly; over time, the price averages close to value. See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md), [dividend-policy](../concepts/dividend-policy.md).

**★ M33 · Newspapers: local primacy is the only surviving moat.** *Buffett:* papers had "dozens and dozens" of areas of primacy 30–40 years ago (apartments, jobs, sports scores, stock prices, classified). The internet stripped every one. "Newspapers have to be primary about something of interest to a significant percentage of the people that live within their distribution area." That something is almost always hyper-local: "my city, local sports, my neighbors … things about my local institutions." Three problems: distribution costs; most papers give away online what they charge for in print ("I don't know of any business plan that has sustained itself for a long time … that has charged significantly in one version and offers the same version free"); and they must stop giving the product away online. Berkshire will buy more papers in communities with "a sense of community." *Munger:* "Some of these newspapers, we hope, will be the same kind of investments [as World Book] — they're not going to be our great lollapaloozas." *Supersession flag:* Berkshire sold its entire newspaper portfolio to Lee Enterprises in January 2020 for a nominal sum, confirming the secular decline predicted here. See [media-economics](../concepts/media-economics.md).

**M34 · Amazon "almost sure to hurt a lot of businesses a lot" — not Nebraska Furniture Mart.** *Buffett:* Amazon is "a powerhouse" — "very hard to find people who have done business with Amazon that are unhappy." Threatens many retailers but not the Nebraska Furniture Mart (which did over $6 million on a Tuesday during meeting week and is expanding to Dallas on 433 acres). GEICO was affected by the internet and almost missed it; ultimately the internet was a huge benefit. *Munger:* "I think it's terrible for most retailers. Not slightly terrible, really terrible." See [nebraska-furniture-mart](../entities/nebraska-furniture-mart.md).

**★ M35 · "Almost impossible" to copy Berkshire — took a long time, a controlling shareholder, and consistency.** *Buffett:* Berkshire's model requires: very long time; consistency; a culture that doesn't bow to Wall Street fads; a controlling shareholder that allowed management to write 13–14 principles and stick with them; and the accumulated reputation that causes sellers with "large private businesses" to think of Berkshire first and "not think of anybody second." "We don't do well buying businesses at auction … The big private acquisitions are going to come to Berkshire because they want to come to Berkshire." *Munger:* "Not only do I think other people will have a hard time copying it effectively, I think if Warren went back to being 30 years of age with a modest amount of capital and not much else, he'd have a hell of a time doing it again, too." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**M36 · Proxy voting: almost never vote against management.** *Buffett:* in ~50 years, voted against management "a couple of times" — stock option expensing, an egregious grant. General rule: they own businesses they generally like, with managements they generally like, who won't agree with them 100 percent. "We are not in the business of trying to change people … It's like marrying somebody to change them. It just doesn't work very well." *Munger:* "Doesn't work very well with children, either."

**M39 · Walmart Mexico bribery: won't change fundamental earning power.** *Buffett:* "I do not see — I mean, it's a huge diversion of management time and it's costly … but I don't think the earning power of Walmart five years from now will be materially affected by the outcome of this situation." On Berkshire's own ethics management with 270,000 employees: "I will guarantee you at least 20 people are doing something wrong … What you worry about is that it's material and nothing gets done about it, and that you act fast if you hear about something." See [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

**M40 · Hedge-fund bet: S&P trails the funds after four years — but the zero-coupon bond won.** *Buffett:* four years into the 10-year S&P-vs.-five-funds-of-funds bet, the hedge funds lead (as expected in a down year) but the S&P has won the last three. The zero-coupon bond that will fund the $1 million prize has "performed magnificently, much better than Berkshire" — Berkshire has petitioned to switch it to Berkshire stock. "The best thing you could have done was ignored both of us and just looked at where we were putting the money." See [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md).

## Q&A digest — Afternoon session

**A1 · Gen Re: "a major fix-up operation" — culture change takes years.** *Buffett:* Gen Re "may … have been off the track when we bought it, and I didn't spot it." Volume fell dramatically because they had been prioritizing growth and "accommodation business" over underwriting profit. Joe Brandon changed it to 100% focus on underwriting margin, Tad Montross has maintained discipline. Life business kept growing. "There was a major change that had to be made in the culture at Gen Re. And you don't make that overnight, and you don't keep a lot of the business … that you got through a wrong culture when you do straighten it out." *Munger:* "It was a major fix-up operation, but we finally got it done." *Buffett:* "We don't go looking for those, though." See [general-re](../entities/general-re.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**★ A2 · Post-Buffett culture: the succession candidate already has it embedded.** *Buffett:* "I virtually know — that the successor we have in mind … has got the culture as deeply embedded in as I do." Managers will stay not for money (most could retire) but because "they love the kind of environment in which they exist." A hostile takeover is extraordinarily unlikely: size, plus the Buffett family's likely ~20% of votes for a decade after his death, plus A-shares converting to B. "In many ways he'll be better than I am." *Munger:* "the businesses are in place to take it to 400 billion." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

**★ A3 · Capital-consuming businesses OK if they earn ~12% on incremental capital.** *Buffett:* "cash-consuming businesses, by their nature, are unattractive unless the cash they consume gets to earn a reasonable return." Utilities can be expected to earn ~12% on retained capital — "quite satisfactory." BNSF capex will exceed depreciation every year; returns look "pretty darn good." Contrast: a capital-intensive business earning only its cost of capital is "like staying alive with that money … terrible situation." Berkshire will be "very happy if we can earn 12 percent or something like that on equity, particularly when some of that capital … is generated by float, which doesn't cost us anything." *Munger:* "I think it's going to work pretty well." See [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md), [return-on-equity-yardstick](../concepts/return-on-equity-yardstick.md).

**A4 · Float unlikely to grow much — but cost should stay negative.** *Buffett:* the retroactive contracts naturally run off; GEICO's float will grow with premiums; Ajit's book is "a melting ice cube." Was wrong when he thought float at $40B wouldn't grow — it's now $70B. "If I had to bet on whether float will be higher or lower five years from now, I probably would bet just a slight bit higher." But the cost should remain negative even if float stagnates: "even if interest rates go up to 4 or 5 or 6 or 7 percent … I think we may very well be able to do it." *Munger:* Ajit would continue to work miracles, and Matt Rose at BNSF "will carry some of the future freight." *Supersession note:* float reached $147 billion by 2023, far exceeding this cautious forecast. See [float](../concepts/float.md), [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md).

**★ A5 · Stay away from declining businesses — "same energy and intelligence" produces better results elsewhere.** *Buffett:* "generally speaking, it pays to stay away from declining businesses … the same amount of energy and intelligence brought to other types of businesses is just going to work out better." Our own history with textiles, trading stamps, department stores: all failed, yet the capital redeployed from them became billions. "We are not looking for an opportunity to do it again." The exception is a declining business purchased so cheaply, with enough residual cash flow, that the math works — newspapers only qualify if they still own local primacy. *Munger:* "They're not worth nearly as much as growing businesses. But they can still be quite valuable if a lot of cash is going to come out of them." See [cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses](../concepts/cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses.md).

**★ A6 · Never buy IPOs — the seller picks the time; large commissions are automatic red flags.** *Buffett:* "I can't recall any time in the last 30 years, at least, that we've bought a new issue." The logic is airtight: with thousands of businesses available at market prices, "the idea that somebody is bringing something to market today, a seller who has a choice of when to come to market, and that that security … is going to be the single cheapest thing to buy out of thousands and thousands and thousands of businesses in the world is nonsense." *Munger:* "And then when it carries a 7 percent commission, or higher. It's ridiculous … If it's got a really large commission in it, don't read it." What to look at instead: what other smart investors are buying — "not a crazy search method as a sorting device for opportunities to consider." See [investment-friction](../concepts/investment-friction.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**A9 · Apple and Google: "extraordinary companies" but "we have the reverse of an edge."** *Buffett:* "I would not be at all surprised to see them be worth a lot more money 10 years from now, but I wouldn't want to buy either one of them … I do not get to the level of conviction that would cause me to buy them. But I sure as hell wouldn't short them, either." *Munger:* "other people will always understand those two companies better than we do. We have the reverse of an edge." IBM is easier to understand for them; the risk of being "way wrong" on IBM is lower even if Apple/Google may well outperform. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ A10 · BNSF freight economics: 500 miles per gallon of diesel — an irreplaceable asset.** *Buffett:* "We can move a ton of product 500 miles on one gallon of diesel, and that may be three times or so as efficient as trucking." Railroads carry ~42% of all intercity traffic and "I don't think our percentage is going to go down … no matter what the politics may be … it's too compelling to move heavy traffic long distances over steel rail." BNSF will spend $3.9 billion in 2012, paying its own way entirely — the Federal Government will not write a check. *Munger:* BNSF was "enormously helped" when double-stack containerization became possible and when North Dakota oil had no pipelines; will get some bad breaks too, but "averaged out, it's a terrific business with terrific management." See [bnsf](../entities/bnsf.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**A12 · The book-value S&P comparison understates Berkshire's actual advantage.** *Buffett:* the shareholder who says the table is apples-to-oranges is right — but the alternative calculation favors Berkshire more, not less. "Our gain would be probably at least … 35 or so percent higher in aggregate over the time" if both are compared on market value (because Berkshire started at a discount and ended at a premium). "You've been criticized for making yourself look worse." *Munger:* "It's all right. You can bear it." See [berkshire-hathaway](../entities/berkshire-hathaway.md), [intrinsic-value-vs-book-value](../concepts/intrinsic-value-vs-book-value.md).

**★ A15 · Risk management: no magic formula — worst-case thinking plus a large margin of safety.** *Buffett:* the $20 billion cash floor is not the "magic number" — "there is no magic number." Would be alarmed by "somebody that walked in every morning and told us precisely how many dollars of cash we needed to be … secured at three sigma." Organizations with dozens of people with advanced mathematical training making daily computations "don't really get at the problem" — "and they were a lot smarter than we were." He put seven historical financial disasters on his office wall in 1962 for $1 each — including the 1901 Northern Pacific Corner, where a stock went from $170 to $1,000 in one day, collapsing every other stock through margin calls on shorts. A brewer in Troy, New York committed suicide by diving into a vat of hot beer after a margin call. "Life in financial markets has got no relation to sigmas." The correct approach: "think about worst cases all the time, and then we add on a big margin of safety, and we don't want to go back to go." "In 99 years out of 100, your returns will probably be penalized by us being excessively conservative, and one year out of a hundred, we'll survive when some other people won't." *Munger:* "'To a man with a hammer, every problem looks pretty much like a nail.' They've learned these techniques and they just twist the problem so they fit the solution." Business schools have since improved: "they now have taken the Gaussian curve and they've just changed its shape … it's a better curve now, even though it's less precise." At Salomon: "Warren and I, in the Salomon meetings, would look over at one another and roll our eyes when the risk control people were talking." *Buffett:* "They talk about fat tails, but they still don't know how fat to make them." See [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md), [model-risk](../concepts/model-risk.md).

**A16 · Insurance-culture discipline: "cheerful about losing volume."** *Buffett:* "We don't measure ourselves in any way by size, except by the growth in value over time." *Munger:* "I don't think there's another large insurance operation in the world that is more cheerful about losing volume than we are." Every underwriting decision is made independently of prior volume commitments; when the Swiss Re quota-share ended, it changed nothing about what Berkshire would write next. See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**A17 · Fannie and Freddie: "left the tracks" when the profit motive overcame the housing mission.** *Buffett:* "gradually the profit mission sort of overcame the housing mission. Congress hadn't sorted that out yet." Fannie and Freddie can stay in conservatorship indefinitely until a political resolution both parties accept — "a ways off." *Munger:* Canada kept "a more responsible real estate lending system" and had almost no trouble. "We departed completely from sanity and decency and morality in mortgage lending in the United States." Greenspan "overdosed on Ayn Rand … He thought if an ax murder happened in a free market, it was probably all for the best." "It's the duty of the government to step on crazy market booms." See [american-tailwind](../concepts/american-tailwind.md).

**★ A18 · Todd Combs and Ted Weschler: structure and compensation.** *Buffett:* each earns $1 million salary plus 10% of the amount by which their portfolio beats the S&P, calculated on a three-year rolling basis (prevents seesawing). 80% based on own performance, 20% on the partner's — built-in incentive to share ideas. Running $2.75 billion each (up from $1.75B at year-end), through their own brokers, with Buffett only wanting to know when they enter a new name (conflict check). "We hit a home run with both of them. We got better than we deserved." *Munger:* "at least 90 percent of the investment management business of the United States would starve to death on our formula … they'll be terrific for the long pull for Berkshire." *Buffett:* Todd, who ran a hedge fund before, now pays taxes at 35%+ vs. the carried-interest 15% he would have paid; Berkshire compensates with a "free Coke machine." See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).

**A21 · Manager autonomy: "painting their own painting" — Berkshire is a "negative art."** *Buffett:* "we get the opportunity to paint our own painting every day, and we love painting that painting, and it's a painting that will never be finished." Gives the same to managers: "we give them the paint brush, let them keep the paint brush, and we don't go around and tell them to use more red paint than blue paint." Does not tell Clayton Homes to buy carpet from Shaw or paint from Benjamin Moore — "any gains we would get from doing that … I think would be far offset by the change in the feeling of the manager as to whether they're really running their own business." "Berkshire is a negative art in that way. We focus on not messing up something that's already good." *Munger:* "We don't have any standard formulas … every incentive arrangement with a key executive is different from every other." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md). (Raw A13 — "don't tell Clayton to buy from Shaw" — is folded here; the subsidiary-autonomy exchange draws from the same section.)

**A22 · Modest GDP growth creates "staggering" results over time.** *Buffett:* 2.5% real GDP growth with 1% population growth means quadrupling real GDP per capita every century. "In my lifetime, the real GDP per capita has increased six-for-one." Doesn't need 4% to be satisfied. *Munger:* "I think 1 percent per capita in real growth would be a sensational result" for a mature economy with a large social safety net and rising global competition. Even 1% means each generation lives 20%+ better than their parents. See [american-tailwind](../concepts/american-tailwind.md).

**A24 · Short-term stock price worry: "Mr. Market" will treat shareholders fine over time.** *Munger:* "I don't think you're really all that welcome in this room if the short-term orientation is what turns you on." Berkshire's intrinsic value per share has grown while the stock price has stalled — this just means the margin of safety has increased. The business goes on regardless of what the stock does. See [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md), [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**★ A25 · Dividend policy: only when we can't compound above a dollar.** *Buffett:* "by and large, we feel … that we can create more than a dollar of present value by investing" every retained dollar — proven over 47 years. A shareholder who needs income is better off selling 2% of their Berkshire each year than receiving a 2% dividend, "because they would have more money working per share in Berkshire." "The day that Berkshire declares a dividend, the stock will go down … it should go down — because it's an admission … that a compounding machine has lost its ability to continue." Paid 10 cents a share in the 1960s — "a big mistake … we won't repeat that." *Munger:* "the dividends will come in due course because eventually we'll find it difficult to multiply the rabbits, but we hope that that evil day is delayed." MidAmerican alone may deploy $100 billion of capital over the next decade. See [dividend-policy](../concepts/dividend-policy.md), [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md).

**★ A26 · Continuous learning made the record — the investment philosophy was set at 19.** *Munger:* "Berkshire's record would have been terrible compared to the way it turned out if Warren hadn't kept learning and learning and learning all the way … Each decade, to make the record decent, he had to learn to do some things he didn't know how to do at the start of the decade." *Buffett:* "I haven't learned more about a basic investment philosophy. I got that when I was 19 and I still [have it]. I think I've learned more about people over the years." See [warren-buffett](../people/warren-buffett.md), [charlie-munger](../people/charlie-munger.md).

**★ A27 · Learn from other people's mistakes — financial history is an education in human folly.** *Munger:* "what you've done … is to learn a lot from other people's mistakes. That is really a much more pleasant way to learn hard lessons. And we have really worked at that over the years … constant study of other people's disasters and other people's errors has helped us enormously." *Buffett:* "when you look at the folly of humans — I've focused on the folly in the financial area — just the financial area will give you plenty of material if you like to be a follower of folly … 2008 was a good example of that." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ A28 · "We sort of buy barriers; we don't build them."** *Munger:* on how to build barriers to entry in industries with few — "We sort of buy barriers; we don't build them." *Buffett:* "It's very — there are some industries that are just never going to have barriers to entry." Great barriers (Coke) are worth billions, but "our great brands we bought, we didn't create." Alternative: buy at a huge discount from replacement cost something that is essential and physically irreplaceable — BNSF's track would cost three to six times its market value to duplicate. *Munger:* "We have found in a long life that one competitor is frequently enough to ruin a business." See [moat](../concepts/moat.md), [franchise-vs-business](../concepts/franchise-vs-business.md).

**A30 · Float value is compressed by low rates — but won't last.** *Buffett:* "we're essentially getting nothing" on $36–37 billion of cash; "our earning power today is being affected by current Fed policies … that is not going to be a normal rate for many, many — for over the longer term." Past peak: Ajit would generate one-of-a-kind float and Buffett would deploy it at 20–30% before giving it back. The current low-rate environment depresses normalized earning power; when rates normalize, the $70B float at zero cost will become enormously valuable. *Munger:* "it doesn't have to" recur on that scale to still matter. See [float](../concepts/float.md), [bonds-and-inflation](../concepts/bonds-and-inflation.md).

**A33 · Sovereign debt and fiscal virtue: avoid medium- and long-term government bonds.** *Buffett:* "at these rates, I would totally avoid buying medium-term or long-term government bonds." Sovereigns have defaulted many times in history — not the unique safe haven advertised. The U.S. is running a deficit of 8–9% of GDP, which is "huge fiscal stimulus by definition." Both parties know the endpoint (~19% revenue, ~21% spending as % of GDP) but neither will go first. *Munger:* "It is very hard to know how much of this Keynesian stuff will work after you've lost all your fiscal virtue … once you've lost a lot of fiscal virtue … it's all counterproductive, and you're heading for calamity." Recommends spending any deficit money on infrastructure you know you'll need rather than "throwing it off the end of trains." See [bonds-and-inflation](../concepts/bonds-and-inflation.md).

## Connections to other sources

- The Mr. Market framing (M24) is the most elaborate live restatement since [berkshire-1994-meeting](berkshire-1994-meeting.md) M27/A5; here Buffett quotes Graham Chapters 8 and 20 explicitly as "all you need to do to get rich in this world" — a stronger endorsement than in prior meetings.
- The prostate-cancer disclosure (M21) and the succession discussion (M6, A2) are the 2012 sequel to the Sokol/Lubrizol governance crisis in [berkshire-2011-meeting](berkshire-2011-meeting.md) — both meetings center on the post-Buffett question, but 2012 arrives with a named (undisclosed) answer.
- The gold discussion (M29) reinforces the three-category taxonomy from [berkshire-2011-meeting](berkshire-2011-meeting.md) M16 and the 2011 letter; here Buffett adds the 1967 starting comparison ($15 Berkshire vs. $20 gold → $120,000 vs. $1,600) as a concrete long-run rebuttal. *Supersession:* Berkshire briefly held Barrick Gold shares in Q3 2020 before selling in Q1 2021.
- The Combs/Weschler compensation structure (A18) — the first detailed public disclosure of the formula — extends the incentive-compensation reasoning from the annual letters; the 80/20 collaborative feature is more sophisticated than the Lou Simpson model it builds on.
- The newspaper thesis (M33) anticipates what the 2012 and subsequent letters would elaborate: Berkshire was in the middle of buying a string of Nebraska community papers at the time; the "local primacy is the only moat" argument is the explicit thesis behind those purchases, later vindicated by the 2020 sale to Lee Enterprises.
- The BNSF-in-National-Indemnity structure (M31) is the Berkshire-specific form of the float-as-cheap-leverage insight running from the earliest float discussions; here it is presented as a regulatory and competitive advantage no one else can replicate.
- The "almost impossible to copy Berkshire" argument (M35) extends the conglomerate-model reasoning from [berkshire-2011-meeting](berkshire-2011-meeting.md) A18 and [berkshire-2010-meeting](berkshire-2010-meeting.md), adding the specific requirement of a long-tenure controlling shareholder who can resist Wall Street fads as the structural irreplicability.
