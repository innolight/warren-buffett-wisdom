# Berkshire Hathaway 2016 Annual Meeting

Held on April 30, 2016, at the CenturyLink Center in Omaha — and live-streamed for the first time via Yahoo, with simultaneous Mandarin translation — the 2016 meeting is dominated by two live controversies and one structural inflection. The collapse of Valeant Pharmaceuticals and the damage it did to the Sequoia Fund arrived at the meeting as a real-time case study in the Kiewit maxim: intelligent and energetic management without integrity is more dangerous than mediocre management. At year eight of his decade-long bet, Buffett used the cumulative data — the Vanguard S&P 500 index fund ahead by roughly 40 points on a fund-of-funds basket — to deliver the meeting's most extended and analytically complete lecture on why active management must in aggregate destroy wealth relative to passive indexing. And the Precision Castparts acquisition, closed in January 2016 as Berkshire's largest ever, marked the public consolidation of a strategic shift decades in the making: from no-brainer businesses an idiot can manage, to businesses requiring exceptional managers and historically high multiples. The meeting also produced the sharpest articulation in the corpus of why standard due diligence checklists miss the real risks of acquisitions; a GEICO incentive-system case study that demolishes the intuition that rewarding profits is obviously correct; and Munger's Aesop update — a bird in the hand is now worth about nine-tenths of a bird in the bush in Europe. This page keeps the wiki's two-layer convention: a distilled thematic top, then a comprehensive ★-marked Q&A digest as the home for detail.

## Key themes

### The arithmetic of active management: why the hyperactives must lose

At the year-eight mark of his wager, Buffett delivered the most analytically complete argument for passive indexing in the meeting corpus — not as empirical observation but as arithmetic necessity. Divide all investment capital in two: half sits passively, owning exactly its proportionate share of every asset, paying no fees. By definition, this half earns exactly the market return. The other half, the "hyperactives," must in aggregate earn the same gross return, because the market's total return is fixed. But the hyperactives pay fees — management expenses, advisory costs, fund-of-fund layers — so they must as a group earn less than the passives net of expenses. The individual hyperactive can beat the index; the group cannot. Eight years in, the Vanguard fund was ahead by roughly 40 cumulative percentage points. The intermediaries — consultants who recommend complexity over simplicity — have structural incentives to keep the hyperactives active: "No consultant in the world is going to tell you, just buy an S&P index fund and sit for the next 50 years. You don't get to be a consultant that way." See [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md), [investment-friction](../concepts/investment-friction.md). *(M29)*

### Integrity + intelligence = danger; pattern recognition and the Valeant lesson

Pete Kiewit's maxim, relayed by Buffett: find someone intelligent, energetic, and with integrity — "and if they don't have the last, be sure they don't have the first two." Valeant was the live case study: intelligent, energetic management pursuing a business model built on price gouging and accounting manipulation that Senate hearings confirmed was "not a pretty picture." Berkshire had been pitched Valeant multiple times and declined. The Sequoia Fund's manager — successor to Bill Ruane, whose fund Buffett helped found in 1969 for ex-partners — became "overly entranced with a business model" and concentrated the fund against board objections. Munger's verdict: "Valeant, of course, was a sewer, and those who created it deserve all the opprobrium that they got." The generalizing principle: "Pattern recognition gets very important in evaluating humans and businesses. The patterns aren't 100 percent, and none of them exactly repeat themselves, but there are certain things in business and securities markets that we've seen over and over, and that frequently come to a bad end." The chain-letter structure is one such pattern — "nobody calls them chain letters because that has a connotation that will scare you off." See [institutional-imperative](../concepts/institutional-imperative.md), [concentration-vs-diversification](../concepts/concentration-vs-diversification.md). *(M28)*

### From no-brainer businesses to exceptional-manager businesses: the size trap

The 2016 meeting marks the public consolidation of a strategic evolution underway for decades. In the early years, Berkshire bought businesses that could survive mediocre management — moated, capital-light, no-brainer economics. Munger: "We buy a business an idiot can manage, because sooner or later an idiot will." The size of capital to deploy now makes such businesses unavailable at scale. What replaced them is Precision Castparts — requiring "a very superior management that's going to stay superior for a long time," sold at a historically high multiple, where customer dependency and quality justify the premium. Buffett acknowledged the downside with disarming honesty: capital intensity "acts as an anchor on returns" and the extraordinary 100%-per-annum returns on invested capital of the early days are gone. When the no-brainer businesses are no longer available at scale, the entire edge shifts to identifying exceptional managers and then getting out of their way. The Berkshire model for such acquisitions: unlimited canvas, no earnings-guidance pressure, "if he needs capital, I've got an 800 number." See [moat](../concepts/moat.md), [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md). *(M7, M8)*

### Incentive design must match the unit, not the org chart

GEICO's compensation system uses exactly two variables across 20,000+ employees: growth in policies in force, and profitability of seasoned business. Not total profits — because profits decline when you grow aggressively, so rewarding profit alone would incentivize shrinking the book. Not gross premium growth — because average premium inflation is outside employees' control. Two clean variables, unchanged since 1995, scaling in multiplier but not in kind as seniority rises. The anti-pattern is the GFC: banks rewarded profits measured against accounting rules that let lenders use historical loss rates on far riskier new books — "so an idiot could make a lot of money by just making way-gamier loans at high interest, and accruing a lot of interest." Phantom profits; real capital destruction. Munger: "That was insane for the accountants to allow that. Literally insane. That's not too strong a word." The rule: you get what you reward for, so the designer's job is to identify what actually creates value in a specific business — and that answer is never the same twice across 70–80 different companies. See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md), [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md). *(A21)*

### What checklists miss: the real risk in acquisitions is economics and character

Berkshire's handshake-deal process is not carelessness — it reflects a clear diagnosis of where acquisition mistakes actually originate. "The mistakes are always about making an improper assessment of the economic conditions in the future of the industry of the company. They're not a bad lease. They're not a specific labor contract. They're not a questionable patent." See's Candy had ~150 leases; Precision Castparts has 170 plants with some pollution exposure. "Those are not what determine whether a $32 billion acquisition is going to look good five or ten years from now." What determines it: will the manager behave differently once the transaction is closed? Are the basic economics durable against a structural disruptor? Will Amazon kill the business in five years? No checklist answers those questions. And checklist-driven negotiations tend to blow up: "People can get obstinate about very small points, and it's silly to be obstinate, but people get silly sometimes." Munger's close: "How many people who, in this room, are happily married, carefully checked their spouse's birth certificate and so on?" See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md). *(A12)*

### Interest rates as gravity: Aesop's fable at zero

Buffett updated Aesop's bird-in-hand principle for 2016's rate environment: "A bird in the hand is worth two in the bush. But a bird in the hand now is worth about nine-tenths of a bird in the bush in Europe." The fundamental logic of investing — comparing present cash to future cash at a discount rate — means any movement in the rate changes the value of every asset. Near-zero rates justify paying more for businesses than at 6%; Buffett confirmed he paid more for Precision Castparts than he would have in a normal-rate environment. The parallel principle applies to Berkshire's insurance operations: some long-tail contracts are written at a probable underwriting loss, because the option value of having long-dated capital available to a flexible deployer exceeds the loss. Munger: "We're willing to pay a little money now to have just a certainty of having a lot of money available in case something really attractive comes up in a difficult time." The 2008–09 crisis validated this option cost many times over. See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [float](../concepts/float.md), [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md). *(A17, A28)*

### Amazon and the capital-allocator's structural advantage

The Amazon disruption question produced a principle that goes beyond e-commerce: the advantage of having no industry identity to defend. Buffett: "We didn't think of ourselves as department store guys, or steel guys, or tire guys... we've thought of ourselves as having capital to allocate. If you start with a given industry focus and spend your whole time working on a way to make a better tire... I think it's hard to have the flexibility of mind that you have if you just think you have a large and growing pile of capital, and you try to figure out the best next move." GEICO's eventual internet pivot — "we were slow on the internet" but then "jumped in with both feet" — illustrates the principle in action. Even so: "We are not going to out-Bezos Bezos, by a long shot." Recognizing the limits of the circle is as important as recognizing its advantages. Munger: "We failed so thoroughly in retailing when we were young that we pretty well avoided the worst troubles when we were old." See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md). *(M12)*

## Q&A digest — Morning session

**M5/M6 · Normalized earnings are the goal; never manage to a quarter.** *Buffett:* Q1 2016 shows GEICO underwriting hurt by Texas hailstorms, railroad earnings down (secular coal decline), and Precision Castparts and Duracell added during the quarter so their full earnings are not reflected. The share count has grown only 8.2% in 17 years since the Gen Re merger. Goal: increase normalized earning power per share over time, never manage to a quarterly number. "We make no attempt to manage earnings in any way... We make investment decisions solely on the basis of what we think the best investment decision is, not on the basis of how it will affect earnings in any quarter." See [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md).

**★ M7 · "One of the problems of prosperity" — capital intensity limits return.** *Buffett:* the ideal business takes no capital but grows — See's Candy and the Buffalo Evening News in their prime. As capital compounds, the universe of such businesses shrinks relative to the capital to be deployed. Berkshire is now forced into capital-intensive utilities and railroads earning 11–12% on capital versus the 100%-per-annum returns of early acquisitions. "Capital — increasing capital — acts as an anchor on returns in many ways." *Munger:* "When our circumstances changed, we changed our minds. Slowly and reluctantly." Plan B (decent returns on capital-intensive businesses) "is working pretty well. In many ways, I've gotten so I sort of prefer it." See [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md), [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md).

**★ M8 · Precision Castparts: the exceptional-manager model in full.** *Buffett:* closed end of January 2016 — Berkshire's largest acquisition. Mark Donegan is "one of a kind" as a manager; the main asset acquired is the human one. As a Berkshire subsidiary, Donegan no longer needs to explain quarterly earnings to analysts, negotiate bank lines, or travel to Omaha to justify capital decisions. "His canvas has been broadened, in large, with the acquisition by Berkshire." *Munger:* the evolution from "a business an idiot can manage" to businesses requiring very superior management that stays superior — "we've gotten almost as good at picking the superior managers as we were in the old days at picking the no-brainer businesses." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**★ M10 · Munich Re and Swiss Re sold: reinsurance structurally worse.** *Buffett:* sold entire positions in both (Berkshire had owned ~10% of Munich Re, ~3% of Swiss Re). Reinsurance supply has gone up while demand has not: new capacity from offshore investment managers using reinsurance entities as "beards" — domicile in a friendly tax jurisdiction, actually run a hedge fund, use the reinsurance label as cover. Near-zero rates erode float income. Berkshire has more flexibility than competitors who are "pretty well tied to a given type of business model." *Munger:* "There's a lot of new capacity in reinsurance and very heavy competition... we like the place with more competitive advantage. We're learning." The basic economic summary: "supply has gone up and demand has not." See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md), [float](../concepts/float.md).

**M11 · GEICO vs. Progressive: auto death rate hurt loss ratios.** *Buffett:* GEICO's 2015 combined ratio of 98.0 vs. Progressive Direct's 95.1 reflects an industry-wide increase in both accident frequency and severity — for the first time in many years, auto deaths per 100 million miles driven went up, likely from distracted driving. GEICO adjusted rates. The long-run record: GEICO has grown from roughly 2% to 11% of the private-passenger auto market since Berkshire took 100% ownership. *Munger:* "GEICO's quintupled its market share since we bought all of it... I don't think we should worry about the fact that somebody else had a good quarter." See [geico](../entities/geico.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**★ M12 · Amazon and the capital-allocator's structural advantage.** *Buffett:* Amazon's disruption is "really huge" and the full effect on industry "is far from having been seen." Berkshire's structural advantage: it never defined itself by industry. "We've thought of ourselves as having capital to allocate." GEICO's eventual internet pivot illustrates the adaptive capacity this unlocks. Even so: "We are not going to out-Bezos Bezos, by a long shot." If you own shopping malls, "I'd be thinking plenty hard about what they might look like 10 or 20 years from now." *Munger:* "We failed so thoroughly in retailing when we were young that we pretty well avoided the worst troubles when we were old... our biggest retailers are so strong that they'll be among the last people to have troubles from Amazon." See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**M13 · Coke and the "measure detriment without advantage" error.** *Buffett:* defends Coca-Cola through revealed preference — 1.9 billion eight-ounce daily servings since 1886. Attributing obesity-related illness specifically to the Coca-Cola fraction of a high-calorie diet is "quite spurious." *Munger:* "They measure the detriment without considering the advantage. That's really stupid. That's like saying we should give up air travel through airlines because 100 people die a year in air crashes. The benefit is worth the risk." The principle transfers broadly: any argument that counts costs without counting benefits, or vice versa, is incomplete and likely motivated. See [coca-cola](../entities/coca-cola.md).

**M14 · Renewables math: MidAmerican's consolidated-tax-return advantage.** *Buffett:* MidAmerican's deep investment in wind (Iowa will reach ~85% wind generation by 2020; $3.6B new commitment) is enabled by the federal production tax credit (2.3¢/kWh) and, critically, by sitting inside Berkshire's consolidated tax return — "there's really no one situated as well as MidAmerican Energy to really put its foot to the floor in terms of developing wind and solar" because tax credits require profitable taxable income to absorb them. Iowa rates stay frozen until 2029. *Munger:* "We're doing way more than our share of shifting to renewable energy, and we're charging way lower energy prices to our utility customers." See [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md).

**★ M15 · Derivatives: discontinuity risk remains a systemic time bomb.** *Buffett:* derivatives are still "a potential time bomb in the system" because of discontinuity risk. In continuous markets, collateralization and mark-to-market contain problems. But if markets stop — post-9/11, WWI closure of the NYSE, nearly on October 20, 1987 — netting arrangements and collateral can fail in ways impossible to calculate in advance. The Gen Re inherited book cost ~$400M to unwind in a benign market with no pressure. He knows of one currently mismarked position: "it would blow your mind." *Munger:* auditors "blessed that big derivative position as being worth a lot of money — they were only off, what, many hundreds of millions." Berkshire won't enter derivative contracts requiring collateral; collateral calls during a discontinuity could force sales at the worst moment. See [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md), [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md).

**★ M16 · Float still useful — but less so at near-zero rates.** *Buffett:* some float now sits in Europe at negative rates. Even so, float remains an asset: Berkshire's broader range of deployment options — capital cushion, diverse earning streams — lets it use float in ways unavailable to typical insurers. "Float is not worth as much to insurance companies now as it was 10 years ago. And that's true at Berkshire. I think it's worth considerably more to us than it is to the typical insurance company." Float remains shown as a liability but is "actually a huge asset." Cash position was ~$50B, would be back over $60B when the Kraft Heinz preferred was called. See [float](../concepts/float.md), [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md).

**★ M17 · BNSF coal decline is secular; don't mark wholly-owned businesses to market.** *Buffett:* coal is ~20% of BNSF revenues and its decline is secular. The railroad earned less in 2016 than in 2015. But "we are not marking our wholly-owned businesses to market because we know we're going to hold them forever." The equity market's enthusiasm and subsequent selloff in railroad stocks is short-termism irrelevant to perpetual holders. "We'd love to be able to buy a second thing exactly like [BNSF] at that price." The network remains irreplaceable. *Munger:* "Nothing to add." See [bnsf](../entities/bnsf.md), [intrinsic-value-vs-book-value](../concepts/intrinsic-value-vs-book-value.md).

**★ M18 · Stocks as businesses; don't envy lottery winners.** *Buffett:* when IPO winners appear on television and short-term speculators seem to prosper, the discipline is to stay anchored to business fundamentals. "People win lotteries every day, but there's no reason to have that affect you at all." The frame: when you buy a stock, "get yourself in the mental frame of mind that you're buying a business, and if you don't look at a quote on it for five years, that's fine. You don't get a quote on your farm every day." *Munger:* "your children are right to look for people they can trust in dealing with stocks and bonds. Unfortunately, more than half the time, they will fail." See [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md), [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**M19 · Nevada solar: who pays for the externality?** *Buffett + Greg Abel:* Nevada's utility commission ended the arrangement allowing ~17,000 rooftop solar owners to sell back power at ~10¢/kWh when NV Energy could purchase or produce it at ~3.5¢. The commission's decision: 99% of customers should not subsidize the 1% who can afford rooftop installations. Principle: externality benefits (reduced CO₂) are global; the subsidy should be borne by society broadly (via federal tax credits) rather than by individual ratepayers. By 2019, NV Energy will retire 76% of its coal capacity. See [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md).

**M20 · We don't predict commodity prices.** *Buffett:* investments in Phillips 66, Kinder Morgan, and Precision Castparts all carry oil/gas revenue exposure, but none were made based on commodity-price predictions. "We haven't the faintest idea what the long-term price of oil is." *Munger:* "I'm even more ignorant than you are." The discipline generalizes: Berkshire doesn't hedge cocoa, sugar, or oil. Skip macro commodity forecasting; focus on business quality. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ M22 · Berkshire does fine under any president; the American tailwind.** *Buffett:* regardless of which candidate wins, Berkshire will continue to do fine. The larger context: in Buffett's lifetime, U.S. real GDP per capita has gone up six-for-one. "Twenty years from now, they'll be far more output per capita in the United States, in real terms, than there is now... No presidential candidate or president is going to end that." Owners of businesses have been insulated from the rate environment that has crushed fixed-income savers — returns on tangible equity remain strong even at near-zero rates. *Munger:* GDP figures "greatly understate" real improvements because many gains don't translate cleanly into monetary terms. See [american-tailwind](../concepts/american-tailwind.md).

**M23 · BNSF CEO on rail mergers: not now, maybe later.** *Matt Rose (BNSF CEO):* the STB's 1999 rule change — any merger must be accretive to competition — effectively blocked large combinations. Canadian Pacific's hostile bid for Norfolk Southern failed because customers, labor, and communities saw no benefit. A final round of consolidation may come as U.S. population growth creates capacity scarcity; "we don't see the dynamics in place" now. See [bnsf](../entities/bnsf.md).

**M24 · Wells Fargo: attracted by general banking, not investment banking.** *Buffett:* Berkshire's largest non-control equity position was bought for WF's core retail and commercial banking — not the investment banking that came with Wachovia. *Munger:* "we fear the genre more than we love it." WF has "a very cheap base of money" and Berkshire is "very likely" to eventually exercise its Bank of America warrants. *Supersession flag:* Berkshire dramatically reduced its Wells Fargo position starting in 2020 following the unauthorized-account scandal, which predated this meeting but was not yet a focus here. See [wells-fargo](../entities/wells-fargo.md).

**M25 · Activists breaking up Berkshire: very unlikely; buybacks as natural defense.** *Buffett:* buybacks near intrinsic value close the spread that activists need. The parts would be worth less than the whole — MidAmerican's renewables investment could not have been executed without the Berkshire capital cushion. Post-death, his estate will be the largest Berkshire shareholder by votes for "some years" as stock distributes to philanthropy. The long-hold shareholder base further reduces activist risk. See [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md), [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**M26 · Pure leasing is unattractive; service moat matters.** *Buffett:* XTRA (truck leasing) and Union Tank Car work because they bring service, not just financing. Pure money-leasing is unattractive against banks with 10bp cost of funds. Aircraft leasing: "a scary business" — "people have done well by using short-term money to finance longer-term assets which have big residual risks, and that just isn't for us." See [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md).

**M27 · "We're not targeting competitors for destruction" — widen the moat.** *Buffett:* competitive orientation is to serve customers well, keep costs down, and think about what customers will want in 10 years. "If you take care of your customer, the customer takes care of you." Recognizing when something is unfixable is equally important — Berkshire kept a Baltimore department store in 1966 until it became clear the model was broken. *Munger:* "We're not targeting competitors for destruction. We're just trying to do the best we can everywhere." See [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**★ M28 · Sequoia and Valeant: concentration, pattern failure, and integrity.** *Buffett:* when closing his partnership in 1969, Buffett directed ex-partners to two managers he trusted intellectually and morally: Sandy Gottesman and Bill Ruane. Ruane set up Sequoia Fund specifically for partners with smaller sums; his record from inception through 2005 death was excellent. The successor manager built a concentrated Valeant position despite board objection, then increased it as doubts accumulated. Result: catastrophic losses from what Senate hearings showed was a roll-up-and-price-gouge model. Buffett's diagnosis: the manager "got overly entranced with a business model." Kiewit's maxim applies: "find somebody that's intelligent, energetic, and has integrity. And... if they don't have the last, be sure they don't have the first two." *Munger:* "Valeant, of course, was a sewer, and those who created it deserve all the opprobrium that they got." See [concentration-vs-diversification](../concepts/concentration-vs-diversification.md), [institutional-imperative](../concepts/institutional-imperative.md).

**★ M29 · Hedge fund bet year 8: the hyperactives vs. the low-energy.** *Buffett:* after eight years, the Vanguard S&P 500 index fund leads the basket of five fund-of-funds by roughly 40 cumulative percentage points. Buffett extended the argument from data to arithmetic: split all investment capital in half — half "low-energy," owning everything proportionally, paying no fees; half "hyperactive," active, paying fees. Both halves earn the same gross return (markets must clear); the hyperactives earn less net because fees come out. "The people who don't do anything have to do better than the people that are trying to do better. It's that simple." Consultants have structural incentives to recommend complexity: "No consultant in the world is going to tell you, just buy an S&P index fund and sit for the next 50 years. You don't get to be a consultant that way. And you certainly don't get an annual fee that way." *Munger:* "You're talking to a bunch of people who have solved their problem by buying Berkshire Hathaway." See [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md), [investment-friction](../concepts/investment-friction.md).

## Q&A digest — Afternoon session

**A1 · Online commercial insurance experiment.** *Buffett:* Berkshire runs two online commercial insurance portals — "BIG" (Applied Underwriters, workers' comp) and an Ajit-run specialty operation — plus commercial auto through GEICO. "When you look at what has happened, just take Amazon, you want to try a lot of things." The speed of consumer migration from phone to internet in personal auto surprised even Buffett; the same shift may occur in commercial lines. See [geico](../entities/geico.md), [direct-distribution-moat](../concepts/direct-distribution-moat.md).

**★ A2 · Culture will endure for "many, many decades."** *Buffett:* the culture's durability is institutional, not personal. Three reinforcing mechanisms: board members who understand it; managers who self-selected into it; and a shareholder base that "clearly recognizes the special nature of the culture." Non-executive chairman (Howard Buffett) is "a 1-in-100 or 1-in-500" safeguard against a succession mistake — not the main factor. Size will be the main challenge. *Munger:* "I really think the culture is going to surprise everybody — how well it lasts... There's just so much power in place." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**★ A3 · Board criteria: business-savvy, shareholder-oriented, personal Berkshire interest.** *Buffett:* Berkshire applies three tests and only three — big names, diversity boxes, and prestige are not on the list. Contrast: Theranos had "very big names on their board." Berkshire directors own stock purchased at market prices (no grants, no options) and are not there for the $200,000-a-year check for 10% of their time. *Munger:* the archbishop needing surgery "did not look around for the leading Catholic surgeon" — he looked for the best surgeon. Selecting directors on demographic criteria rather than competence and alignment is a governance failure by the same logic. See [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md), [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**★ A4 · Buybacks: only at demonstrably below intrinsic value.** *Buffett:* Berkshire's 1.2× book trigger; the stock came close but likely never hit it during early 2016. At 1.2×, buying back stock is "the surest way of making money per share" — buying dollar bills for less than a dollar. Two tensions: (1) Buffett genuinely dislikes buying out partners at prices he knows are far below intrinsic value; (2) if cash becomes impossible to deploy, the threshold may need to rise. The broader principle: buybacks divorced from a price metric are "totally crazy" — like pledging to buy a company "at whatever price." *Munger:* elsewhere in corporate America, buyback plans "get a life of their own" and companies "buy back stock at very high prices that really don't do the shareholders any good at all." See [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md), [intrinsic-value-vs-book-value](../concepts/intrinsic-value-vs-book-value.md).

**A5 · Nebraska Furniture Mart Dallas: our largest-volume store.** *Buffett:* the Dallas/Fort Worth store, open about a year, is already Berkshire's highest-volume furniture location. Early delivery problems (demand exceeded routing capacity) have been resolved. Expected to reach $1 billion in annual sales. Over 20 auto dealerships in the area; 400+ acres; Toyota and Lexus relocating nearby. See [nebraska-furniture-mart](../entities/nebraska-furniture-mart.md).

**A6 · Cyber/nuclear/biological/chemical: the only external threat to Berkshire.** *Buffett:* the greatest risk to Berkshire — and to civilization — is a mass-casualty attack. "It will happen." As noted in the annual report, this is the only external factor that could genuinely damage Berkshire's long-term economic well-being. Buffett has supported the Nuclear Threat Initiative and Union of Concerned Scientists; effective philanthropic levers remain elusive. *Munger:* "We haven't been very good at getting the government to follow any of our advice." See [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md).

**A7 · Lubrizol: on schedule, one acquisition mistake.** *Buffett:* the core lubricant additives business (one of four competitors globally; Berkshire is the leader) has performed "almost exactly as you would anticipate." One large acquisition in oil-field specialty chemicals was made "just about the time that oil took a nosedive" and was a clear mistake. The fundamental earning power of the additives business is undimmed. *Munger:* "Nothing to add." See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**★ A8 · Microeconomics is what we do; macroeconomics is what we put up with.** *Munger:* when a questioner asked about "macro factors," Munger corrected the confusion: "We pay a lot of attention to those [micro factors]. If you talk about macro, we don't know any more than anybody else." Buffett's method: deep immersion in unit economics, cohort behavior, and store-by-store patterns. On cognitive bias: "We try and avoid the worst anchoring effect, which is always your previous conclusion. We really try and destroy our previous ideas." And: to earn the right to disagree with someone, you must be able to state their case better than they can. *Munger:* "Microeconomics is what we do, and macroeconomics is what we put up with." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [inversion](../concepts/inversion.md).

**A9 · "I would much rather make money for Berkshire than for myself."** *Buffett:* clarifies the Seritage investment (a ~$2B market-cap REIT, below Berkshire's minimum scale) and corrects a Phillips 66 attribution error (he has never owned a share). His 99% personal concentration in Berkshire is not sacrifice but preference: "my personality — everything's more wound up in how Berkshire does than I am myself, because I'm going to give it all away." *Munger:* 50+ years without a significant conflict of interest; Berkshire is the primary investment for both. See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md), [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).

**A10 · Cash flow = normalized earnings + float growth.** *Buffett:* Berkshire's practical cash generation is net income plus increase in float — float has added $80+ billion to investment capacity over time above what earnings alone would have provided. Capex at BNSF and BHE will run above depreciation for a long time; depreciation is an inadequate measure of steady-state capex for railroads. *Munger:* "We've lived in a torrent of money, and we were constantly deploying it, and disbursed assets, and we were wising up as we went along. That's a pretty good system." See [float](../concepts/float.md), [owner-earnings](../concepts/owner-earnings.md).

**★ A11 · Pattern recognition + emotional control: Berkshire's "secret."** *Buffett:* when asked what gives Berkshire its edge, the answer combines Ben Graham (investing framework) and Charlie Munger (understanding business quality) with a lifetime of observing which businesses work and which don't. "Pattern recognition gets very important in evaluating humans and businesses." The critical non-cognitive component: "You do need emotional control. We see very smart people do very stupid things... people get very rich and then leverage themselves up in some way that they lose everything. They are risking something that's important to them for something that isn't important to them." *Munger:* "There's just a few simple tricks that work — particularly if you've got a temperament that has a combination of patience and opportunism in it... we're really trying to behave well." See [risk-vs-volatility](../concepts/risk-vs-volatility.md), [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**★ A12 · Due diligence checklists don't find the real risks.** *Buffett:* Berkshire's quick, handshake-based process reflects a clear diagnosis of where acquisition mistakes come from. "The mistakes are always about making an improper assessment of the economic conditions in the future of the industry of the company. They're not a bad lease. They're not a specific labor contract. They're not a questionable patent." A See's Candy acquisition has ~150 leases; Precision Castparts has 170 plants with pollution issues somewhere. "Those are not what determine whether a $32 billion acquisition is going to look good five or ten years from now." What matters: does the manager behave differently once cashed out? Are basic economics durable? No checklist answers those. And: checklist-driven negotiations tend to blow up over minor points. *Munger:* "How many people who, in this room, are happily married, carefully checked their spouse's birth certificate and so on?" See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ A13 · Ajit takes over Gen Re; no org chart, no titles.** *Buffett:* Tad Montross (39 years, "sensational job") retires; Ajit Jain absorbs Gen Re oversight. The broader principle: able people can handle far more than any preconceived org-chart norm would suggest. "There is no limit to what talented people can accomplish." Berkshire never asks how many people can report to whom — "every decision that comes up, we just try and figure out the most logical thing to do at that time." *Munger:* "The unable people by and large you can't fix. So I think you're forced to use our system if you have your wits about you. We don't pay any attention to titles... If a charwoman gave us a good idea, we'd accept it cheerfully." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md).

**A14 · "The rating agencies are wrong" on Berkshire.** *Munger:* Berkshire's sub-AAA credit rating reflects not fitting the rating models, not a question of creditworthiness. Buffett always opens rating-agency negotiations at quadruple-A. *Munger:* "The rating agencies are wrong — and set in their ways." See [berkshire-hathaway](../entities/berkshire-hathaway.md).

**A15 · 3G and Kraft Heinz: lean staffing is not cruelty.** *Buffett:* the test of whether 3G's cost cuts damage volume is a three-to-five-year question; so far, no evidence of volume damage attributable to cuts. The underlying principle: Tom Murphy never hired people he didn't need, and "therefore they never had layoffs." Overstaffing isn't neutral — "sloppy thinking in one area probably indicates there may well be sloppy thinking elsewhere." *Munger:* "Generally speaking, I think the leanly-staffed companies do better at everything than the ones that are overstaffed. I think overstaffing is like getting to weigh 400 pounds when you're a normal person." *Supersession flag:* In 2019, Berkshire took a $15.7 billion Kraft Heinz write-down; Buffett subsequently acknowledged overpaying and that 3G's cost-cut strategy had damaged long-term brand investment, partially vindicating the concerns raised at this meeting. See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md), [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).

**A16 · Van Tuyl economics better than they appear.** *Buffett:* Van Tuyl was purchased for $4.1B but included ~$1B in securities and will carry significant amortization charges for several years — the economics are "almost exactly on schedule" and better than the headline number suggests. Jeff Rachor "really fits the Berkshire mold." Subsequent auto dealership acquisitions have been scarce because sellers think Berkshire paid more than it did and price accordingly.

**★ A17 · Negative interest rates: Aesop updated, valuation adjusted.** *Buffett:* moving from zero to negative rates is not categorically different from moving from 4% to 3.5% — just another half-point reduction. What is dramatic is being near zero in the first place. "The nature of it is that you'll pay more for a business when interest rates are zero than if they were, like, 15 percent when Volcker was around." He confirmed paying more for Precision Castparts because rates were near zero. Aesop: "a bird in a hand is worth two in the bush. But a bird in the hand now is worth about nine-tenths of a bird in the bush in Europe." *Munger:* "I don't think anybody really knows much about negative interest rates. We never had them before... Our advantage is that we know we don't understand it." And: "If you're not confused, you haven't thought about it correctly." See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [bonds-and-inflation](../concepts/bonds-and-inflation.md).

**★ A19 · American Express: alert to threats, still held.** *Buffett:* payments is "an area of intense interest to a lot of very smart people who have got a lot of resources, and rapid change." Buffett "personally feel[s] OK about American Express" and is happy to own it, but its position "has been under attack for decades, more intensively lately, and it will continue to be under attack." The obligation is continuous alertness: "You have to be thinking all the time... whether there's been something that really changes the game in a big way." *Munger:* "A lot of great businesses aren't quite so great as they used to be... When I think of the power of General Motors when I was young... They looked totally invincible. Torrents of cash... They wiped out all the shareholders." *Supersession note:* Berkshire reduced its AmEx position in subsequent years but later rebuilt it as a "forever" holding; the investment proved profitable over time, validating patience. See [american-express](../entities/american-express.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**★ A21 · Incentive design: GEICO's two variables and the GFC accounting failure.** *Munger:* "You get what you reward for. So if you have a dumb incentive system, you get dumb outcomes." *Buffett:* GEICO's compensation since 1995 uses exactly two variables — growth in policies in force, and profitability of seasoned business. Not total profit: "the dumbest thing you could do" because it incentivizes shrinking the book. Not gross premium growth: inflation in average premiums is outside employees' control. "Totally aligns the goals of the organization with the goals of the owner." No corporate-wide master plan — "the idea of having a coordinated arrangement for incentive compensation across 70 or 80 businesses is just totally nuts." GFC corollary (Munger): banks' profit-sharing on phantom GAAP profits — historical loan-loss provisions on far riskier new books — caused "an idiot" to make money by accruing interest on way-gamier loans while the accounting blessed it. "That was insane for the accountants to allow that. Literally insane. That's not too strong a word." See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md), [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md), [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md).

**A22 · BNSF capex: depreciation is an inadequate yardstick.** *Buffett:* even at $4.3B (down from $5.7B in the crisis-driven 2015), "the true maintenance capex... is higher than 60 percent of that number" to keep the railroad in competitive shape. Depreciation charges systematically understate the true steady-state capital need. "Positive train control" has added another ~$2B in aggregate. Berkshire's overall reported figures are conservative — more intangible amortization than real economic expense — but not at the railroad. See [bnsf](../entities/bnsf.md), [owner-earnings](../concepts/owner-earnings.md).

**A23 · Low oil prices are net good for the US.** *Buffett:* the U.S. is a net oil importer — lower oil prices benefit ~330 million consumers and hurt oil-producing regions and capital values. The consumer benefit is diffuse and slow; the capital-value contraction is immediate and concentrated. "Net, the United States is better off, and Saudi Arabia is worse off, when prices of oil are lower."

**A24 · Float and cash: don't over-analyze which subsidiary pocket holds it.** *Buffett:* with $60B+ earning near-zero, it doesn't matter which subsidiary holds the cash. Berkshire will never go below $20B. The supplier-payment principle: "there's something to be said for leaning over backward to have a win-win relationship with both suppliers and customers, always" — Berkshire does not extend payables aggressively even though it could.

**★ A25 · "Things are good enough at Berkshire. We don't need to inflate the figures."** *Munger:* asked whether Berkshire could separate out "one-time" restructuring charges as virtually every major corporation does, Munger compared the proposal to killing one's mother for the insurance money. Berkshire has never taken a restructuring charge. *Buffett:* Berkshire's figures are conservatively stated overall — more intangible amortization than real economic expense — though railroad depreciation overstates true earnings. "I've never wanted to report one of these things where I have the whole adjusted earnings set out and say, this is what you're supposed to pay attention to, because every one of those I've seen virtually results in some inflation of figures. Things are good enough at Berkshire. We don't need to inflate the figures." See [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md), [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md).

**★ A27 · A fantastic manager in a large domain creates enormous value.** *Buffett:* losing Ajit Jain would be "very significant" for National Indemnity and Berkshire's reinsurance operations — "there's not another Ajit in the house." The principle generalizes: "having a fantastic manager that has a large business — potential business — available to them, and who makes the most of it... it's huge over time." Examples: Jeff Bezos at Amazon; Tom Murphy and Dan Burke at Capital Cities — "the value of Tom Murphy and Dan Burke was the difference between zero and what they ended up with... They built that thing from a bankrupt UHF station in Albany." *Munger:* "Ajit has a longer shelf life than we do." See [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md), [tom-murphy](../people/tom-murphy.md), [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**★ A28 · Float at underwriting loss = option cost for long-dated capital.** *Buffett:* some Berkshire reinsurance contracts are written with a probable expected underwriting loss — a deliberate choice. Accepting a small premium loss today secures long-dated investable capital that Berkshire (with unusual flexibility) can deploy at an attractive rate when opportunities arise. *Munger:* "We're willing to pay a little money now to have just a certainty of having a lot of money available in case something really attractive comes up in a difficult time." This option came in handy in 2008–09. Near-zero rates make the arithmetic harder, but Berkshire's unusual deployment options partially compensate. *Buffett:* "It's an option cost." See [float](../concepts/float.md), [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**A29 · No nationwide residential real estate bubble.** *Buffett:* real estate is less attractive than in 2012. Some commercial properties at very low cap rates have "more potential for loss than gain." But no nationwide residential bubble: "I don't think the next time around the problem is going to be a real estate bubble." See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md).

**A30 · Todd Combs and Ted Weschler: cultural fit over track record.** *Buffett:* both portfolio managers were selected primarily for character and cultural fit, not raw performance. They are expanding beyond their $9B portfolios each into operational and deal work. *Munger:* "That trustworthiness is more important than the brains. It's not that they don't have the brains, but we wouldn't hire anybody, no matter how able, if we didn't trust them." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).

**A32 · IBM: "coping with a considerable change."** *Munger:* IBM is "obviously coping with a considerable change in the computing world, and it's attempting something that's big and interesting, and God knows whether it's going to work modestly or very well." *Buffett:* "We'll find out whether the strengths are strengths." Neither offers a bullish case; both are explicitly waiting to see. *Supersession flag:* Buffett sold virtually all of Berkshire's IBM shares in 2017, telling CNBC he had reassessed the competitive position — the ambiguity expressed here was the leading indicator. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

## Connections to other sources

- The hedge fund bet progresses from year 7 ([berkshire-2015-meeting](berkshire-2015-meeting.md): S&P 63.5% vs. funds 19%) to year 8 (2016: roughly 79.5% vs. 22%), providing the fullest cumulative empirical spine for the [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md) case. The 2016 addition is the arithmetic proof — the hyperactive/low-energy thought experiment — which moves the argument from data to structural necessity.
- The reinsurance supply/demand analysis and "beards" argument (M10) extends and reinforces the 2015 meeting's reinsurance-facades discussion ([berkshire-2015-meeting](berkshire-2015-meeting.md) A12). In 2015, the issue was alternative capital degrading the market; in 2016, it has progressed to selling entire positions in Munich Re and Swiss Re.
- The Valeant/Sequoia post-mortem (M28) mirrors the Teledyne/Singleton incentive-scandal discussion in [berkshire-2015-meeting](berkshire-2015-meeting.md) M31 — both show how a smart, driven culture can corrupt behavior, the 2015 example from ego-protection motives, the 2016 example from a flawed business model entrancing a previously excellent manager.
- The IBM ambiguity here (A32) carries forward from [berkshire-2015-meeting](berkshire-2015-meeting.md) M13 ("When we bought it, it was a two-to-nothing vote") and [berkshire-2013-meeting](berkshire-2013-meeting.md). The supersession connects forward: Buffett sold virtually all of the IBM position in 2017, with the ambivalence expressed here as the leading indicator.
- The capital-intensity/size-penalty theme (M7) extends the earliest size-as-disadvantage discussion from [berkshire-1994-meeting](berkshire-1994-meeting.md) A14 ("Size is a disadvantage") across 22 years: the 1994 version is about market-impact costs in trading; the 2016 version is about being forced into capital-intensive, lower-return businesses by the sheer scale of capital to deploy.
- The Aesop/interest-rate update (A17) is the 2016 instance of the interest-rate-as-gravity framework developed across the 2013 and 2015 meetings, here extended to negative territory for the first time in the meeting corpus.
- The exceptional-manager model for Precision Castparts (M8) is the live application of the "next 50 years" thesis discussed at the 50th-anniversary [berkshire-2014-meeting](berkshire-2014-meeting.md): when no-brainer businesses are unavailable at scale, the edge shifts entirely to manager selection and owner-operator culture.
