# Berkshire Hathaway 1986 Annual Letter

The 1986 letter's enduring contribution is its Appendix, which introduces the owner-earnings formula — the definition that cuts through the GAAP/cash-flow tangle to isolate what a business actually earns for its owners, illustrated by showing Scott Fetzer's identical economics under two different accounting presentations ($40.2M pre-acquisition reported earnings vs. $28.6M post-acquisition). Alongside the formula, the letter crystallizes the "moat-around-the-castle" image in canonical form for the first time, applied to GEICO's structural cost advantage. A third durable framework emerges from the Tax Reform Act analysis: franchise holders capture tax cuts as profit, while price-competitive businesses pass them through to customers — the seedbed of the 1991 franchise-vs-business essay. Threading through these lessons is the first explicit statement of Berkshire's permanent-ownership policy for Capital Cities, GEICO, and The Washington Post. Net worth rose 26.1% — but the letter's lasting value lies in what it defined, not in the year's results.

## Key themes

### Owner earnings: the formula that supersedes GAAP and "cash flow"

The 1986 Appendix is the most consequential analytical section in any Berkshire letter of the 1980s. Buffett presents the identical economics of Scott Fetzer under two accounting regimes: "Company O" (pre-acquisition) reports $40.2M of net income; "Company N" (post-acquisition) reports $28.6M. The $11.6M gap is entirely purchase-price accounting — non-cash inventory charges, inflated depreciation from asset write-ups, goodwill amortization, and deferred-tax timing — none of which reflects economic cost. The business's real earning power is unchanged by the acquisition.

The formula: owner earnings = **(a) reported earnings + (b) depreciation, depletion, amortization, and other non-cash charges − (c) average annual maintenance capex required to hold competitive position and unit volume.** The critical term is (c). Wall Street's conventional "cash flow" — (a) + (b) — systematically omits it. For manufacturing, retailing, extractive industries, and utilities, (c) is always significant; skipping it is the analytical tool of investment bankers marketing unjustifiable deals.

For Scott Fetzer, (c) ≈ $8.3M (old depreciation), far below post-acquisition (b) of $19.9M. Owner earnings therefore substantially exceed post-acquisition GAAP earnings — the business got better when Berkshire bought it; only the accounting changed. A beginner who internalizes this can interrogate any sell-side cash-flow model with appropriate skepticism for the rest of their career.

### The two CEO jobs: manager selection and capital allocation

Buffett and Munger distill Berkshire's entire management philosophy to two jobs: attract and keep outstanding managers; allocate the capital they generate. Capital allocation carries unusual weight at Berkshire because of compounding arithmetic: the firm earns above-normal returns, retains everything, and has subsidiaries requiring little incremental capital to grow. At 23% annual return on equity with full retention, the quality of each reinvestment decision compounds at a rate that dwarfs its importance at a typical 5%-returning, half-distributing business.

The 1986 letter is notable for Buffett's frank admission of failure on job two: he could not skillfully deploy the capital his managers generated — no major equity purchases, capital stockpiled in bonds. The discipline of restraint (not deploying into an overpriced market) is as much a part of the lesson as skill in deploying when prices are right.

The selection side has an equally firm standard: "Working with people who cause your stomach to churn seems much like marrying for money — probably a bad idea under any circumstances, but absolute madness if you are already rich."

### The moat named: GEICO's cost advantage crystallized

The concept of a structural competitive advantage had appeared in Berkshire letters since 1982. The 1986 letter gives it its enduring image. GEICO's expense ratio of 23.5% — against competitors running 15 points higher, including Allstate and State Farm — is a structural gap that agent-based auto insurers cannot close without dismantling their own distribution model. "The difference between GEICO's costs and those of its competitors is a kind of moat that protects a valuable and much-sought-after business castle."

The letter adds the dynamic dimension: GEICO's chairman is "continually widening the moat by driving down costs still more." A moat is not a static fact but an ongoing operational discipline — the template that Tony Nicely will extend through the 1990s into the GEICO virtuous circle.

### Commodity insurance: statesmanship cannot repeal supply

Insurance industry leaders periodically call for pricing discipline — "statesmanlike" behavior. Buffett's dismissal: this is as futile as asking a Nebraska corn grower to persuade worldwide farmers to price with more restraint. "What's needed is not more statesmen, but less corn." The commodity condition is structural: capital rushes in when profits appear, capacity surges, prices fall.

The 1986 cycle illustrates it precisely: premium volume soared 22.6% versus 15.5% loss growth, producing the temporary profit window Buffett forecast in 1985. But premiums were already decelerating quarter by quarter (27.1% in Q1 to 18.7% in Q4), and Berkshire expected the improvement to end by 1988-89. Berkshire's structural response — standing at rational prices when competitors leave, stepping back when they return irrationally — is the systematic inverse of institutional herd behavior.

### Franchise holders capture tax cuts; price-competitors pass them through

The Tax Reform Act of 1986 prompts the clearest statement yet of how competitive structure determines who captures cost improvements. When a tax cut reduces corporate costs: regulated utilities pass it through formally; price-competitive industries pass it through via market forces; unregulated franchise businesses keep it as profit, because their prices are set by value delivered, not cost. Buffett's test: "Do you really expect the fees of this expert (the local 'franchise-holder' in his or her specialty) to be reduced now that the top personal tax rate is being cut from 50% to 28%?" Franchise holders' prices are insulated from their cost structure; commodity businesses' prices are not.

This framework, applied here to analyzing the Act's impact on Berkshire's various businesses, is the direct seedbed of the fuller franchise-vs-business canonical essay in the [berkshire-1991-letter](berkshire-1991-letter.md), where the three-condition franchise test is formally systematized.

### Permanent partial ownership declared as explicit policy

Berkshire's three major equity positions — Capital Cities/ABC, GEICO, The Washington Post — hold a combined market value of over $1.7 billion while delivering only ~$9 million in annual dividend income. The apparent yield is irrelevant; the retained earnings compound in the businesses and are expected to flow back as capital gains. "We expect to keep permanently our three primary holdings... Even if these securities were to appear significantly overpriced, we would not anticipate selling them."

This declaration does double duty: it explains the contrarian discipline (the market price is not the signal to act on) and it provides the conceptual foundation for what the [berkshire-1990-letter](berkshire-1990-letter.md) will formalize as look-through earnings — permanent partial ownership only makes sense if the retained earnings you never receive as dividends still count toward your return. The corollary critique of institutional hyperactivity: "Bull markets can obscure mathematical laws, but they cannot repeal them." Stockholders as a whole must underperform the businesses they own, because of friction costs — the mathematical reason that passive, permanent ownership beats the trading-room norm.

## Notable quotes

> These represent (a) reported earnings plus (b) depreciation, depletion, amortization, and certain other non-cash charges such as Company N's items (1) and (4) less (c) the average annual amount of capitalized expenditures for plant and equipment, etc. that the business requires to fully maintain its long-term competitive position and its unit volume.

> "Cash Flow" is meaningless in such businesses as manufacturing, retailing, extractive companies, and utilities because, for them, (c) is always significant.

> The difference between GEICO's costs and those of its competitors is a kind of moat that protects a valuable and much-sought-after business castle.

> What's needed is not more statesmen, but less corn.
