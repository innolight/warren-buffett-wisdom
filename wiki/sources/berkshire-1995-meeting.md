# Berkshire Hathaway 1995 Annual Meeting

The 1995 meeting is the one that gives the **moat-and-castle metaphor** its fullest spoken form — "a wide and long-lasting moat … protecting a terrific economic castle with an honest lord in charge" (M25), with Munger translating it into the textbook economics of low agency cost and economies of scale. Around that center, Buffett and Munger spend ~80 unscripted exchanges restating the durable Berkshire system: that capital allocation is a discipline you teach by charging managers for money; that valuation is future cash judged in your head and protected by a margin of safety, not a spreadsheet; that the circle of competence is a fence you do not climb over even for Bill Gates; and that the cheapest defense against ruin is to deal only with honest people and never try to win a loss back the way you lost it. Per the wiki's two-layer convention, a short distilled top of the durable themes precedes a **comprehensive Q&A digest** that captures every value-bearing exchange.

## How to read the Q&A digest

- **★** marks a timeless, load-bearing exchange worth citing in the future; unmarked entries still carry investor value but are secondary or period-specific.
- Answers are attributed separately to **Buffett** and **Munger** — a Munger line appears only when he added a distinct lesson (he answered "Nothing to add" to most). This keeps the wisdom fairly credited to both.
- **M#/A#** map to the numbered items in the raw morning/afternoon transcript for traceability.

## Key themes

### The moat protects an economic castle — and most moats aren't worth a damn

The meeting's flagship image: business is "a business with a wide and long-lasting moat around it … protecting a terrific economic castle with an honest lord in charge of the castle." The honest lord matters because, Munger glosses, "the honest lord is low agency cost"; the moat itself is "by and large, advantages of scale — scale of market dominance … [or] scale of intelligence." The hard part is durability, because "all moats are subject to attack in a capitalistic system … most moats aren't worth a damn." Buffett's durability test is whether a business has to be "smart once" (the southern newspaper publisher who credited his success to "monopoly and nepotism"; or owning a network-TV affiliate 30 years ago, where "the one decision … overcame almost any deficiency") or has to "stay smart" (retailing, where competitors are "in your store the next day"). Munger's Title Insurance & Trust cautionary tale (A9) is the proof the moat can vanish: a virtual monopoly whose hand-maintained title plant was made worthless by the computer, until "the aggregate earnings of all the title insurance companies combined went below zero." See [moat](../concepts/moat.md), [franchise-vs-business](../concepts/franchise-vs-business.md).

### Capital allocation is a discipline you teach by charging for money

Buffett charges wholly-owned managers "between 14 percent and 20 percent" for incremental capital and credits them at the same rate when they release it, because "we want our managers to understand just how highly we do value capital … money costs money." The point isn't the number, it's the behavioral fix: "sometimes managers, when using other people's money, start thinking of it a little bit like free money," and "a batting average that does not include a cost of capital is a phony batting average" (M7). The discipline scales up into opportunity cost: every new idea is measured against the best one you already own — Coca-Cola as a "measuring stick" that "screens out … 99 percent of what you see," an "enormous thought conserver" that institutions throw away when they mandate "2 percent in international equities" (A43). Berkshire's own contribution to a subsidiary is mostly negative: redeploying capital the business can't use, supplying capital it can, and otherwise — Munger — "what we don't do" (A34). See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md), [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).

### Valuation is future cash judged in your head — with a margin of safety, not three decimals

Asked how many years out he discounts, Buffett confesses there is "no piece of paper" — "we really like the decision to be obvious enough to us that it doesn't require making a detailed calculation" (M21); Munger compares it to a Nobel laureate who banned the calculator because "we're picking up these great nuggets of gold just by organized common sense." Book value is "virtually … not a consideration at all" because the best businesses earn high returns on little capital (M31). What counts is the *future*: Coke, bought in 1988–89 at ~$11, now earns more than a fifth of that per year (~$2.30–2.40, so the $11 cost is "under five times this year's earnings") — "It's the future that counts … go where the puck is going to be, not where it is" (A47). Because precision is an illusion, "there ought to be such a margin of safety that … you don't have to carry it out to three decimal places." See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [intrinsic-value-vs-book-value](../concepts/intrinsic-value-vs-book-value.md), [margin-of-safety](../concepts/margin-of-safety.md).

### Stay inside the circle — and refuse to enlarge it, even for genius

The first question Buffett asks of any investment is "can I understand it?" (M33). He declines high-tech wholesale — even Microsoft, even with Gates as a friend ("There's no chance we'll be in businesses we don't understand," A24) — while insisting Graham's principles "are perfectly valid when applied to high-tech companies … it's that we don't know how to do it" (M10). The honesty extends to admitting an entire domain is beyond him: "Foreign exchange baffles me, frankly" (A28). The governing rule: "if we have trouble finding things within our circle, we will not enlarge the circle. … we'll wait." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

### Pay for owner-level results; let the .400 hitters swing

Berkshire runs "no two businesses that have the same arrangement" — capital charges where capital matters, none where it doesn't, thresholds tuned to each business's difficulty (A27). There is no company-wide formula and "no budgets that come up to headquarters": "We let .400 hitters swing the way they want to swing," because most managers are already wealthy and the job is to keep the work more fun "than anything else they can do in life." The two standing instructions to every manager: "think like an owner" and "tell us the bad news immediately, because good news takes care of itself" (M28). See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md), [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

### Honest accounting and honest people are a filter, not a nicety

On the FASB's retreat over expensing stock options, Buffett is scathing — managements "used a lot of muscle in Washington" to keep "the value of pi … at three instead of 3.14," and "the most intelligent form of capital formation follows from the most accurate form of accounting" (M17); Munger: "corruption won." The corollary is a clean decision rule for investors: "when the accounting confuses you, I would just tend to forget about it as a company … we have never had any great investment results from companies whose accounting we regarded as suspect" (A11). And the same filter governs partners: "you can't make a good deal with a bad person," so Berkshire skips the due-diligence armor and simply avoids bad actors (A31). See [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md), [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md), [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

### Don't try to make it back the way you lost it

The behavioral spine of the meeting, stated on USAir: "you don't have to make it back the way you lost it … in fact, it's usually a mistake" (M11). Munger ties it to gambling — "that's the reason so many people are ruined … they get behind and then they feel they have to get it back the way they lost it." Buffett's companion insight removes the emotion: "the stock does not know that you own it"; your purchase price, who told you, how you feel — "it doesn't give a damn." By the afternoon (A48) USAir is simply "a mistake," softened only by "the return *of* principal" that the dividends provided. See [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md), [turnarounds-seldom-turn](../concepts/turnarounds-seldom-turn.md).

## Q&A digest — Morning session

**M3 · Preferred stock is just another currency — dilution comes from value, not form.** *Buffett:* authorizing preferred adds a tax-friendly acquisition currency for sellers who want a fixed or adjustable income rather than common; the form is neutral because "we will equate the value of cash, versus a straight preferred, versus … common stock." Dilution happens "only if we receive less in value than we give" — true of cash too. Issuing stock worth more than you receive is John Medlin's "chain letter in reverse." *Munger:* on a banker who urged issuing options because "it didn't cost anything" — like "paying them money … to behave like Judas in your very midst." See [stock-as-acquisition-currency](../concepts/stock-as-acquisition-currency.md).

**M4 · Helzberg's — a deal born on a street corner.** *Buffett:* met Barnett Helzberg (a shareholder) by chance near the Plaza; bought ~150-store Helzberg's Diamonds, which does "very close to double" competitors' sales per store. Paid largely in common stock because Barnett "has been in no hurry to write a large check to the government" — the currency flexibility (M3) made the deal possible. See [helzbergs](../entities/helzbergs.md).

**M5 · Permanence is the product Berkshire sells.** *Buffett:* putting family on the board creates "as long a term and permanent ownership structure as can really be done," which is what a seller who "built a business since 1915" is really buying — not just price, but knowing who they "deliver thousands of people … to" and that no consultant will later say "this doesn't fit our strategic plan." See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md), [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**★ M7 · "Money costs money" — and a phony batting average.** *Buffett:* charges wholly-owned managers 14–20% on incremental capital and credits the same rate on capital released, so they don't treat other people's money as "free money"; HQ sees no capital budgets. "A batting average that does not include a cost of capital is a phony batting average." See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md), [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).

**M8 · The two — and only two — reasons to buy insurance.** *Buffett:* (1) to cover "a loss that you are unable or unwilling to bear yourself," or (2) because the policy is mispriced cheap in your favor. Berkshire sells the first kind and avoids the second; note the agency distortion — a CEO over-insures so he never has to tell his board a loss was uninsured. See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**★ M9 · Catastrophe insurance: ease of entry vs. ability to pay.** *Buffett:* the cat business is "particularly attractive for promoters" — write enough earthquake cover and "you'll either … have essentially no losses or … go broke," a great two-year record to sell on. Berkshire's edge is unmatchable balance sheet: "when we say we can take a billion-dollar loss, we can take a billion-dollar loss," so the largest reinsurers "come to Berkshire … because they know that we'll pay." See [super-cat-insurance](../concepts/super-cat-insurance.md), [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md).

**★ M10 · Graham's principles work in tech — we just don't.** *Buffett:* the principles "are perfectly valid when applied to high-tech companies," but outside his circle he won't apply them; Gates would "apply pretty much the same yardsticks," only with a margin of safety "adjusted for the mathematical risk." Won't enlarge the circle: "we'll wait. That's our approach." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [margin-of-safety](../concepts/margin-of-safety.md).

**★ M11 · "You don't have to make it back the way you lost it."** *Buffett:* on the USAir write-down to $89M — trying to recoup a loss in the asset that caused it is "usually a mistake"; "the stock does not know that you own it … it doesn't give a damn." *Munger:* "that's the reason so many people are ruined by gambling. They get behind and then they feel they have to get it back the way they lost it." See [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md), [usair](../entities/usair.md).

**★ M12 · Fads and the "high priests."** *Buffett:* economic value added (EVA) "has some flaws … it sort of forces itself to come out with the right answers"; fad-merchants "make them a little more complicated than needed so that you have to call in the high priest" — like writing "a 300-page book that just says, 'Listen to your customer.'" *Munger:* EVA is "way less silly than the capital assets pricing model. So … academia's improving." See [model-risk](../concepts/model-risk.md).

**★ M13 · Derivatives create risk rather than transfer it.** *Buffett:* Berkshire uses two modest types and watches counterparty risk closely, but interest-rate derivatives often serve no business purpose — "they essentially had a huge … gambling element … a creation of risk, not the transfer of risk." They've made the 1920s' 10% margin rules "mean nothing anymore." *Munger:* "If I were running the world, we wouldn't have options exchanges. The derivative transactions would be about 5 percent of what they are." See [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md), [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md).

**M14 · Salomon is unforecastable, but the preferred is money-good.** *Buffett:* can't predict a trading house's returns "two or three months, let alone … two or three years"; the $700M preferred (five annual redemption dates, cash-or-stock option) is "a hundred percent money-good," while ~6M common shares carry an ~$80–90M loss. "Giving options is generally a mistake, and accepting options is usually a good idea." See [salomon](../entities/salomon.md).

**M15 · Phil Carret, 98, "the greatest long-term investment record."** *Buffett:* found Carret 40-odd years ago in Moody's; "anybody that can get Phil to talk to them, listen carefully." A nod to durability and lifelong compounding.

**M16 · American Express: the card is everything, and the moat has narrowed.** *Buffett:* the card "will forever … become ever more competitive"; AmEx once owned travel-and-entertainment but now has "'the' card in certain areas … nothing like as broad … as before." IDS is ~a third of earnings, "but the real key will be how the card does over time." See [american-express](../entities/american-express.md).

**★ M17 · "Corruption won" on stock-option accounting.** *Buffett:* the FASB "did cave" and auditors "caved to their clients"; the most accurate accounting drives the best capital formation. Likens the lobbying to an Indiana bill to set pi at 3 because 3.14159 was "too difficult." Had Berkshire paid wages in options, the unrecorded cost might run "in the billions." *Munger:* "dishonor won … corruption won." See [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md), [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md).

**M20 · "An idiot … would have a very good record."** *Munger:* "few business operations have ever been constructed to require so little continuing intelligence in corporate headquarters … An idiot who was willing just to sit here would have a very good record." *Buffett:* the wholly-owned and partly-owned businesses run themselves; the open question after he's gone is only capital allocation. See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**★ M21 · There is no piece of paper.** *Buffett:* despite a "simple and direct equation," "there never was a piece of paper that shows what our calculation on Helzberg's or See's Candy or The Buffalo News was … we really like the decision to be obvious enough … that it doesn't require making a detailed calculation." *Munger:* the Caltech laureate who banned the calculator — "we're picking up these great nuggets of gold just by organized common sense … we're not going to resort to any damn placer mining"; "not only did we not have any staffing papers, we didn't have any staff." See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md).

**M22 · "Something will happen" — be ready.** *Buffett:* expects to use preferred within five years; closed the partnership in 1969 when nothing made sense, then 1973–74 "there were all kinds of things to do." "People will behave … just as foolishly in the future as they have in the past." See [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

**M23 · Float as flexible as equity — when net worth is large.** *Buffett:* the >$3B of float at sub-zero cost is "a very valuable asset"; with a small equity base he'd restrict its investment, but "we have so much net worth that … that float is just about as useful to us as equity money." See [float](../concepts/float.md), [cost-of-float](../concepts/cost-of-float.md).

**★ M25 · The moat, the castle, and the honest lord.** *Buffett:* find "a business with a wide and long-lasting moat … protecting a terrific economic castle with an honest lord," then judge how permanent the moat is and whether the lord will "take it all for himself." "Most moats aren't worth a damn." The "smart once" (network-TV affiliate) vs. "stay smart" (retailing) distinction. *Munger:* "the honest lord is low agency cost"; moats are "by and large, advantages of scale … of market dominance" or "scale of intelligence." See [moat](../concepts/moat.md), [franchise-vs-business](../concepts/franchise-vs-business.md).

**M27 · Berkshire gives you enough to value the subsidiaries yourself.** *Buffett:* the non-GAAP pages supply "enough information about the capital employ[ed], the margins … that you can make estimates that are probably just about as good as ours"; introduces Don Keough, Kay Graham, Tom Murphy — three businesses with "about 6 1/2 billion of profit … so far." See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md), [washington-post](../entities/washington-post.md), [capital-cities](../entities/capital-cities.md).

**M28 · Bad news immediately; think like an owner.** *Buffett:* the only two instructions to managers — and the standing interest in bad news, since "good news takes care of itself." Salomon's culture clash is the inevitable "tension … between compensation to the people that work there and compensation to the owners," sharpest in airlines where labor contracts leave "capital [to] receive any compensation." See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md), [salomon](../entities/salomon.md).

**M29 · Read the second edition of *Security Analysis*.** *Buffett:* prefers the 1940 second edition (cheaper than the first, "basically the same book," the last Graham wrote at 100%); later editions drifted from "both Graham's thinking and … his way of expressing himself." See [ben-graham](../people/ben-graham.md).

**★ M30 · Berkshire doesn't sell good businesses.** *Buffett:* the refusal "goes back a long way," predates the size problem, and costs little — "we don't see a reason to go around ending friendships." It's an explicit, enunciated limitation: "that's part of what you buy here." *Munger:* "I don't think there's any other corporation in America that has done as well … if you measure the human quality of the people who are in it. … why would we tinker with it?" See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**★ M31 · Graham's three principles outlast any formula.** *Buffett:* book value "is not a consideration … at all." The durable core of Graham is three ideas: the attitude toward the market (Ch. 8 of *The Intelligent Investor*), the margin of safety, and seeing "stocks as businesses." With those, "the exact … evaluation technique you use is not really that important." Walter Schloss "hewed much more" to Graham's statistical style and has "an absolutely sensational record" — on smaller sums. See [margin-of-safety](../concepts/margin-of-safety.md), [walter-schloss](../people/walter-schloss.md).

**★ M32 · Don't believe projections.** *Munger:* projections "on average, do more harm than good in America," prepared by "people who have an interest in a particular outcome" — "a mine is a hole in the ground owned by a liar … a projection prepared … by anybody with a commission … will frequently be a lie," and the worst kind is when the preparer "has gotten to believe it himself." *Buffett:* "we've never looked at a projection"; once asked that a forecaster's past projections be shown alongside the new one — "a very rude act … regarded as apostasy." See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), [institutional-imperative](../concepts/institutional-imperative.md).

**★ M33 · The first question: can I understand it?** *Buffett:* "the first question is, can I understand it?" — then "does it look like it has good economics? Has it earned high returns on capital?" *Munger:* "we tend to judge by the past record … if the thing has a lousy past record and a bright future, we're going to miss the opportunity." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [return-on-equity-yardstick](../concepts/return-on-equity-yardstick.md).

## Q&A digest — Afternoon session

**A1 · Banks sit inside the circle; the press is usually wrong.** *Buffett:* "no different … criteria we apply to banks than to other businesses" and "no sector allocation theories whatsoever"; bought the Illinois National Bank in 1969. "Maybe half … of the reports about our activities … in the press are erroneous," and SEC holdings publish over a year late. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [wells-fargo](../entities/wells-fargo.md).

**★ A3 · Coca-Cola is "doing exactly the right thing" with cash — and so is the no-dividend rule.** *Buffett:* the right sequence is reinvest in the business, then pay a dividend, then "repurchase shares in a big way"; Berkshire pays nothing only "as long as we can use … every dollar we retain to produce more than a dollar of … market value." Admires Gates's discipline holding $4.5B cash and "not doing something unintelligent with it." See [dividend-policy](../concepts/dividend-policy.md), [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md), [coca-cola](../entities/coca-cola.md).

**A4 · Wall Street pay: market rate on people vs. market rate on capital.** *Buffett:* you pay market rates to retain people, but the test is whether you're "getting a market rate on capital" — harder to measure short-term; when pay exceeds the business's earning power, "capital will flow away," as in airlines stuck on "contractual rates." Goldman partners "were paid nothing" in 1994 yet mostly stayed, having a longer horizon. *Munger:* putting in a new comp system before "a very bad year" guarantees blame; "Wall Street generally has more envy-jealousy effects than … elsewhere." (Folds the A5 envy aside: envy is "the only one of the sins that you could never possibly have any fun at.") See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).

**★ A6 · Cash is a residual, never a market call.** *Buffett:* "Cash at Berkshire is a residual … we would like to have no cash at all times." Cash on the balance sheet is "an acknowledgement … that we have not found anything … attractive at that point. It's never a policy of ours to hold a lot of cash" — and never a bet that the market will fall. See [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md), [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md).

**A7 · Newspapers: still exceptional, no longer bulletproof.** *Buffett:* the economics "aren't quite as good as … 15 years ago" — an "exceptionally good business, but … not quite as bulletproof"; still, "I can't think of many other businesses that … I would rather own than a newspaper in a single-newspaper town." *Munger:* proprietors are "getting a touch of paranoia for the first time" over the electronic revolution and young non-readers; people "don't seem to care what floor they're at, just whether the elevator is going up or down." See [media-economics](../concepts/media-economics.md).

**A8 · Berkshire itself began as a semi-hostile purchase.** *Buffett:* 40 years ago Berkshire's chairman Malcolm Chace favored the buy while president Seabury Stanton "would not have been in favor of it" — "not hostile, but …" He has no fixed policy on hostile bids reaching a board he sits on. *Munger:* "I don't think our behavior is totally predictable." See [berkshire-hathaway](../entities/berkshire-hathaway.md).

**★ A9 · Graham vs. Fisher — and a moat that vanished.** *Buffett:* "a hundred percent Ben Graham" on the three principles and "a hundred percent Phil Fisher" where they don't contradict; Graham's statistical style is "more teachable" but "not workable with really large sums," and "if you own a wonderful business … you don't want turnover." *Munger:* "a very great many" of Fisher's wonderful businesses "didn't last" — Title Insurance & Trust dominated California until the computer let rivals build a title plant "for a few million dollars," and industry-wide earnings "went below zero — starting with a virtual monopoly … technology is sometimes your friend and … sometimes your bitter enemy." See [moat](../concepts/moat.md), [concentration-vs-diversification](../concepts/concentration-vs-diversification.md).

**A10 · Munger's share sales — plain candor.** *Munger:* "I gave away the Berkshire because I thought it was the right way to behave, and I sold some because I had uses for the money." No hidden signal — "He doesn't know anything I don't know."

**★ A11 · Confusing accounting? Walk away.** *Buffett:* "when the accounting confuses you, I would just tend to forget about it as a company … we have never had any great investment results from companies whose accounting we regarded as suspect"; suspicious prepaid/deferred or bloated inventories, and life insurers, deserve a second look. "Accounting can offer you a lot of insight into the character of management." *Munger:* "I made a short sale once that worked out well … in a case like that." See [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md), [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md).

**A12 · Lloyd's slipped on "slop and folly."** *Buffett:* Lloyd's "lost its relative position … significantly" on bad results that drove capital out; Berkshire's worst-ever insurance problem came from certain Lloyd's syndicates ~20 years ago — "an early lesson in what was coming." *Munger:* "too many commissions coming off the top … too much fine tailoring and three-hour lunches with fine wines." See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**A13 · Retain a dollar only if it makes more than a dollar.** *Buffett:* Berkshire has essentially never repurchased shares, judging that retaining a dollar to "turn dollar bills into something larger" beats it — measured "based on whether we can find anything in a couple of years," not this week. See [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md), [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md).

**A14 · A foreign business is "not too likely."** *Buffett:* ~35–40% of Berkshire's net worth already earns abroad through Coke (80%+ foreign) and Gillette (~two-thirds); but a wholly-owned foreign business is unlikely — he doubts he'd "develop as much confidence in … the management" or "the moat." (Folds A20: "we're open to buying anything … the domicile is not that important," but less likely.) See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [look-through-earnings](../concepts/look-through-earnings.md).

**A16 · GEICO's falling ROE is arithmetic, not decay.** *Buffett:* capital has grown faster than premiums and invested assets, so equal underwriting and investment success "will produce a lower return on capital"; "it's a very good business … but … not a business where, if you double the capital, you can double the earnings easily." See [geico](../entities/geico.md), [return-on-equity-yardstick](../concepts/return-on-equity-yardstick.md).

**A21 · Helzberg's, and the seller's "schizophrenia."** *Buffett:* won't disclose deal figures (sales ~$280M); the typical jewelry store "is not a very good business," but high sales per square foot and "a terrific manager" (Jeff Comment) make it work. *Munger:* sellers want "a little more than it's worth, taking stock," from a buyer who will then "make … just one dumb acquisition — theirs — and thereafter … guard the stock like gold. … You do not want to sell your business for stock to a firm that likes issuing stock." See [helzbergs](../entities/helzbergs.md), [stock-as-acquisition-currency](../concepts/stock-as-acquisition-currency.md).

**★ A22 · Float is the hidden lever in intrinsic value.** *Buffett:* the float page "is probably as important a page as there is in the report"; intrinsic value is "future cash discounted back to the present," with capital allocation "a good part of that." Had they foreseen National Indemnity's float growth from ~$15–20M in 1967, "the intrinsic value … was many multiples of what most people might have thought at the time." See [float](../concepts/float.md), [intrinsic-value-vs-book-value](../concepts/intrinsic-value-vs-book-value.md), [national-indemnity](../entities/national-indemnity.md).

**A24 · No exception, even for Gates.** *Buffett:* bought 100 Microsoft shares personally to get the reports; "There's no chance we'll be in businesses we don't understand, and I won't understand it." Gates "would be a very good guy to make an exception with. But I don't think I'll make an exception." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ A25 · Why don't business schools study Mrs. B?** *Buffett:* Rose Blumkin built a great enterprise from "$500 in 1937 … without a day in school," with no invention and no protected real estate — "something has made her a success … not something that's incapable of being replicated in the habits and the way of thinking," yet schools "present her as a curiosity" while teaching EVA. The deeper puzzle is the "incredible resistance to thinking or change," even when learning is in one's self-interest — "Most men would rather die than think. Many have" (Russell). *Munger:* "It's extraordinary how resistant some people are to learning anything." See [nebraska-furniture-mart](../entities/nebraska-furniture-mart.md), [rose-blumkin](../people/rose-blumkin.md).

**A26 · Do what you like now.** *Buffett:* rejects "I'll take this job I don't like now, and I'll get one I like later" — "there aren't that many years around, so you ought to be doing what you like at the present time." *Munger:* on how much Buffett has changed in 10 years — "about one stone."

**★ A27 · Let the .400 hitters swing their own way.** *Buffett:* "no two businesses that have the same arrangement" — capital charges only where capital matters, thresholds tuned to difficulty, "no budgets that come up to headquarters"; managers must feel "they own their own business," because an independently wealthy seller "would tell us what we could do with it very quickly" if told how to swing. See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md), [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**A28 · "Foreign exchange baffles me, frankly."** *Buffett:* thinks in purchasing-power parity but it "does not work very well as a guide" short or even long term because "the world adapts in different ways." *Munger:* "I have no comment." A clean naming of a domain outside the circle. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ A29 · The small investor's structural edge.** *Buffett:* Berkshire no longer looks at small caps ("I would first look at the size"), but "if you're working with very small amounts of money … there almost always are some significant inefficiency"; as a young man he "went through all of the Moody's manuals … page by page … 20,000 pages," finding bargains "just plain overlooked." See [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md).

**A30 · Cigar butt: the Delta Duck Club.** *Buffett:* Atled ("Delta" backwards), 98 shares, oil under a duck-club's Louisiana land; sold at ~$29,000 while holding ~$20,000/share cash plus ~$11,000 pretax royalties — "is that an efficient market or not? … I don't think so." *Munger:* "When you get down to one duck club membership … you're really scavenging for cigar butts." See [cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses](../concepts/cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses.md), [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md).

**★ A31 · "You can't make a good deal with a bad person."** *Buffett:* Berkshire skips contracts and due-diligence armor and simply avoids bad actors — "we can do fine over time, dealing with people that we like, and admire, and trust." *Munger:* his lawyer father's lesson that the wonderful client Grant McFayden "doesn't have enough law business to keep you in school" because he "extricates himself" from troublesome people, while the "overreaching blowhard" is "a walking minefield … a wonderful client for a lawyer." See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

**A32 · Why one Borsheims, but many Helzberg's.** *Buffett:* Borsheims' single location ($50M+ retail inventory) drives selection and volume that cut operating costs "20 percentage points" and feed lower prices — "very hard to replicate … trying to do it in 10 spots probably wouldn't work well." Sol Price's lesson: success came from "figuring out the customer he didn't want." See [borsheims](../entities/borsheims.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**A33 · American-business ROE clusters at 12–13%.** *Buffett:* the long-run figure has been pushed up lately partly by health-liability charges shrinking equity (a backdoor leverage), but "competitive factors tend to … keep pushing that number down"; 12–13% with 7% rates still makes equity "worth much more … than elsewhere." *Munger:* published averages overstate it — survivors selling at high multiples, losers "constantly being dropped out." *Buffett:* unexpensed options shave "two or three-tenths of a percent." See [return-on-equity-yardstick](../concepts/return-on-equity-yardstick.md), [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md).

**★ A34 · Berkshire's contribution: move the capital, then get out of the way.** *Buffett:* in a high-return business with no internal use for cash, "we actually may contribute significantly … by taking the capital out" and redeploying it across "the whole universe"; conversely it can fund a Helzberg's growing 25% on 20% equity. It also frees managers from "committee meetings … show-and-tell stuff." *Munger:* "I think our chief contribution to the businesses we acquire is what we don't do." See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md), [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**★ A35 · Numeracy, not advanced math.** *Buffett:* "advanced math is of no use in the investment process"; what helps is "a numeracy" that tells you "when things make sense or don't," plus reading "everything in sight … a few hundred annual reports a year." *Munger:* Value Line's "stuff on the log scale paper going back 15 years … is the best one-shot description of a lot of big businesses … I can't imagine anybody being in the investment business … without that thing on the shelf." See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md).

**★ A36 · Wells Fargo: a bet on management quality, not the numbers.** *Buffett:* knew Reichardt and Hazen "were certainly different than the typical banker"; those "human differences … are what enabled them to come through" the real-estate collapse — "we couldn't have told that … if we hadn't gone a little further … than just looking at numbers." *Munger:* with the "biggest collapse in 40 or 50 years" in real estate, an average loss rate "would've [made] the place … broke" — "we were basically betting that their real estate lending was way better than average. And indeed, it was." See [wells-fargo](../entities/wells-fargo.md), [lending-discipline](../concepts/lending-discipline.md).

**A37 · Negative shareholders' equity isn't dangerous.** *Buffett:* "nothing magic about whether shareholder equity is positive or negative" — Coca-Cola ($5B equity, $75B market value) could buy in $10B of stock to negative equity with sound credit; "you should buy in your stock when you don't have a use for the money," more compellingly the bigger the discount to intrinsic value. *Munger:* maybe fine for Coca-Cola, "but I don't think it would be a good idea for General Motors." See [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md), [intrinsic-value-vs-book-value](../concepts/intrinsic-value-vs-book-value.md).

**A38 · Credit cards: you need an edge in some segment.** *Buffett:* the growth of card usage "is not a big factor with us"; what matters is that customers "are quite willing to shift cards," so "you better have some way of playing one part of the game better than others or natural capitalistic forces are going to grind you down." See [american-express](../entities/american-express.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**A39 · Banking will change a lot over 20 years.** *Buffett:* branch value is "diminish[ing] significantly"; over 20–30 years banking will change "in some very major ways," with Microsoft and others eyeing the market — "they may figure out a way to go around the present players." *Munger:* Merrill Lynch already "went around the rest" with cash-management accounts; recommends Joe Nocera's history of the credit card. See [media-economics](../concepts/media-economics.md).

**A42 · The national debt isn't frightening relative to GDP.** *Buffett:* net debt ~60% of GDP vs. ~125–150% after WWII, which proved "quite sustainable"; "you can't talk about debt levels without relating it to the ability to pay" — the country also owns a 35% "profits interest" in all corporations via the tax rate. Dislikes debt rising *as a share* of GDP because "that sets a lot of things in motion." *Munger:* the real danger is a "Serpico effect … where you start rewarding what you don't want more of." Largely period-bound macro. See [bonds-and-inflation](../concepts/bonds-and-inflation.md).

**★ A43 · Your best opportunity is your measuring stick.** *Buffett:* "if I'm going to look at another business I will say … 'Why would I rather have this than more Coca-Cola?'" Institutions that allot "3 percent in emerging growth countries … some damn thing like that" abandon the comparison that should screen out 99% of ideas. *Munger:* "the best thing you have easily available is your measuring stick … an enormous thought conserver … not taught in the business schools," and Nietzsche "laughed at the man who thought he could walk better because he had a lame leg." See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md), [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md).

**A44 · Berkshire is many baskets, but tax-set for "forever."** *Buffett:* owning Berkshire isn't "all eggs in one basket" given its many good businesses (though it is one legal entity for liability purposes); "if you buy Berkshire, you should only think about buying it for a very long period of time" — it's "set up, taxwise, fine for somebody that's going to … own it forever," not for a one-year holder. See [tax-deferral](../concepts/tax-deferral.md), [concentration-vs-diversification](../concepts/concentration-vs-diversification.md).

**★ A45 · The two agency costs, and a preference for candor.** *Munger:* two costs from a manager — "he favors himself at the expense of the shareholders," or "he's foolish by nature" — "you have to judge those two aspects of human character"; but "some businesses so good that they'll easily stand a lot of folly in the managerial suite." *Buffett:* "we like people who are candid … smart … and focused on the business. It's not real complicated." See [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**A46 · Capital is captive in a bad business.** *Munger:* "approximately half the investors are going to get below-average results … they didn't exactly accept it in advance." *Buffett:* money in a sub-profitable business "is likely to stay tied up," needing "a takeover, or a proxy fight" to free it — "capitalism does not operate so efficiently as to move capital around promptly when it's misallocated"; Berkshire can move it internally. See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md), [turnarounds-seldom-turn](../concepts/turnarounds-seldom-turn.md).

**★ A47 · It's the future, not the current multiple.** *Buffett:* Coke bought 1988–89 at ~$11 now earns ~$2.30–2.40 — "under five times this year's earnings, but … a pretty good size multiple back when we bought it"; "go where the puck is going to be, not where it is." Interest rates matter ("much differently if … 11 percent or 5 percent") but not "7.3, or 7.0, or 7.5." Demand "such a margin of safety that … you don't have to carry it out to three decimal places." See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [margin-of-safety](../concepts/margin-of-safety.md).

**A48 · USAir was a mistake — return *of* principal, not *on* it.** *Buffett:* flatly "It was a mistake," softened only by "five years of dividends at a good rate"; "it isn't the return *on* principal that you care about, it's the return *of* principal." See [usair](../entities/usair.md), [turnarounds-seldom-turn](../concepts/turnarounds-seldom-turn.md).

**A49 · A book recommendation.** *Munger:* recommends Max Bazerman's *Judgment in Managerial Decision Making* — used in some business schools, "not written in a spritely way … but there's a lot of wisdom in it."

**★ A50 · No stock split — protect the shareholder base.** *Buffett:* concedes the high per-share price is "anywhere from awkward to disadvantageous" for gifting, but a split would "almost … cinch" a less sophisticated, shorter-horizon shareholder base, and "you can't reconstruct it if you destroy it." The ideal is for "the stock price exactly parallel to change in intrinsic value," so no period's shareholders are treated unfairly; Berkshire's 3% turnover vs. double-digits elsewhere is the proof. *Munger:* "Look around you. Are we really likely to do a lot better? This is a good bunch." See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md), [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md).

## Connections to other sources

- The moat-and-castle metaphor here is the spoken anchor for the [moat](../concepts/moat.md) concept and a forward echo of the franchise discussion in [berkshire-1991-letter](berkshire-1991-letter.md); the "smart once vs. stay smart" axis sharpens the [franchise-vs-business](../concepts/franchise-vs-business.md) distinction.
- The USAir post-mortem (M11, A48) continues the writedown reckoning of [berkshire-1994-letter](berkshire-1994-letter.md) and the live 1994-meeting admissions ([berkshire-1994-meeting](berkshire-1994-meeting.md) M40/A13), adding the behavioral rule "don't make it back the way you lost it."
- "Money costs money" and the .400-hitter compensation philosophy (M7, A27) extend the incentive-comp and capital-charge mechanics introduced in the [berkshire-1995-letter](berkshire-1995-letter.md) (the same year's written companion, which Buffett repeatedly references from the floor).
- The stock-option fight (M17) is the spoken, blunter version of the accounting-honesty arguments running through [berkshire-1992-letter](berkshire-1992-letter.md) and [berkshire-1993-letter](berkshire-1993-letter.md); "corruption won" is the meeting-floor verdict.
- The "measuring stick" / opportunity-cost framing (A43) is the conversational seed of the look-through and capital-allocation reasoning developed across the early-1990s letters, and recurs at every later meeting.
- Helzberg's (M4, A21) and the preferred-stock authorization (M3) are the first appearance of an acquisition currency that the [berkshire-1995-letter](berkshire-1995-letter.md) formalizes; the chance street-corner origin reprises the relationship-driven sourcing seen with Nebraska Furniture Mart and Borsheims.
