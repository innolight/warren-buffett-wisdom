# Berkshire Hathaway 1998 Annual Meeting

The 1998 meeting is the **"easy decisions" meeting** — held at the top of a roaring bull market, with Berkshire "drowning in this torrent of cash" (Munger) and finding almost nothing to buy, Buffett and Munger keep returning to a single idea: you get paid the same for the easy pitch as the hard one, so swing only at the fat ones. Across ~108 unscripted exchanges they restate the load-bearing Berkshire system in unusually crisp form — that *time is the enemy of the poor business and the friend of the great one*; that a financial asset is worth only the cash it will eventually hand back (beware the Frozen Corporation and EBITDA); that volatility is not risk and, for a business you understand, is a gift; that the "cost of capital" is nothing fancier than the dollar test; and that you pay for performance, never mediocrity, and buy people who love the business more than money. Per the wiki's two-layer convention, a short distilled top of the durable themes precedes a **comprehensive Q&A digest** that captures every value-bearing exchange.

This meeting was held **May 2, 1998 — before** the General Re acquisition (announced June 1998) and the NetJets/Executive Jet purchase (announced July 1998, closing August); neither is mentioned. Both first appear in [berkshire-1998-letter](berkshire-1998-letter.md).

## How to read the Q&A digest

- **★** marks a timeless, load-bearing exchange worth citing in the future; unmarked entries still carry investor value but are secondary or period-specific.
- Answers are attributed separately to **Buffett** and **Munger** — a Munger line appears only when he added a distinct lesson (he answered "Nothing to add" to most). This keeps the wisdom fairly credited to both.
- **M#/A#** map to the numbered items in the raw morning/afternoon transcript for traceability.

## Key themes

### Quality compounds; mediocrity decays — "time is the enemy of the poor business"

The single sharpest line of the meeting: "Time is the enemy of the poor business, and it's the friend of the great business" (M10). A business earning 5–6% on equity grinds you down no matter how cheaply you bought it — "you may be lucky enough to pick the exact moment when it gets taken over by someone else," but you can't count on the liquidation. A business earning 20–25% on equity held for a long time makes time work for you. This is why Buffett abandoned the "cigar butt" approach he ran in his twenties — "where you try and find a really kind of pathetic company, but it sells so cheap that you think there's one good free puff left in it" — and why the same logic kills the Japanese-value thesis: low price-to-book ratios are *appropriate* for businesses earning 5% on book, because "earnings are what determine value, not book value" (A29). See [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md), [return-on-equity-yardstick](../concepts/return-on-equity-yardstick.md), [cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses](../concepts/cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses.md).

### Stay with the important and the knowable — and don't take the seven-foot bar

"We look for one-foot bars to step over rather than seven-foot or eight-foot bars to try and set some Olympic record" (M8) — because, unlike Olympic diving, "there's no degree of difficulty factor" in investing; you get paid just as well for the simple dive executed cleanly. The governing filter is a two-by-two: "We try to think about things that are important and things that are knowable" (M23), discarding what is knowable-but-unimportant and refusing to act on what is important-but-unknowable. Coke's 1919 IPO — $40 to $19 in a year, then world depression, world war, atomic bombs, all foreseeable and all irrelevant to the only thing that mattered: that it would sell a billion-plus servings a day. The same humility is why Buffett, an admirer of Grove and Gates, still won't touch tech: "that is a seven or eight foot bar that I can't clear … no matter how I train, I can't clear it" (M45). See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md).

### A financial asset is worth only the cash it will eventually return

Ben Graham's "Frozen Corporation" — a company forbidden ever to pay out, liquidate, or be sold — is the device Buffett uses to make the point unanswerable: such an enterprise is worth nothing, because "investing is the art, essentially, of laying out cash now to get a whole lot more cash later on, and something at some point better deliver cash" (A8). Two corollaries follow. First, "the one figure we regard as utter nonsense is the so-called EBITDA," because any business with real fixed assets must reinvest just to stay in place; depreciation is usually a fair proxy for that maintenance capex (A8, A33). Second, the *kind* of growth is everything: the best business returns more cash every year for little reinvestment; the worst "grows a lot" while forcing you to reinvest at a low rate — "and sometimes people are in those businesses without knowing about it" (A8). See [owner-earnings](../concepts/owner-earnings.md), [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md).

### Volatility is not risk — and for a business you understand, it is a gift

Asked by a Wharton student how he risk-adjusts discount rates across businesses, Buffett rejects the premise: risk is "sort of a go/no-go valve" on whether you understand the future at all, not a number you bolt onto the discount rate. "We actually would prefer what other people would call risk" (A44) — a business whose value is certain but whose *stock* swings wildly is the ideal, because the swings let you buy low and sell high on nonsense. The Washington Post falling 50% in months was "the best thing that could've happened"; if See's traded publicly and reacted to its money-losing summer months, "we would buy in July and sell in January." Munger: "What we want is the favorable odds. We figure the volatility, over time, will take care of itself" — and the obsession with beta and correlation coefficients is simply "nonsense" (A32, A44). See [risk-vs-volatility](../concepts/risk-vs-volatility.md), [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

### "Cost of capital" is just the dollar test

Asked the textbook question, Munger calls the standard teaching "incoherent" and "kind of a stupid question … when we keep drowning in this torrent of cash." Buffett supplies the operational answer: capital should be returned to owners the moment you cannot turn a retained dollar into more than a dollar of value; pass that threshold and you simply "look around for the thing that you feel the surest about, and that promises the greatest return weighted for that certainty." So "if we're keeping dollar bills that are worth more in your hands than in our hands, then we've exceeded the cost of capital" (M30). The danger isn't the math — it's that "many managements would be reluctant to distribute money to shareholders even if they would rationalize that they would do better than they actually do." See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md), [dividend-policy](../concepts/dividend-policy.md).

### Pay for performance, never mediocrity — and buy people who love the business more than money

Buffett's compensation objection is precise: "the most exorbitant are not necessarily the biggest numbers. What really bothers me is when companies pay a lot of money for mediocrity" (A2). Berkshire happily pays huge sums for outsized owner results, but the system that "feeds on itself" — every CEO refusing to be "below average," consultants ratcheting it up — is corrosive (Munger: "prostitution would be a step up for them"). The same human judgment dominates acquisitions: three times in four the seller is already rich, so the only question that matters is "whether they love the business or love money" (M56). This is why conventional due diligence is "boilerplate" that "misses the point" — "evaluating the people they're getting in with, and evaluating the economics of the business … that's 99 percent of the deal" (A58). See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md), [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md), [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

### Decentralize to the edge of abdication; centralize only capital

"We centralize money and everything else we decentralize, pretty much" (A7). No strategic-planning department, no mission statement, no operating plans submitted to headquarters, no consultants, and twelve people at headquarters running 40,000 employees (M16). Munger's gloss: "We have decentralized power in the operating businesses to a point just short of total abdication." The discipline is principled, not lazy — once headquarters starts choosing a subsidiary's vendors or second-guessing its capital spending, "then we become responsible for the operation, and they are no longer responsible" (A20). It also keeps frictional cost near zero: Berkshire's after-tax overhead is "a half a basis point of capital value," versus 125 for a typical mutual fund — which is why spinning off subsidiaries for a short-term valuation pop holds "no interest" (A47). See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

## Q&A digest — Morning session

**M7 · What moves a price-earnings ratio.** *Buffett:* relative P/Es move when the market revises a company's or industry's prospects versus all others; absolute P/Es move on expected return on equity and on interest rates — both improved since 1982, lifting stocks in aggregate. See [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md).

**★ M8 · No degree-of-difficulty bonus.** *Munger:* "if our predictions have been a little better than other people's, it's because we tried to make fewer of them." *Buffett:* investing is not Olympic diving — you get paid just as well for the simple dive done cleanly, so "we look for one-foot bars to step over." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ M9 · The efficient-market hypothesis "contaminates" business schools.** *Buffett:* EMH is "a huge edge" for those who ignore it — "if all of your competitors believe the world is flat … we should be encouraging the teaching of efficient market theories." The academic's word "anomaly" just means evidence that contradicts a cherished theory; Darwin wrote down contradictory facts immediately because the mind is conditioned to reject them. *Munger:* "the contamination was massive … but it's waning." See [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md), [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md).

**★ M10 · "Time is the enemy of the poor business."** *Buffett:* a 5–6% ROE business grinds you down however cheaply bought; a 20–25% ROE business makes time your friend — which is why he abandoned the cigar-butt approach ("one good free puff left in it"). *Munger:* "it's not that much fun to buy a business where you really hope this sucker liquidates before it goes broke." See [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md), [cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses](../concepts/cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses.md).

**★ M11 · Filters reject technology in ten seconds.** *Buffett:* the mental filters say "no" to 90%-plus of ideas instantly; anything with a significant technological component — or where future technology could hurt the business — "won't make it through the filter." *Munger:* the filters "are filters against consequences from our own lack of talent." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**M12 · The two jobs, and the "next Buffett."** *Buffett:* Berkshire holds people "leagues ahead" of him and Munger in particular talents; the two non-delegable functions are keeping already-rich managers motivated and allocating capital — "and these days we have to allocate a lot more capital than we had to allocate a decade ago." *Munger:* successors "may be different in many ways. And they may do better." See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md).

**★ M13 · The few reasons to sell.** *Buffett:* "the best thing to do is buy a stock that you don't ever want to sell"; sell only to raise money for something better, or when relative valuations get "out of whack" — though "the real thing to do with a great business is just hang on for dear life." *Munger:* "the ideal way is when you found something you like immensely better." See [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md).

**M14 · Level 3.** *Buffett:* declines to opine on the Kiewit-spawned telecom run by director Walter Scott — "you'll have to make your own judgment on the stock." (Period-specific.)

**★ M16 · Proud to have no strategic plan.** *Buffett:* no strategic-planning department, no predetermined course — "we react to what we think are opportunities." *Munger:* "I also want to say proudly that we have no mission statement." Twelve people at headquarters, 40,000 in the company — "we hope to grow a lot, but we don't hope to grow at headquarters." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**M17 · Y2K.** *Buffett:* not material to Berkshire; the weak link will be governmental units. *Munger:* "it was predictable that the year 2000 would come." (Period-specific.)

**★ M19 · "The secret of life is weak competition."** *Buffett:* bank ROEs near 30% on tangible equity are "unprecedented" and probably unsustainable — sustained 20%+ retention would make corporate profits a ludicrous share of GDP. A thought experiment: 500 cloned Jack Welches running the Fortune 500 would *self-neutralize* like 500 expert chess players in a tournament — "you still have a lot of losers." *Munger:* much of the ROE gain came from the now-popular idea of exiting non-leading businesses and buying back shares "even at very high prices." See [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md), [institutional-imperative](../concepts/institutional-imperative.md).

**M20 · Reading list.** *Munger:* Jared Diamond's *Guns, Germs and Steel* (read twice) — "the way the guy's mind works would be useful in business … always asking why." *Buffett:* *The Quotable Einstein* and a book on Fermat's theorem.

**M21 · Freddie/Fannie and rates.** *Buffett:* less rate-sensitive than once thought, but very low rates that fill a portfolio with 4% mortgages, then a sharp rise, "would be quite painful … no matter what you've done in the way of hedging" — the savings-and-loan problem at giant scale. (Period-specific.)

**M22 · What keeps you awake.** *Buffett:* asks every manager and target "what their nightmare is" (Grove's "silver bullet"), but personally "I can't think of anything I'm worried about at Berkshire … if you're worried about something, the thing to do is get it corrected and get back to sleep." *Munger:* in 30 years, the only thing that costs Warren sleep "is an illness in the family … even in the periods that look tough to other people, it's a lot of fun."

**★ M23 · "What is important and what is knowable?"** *Buffett:* the master filter — discard the knowable-but-unimportant, refuse to act on the important-but-unknowable, and act only where the two overlap. Coke's 1919 IPO ($40→$19 in year one) is worthless to time but priceless to anyone who saw a billion servings a day coming. *Munger:* "we're predicting the currents that will come, just how some things will swim in the currents." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**M24 · Value Line's "perfect snapshot."** *Buffett:* the quickest way to scan key factors on 1,700 stocks every 13 weeks — "we don't care about the ratings … we're looking for facts." Price action itself is "chicken tracks." *Munger:* the charts "are a human triumph … if I were running a business school we would be teaching from Value Line charts." See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md).

**M25 · GEICO unaffected by insurance mergers.** *Buffett:* no merger has hurt GEICO or the reinsurance business — "GEICO operating just as it does, independently, is as competitive as can be." Davidson built it, Byrne rescued it, "Tony Nicely's got it going down the track at about a hundred miles an hour." See [geico](../entities/geico.md).

**★ M26 · Wonderful companies should buy back stock — even at "nose-bleed" prices.** *Buffett:* in Coke's 112 years there were "very few times … when it would not have been smart" to repurchase; Berkshire's Coke stake rose from 6.3% to over 8% through buybacks. He'd prefer they buy at 15× than 40×, but "when we own stock in a wonderful business, we like the idea of repurchases, even at prices that may give you nose bleeds." Damns the option-funded version: "they sell low and then they buy high." *Munger:* a price *can* get high enough to make a buyback foolish — the Insull utilities buying their own shares were "a giant Ponzi scheme at the end." See [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md).

**★ M27 · Float has a negative cost — worth more than equity.** *Buffett:* ~$7B of float comes "with a profit attached," so offered the chance to swap it for $7B of tax-free equity, "we would turn down that proposition." The key is "not what the float is today … [but] what is the float going to be 10 or 15 years from now, and what is the cost." *Munger:* "If the float keeps growing, that is a wonderful thing indeed … it's safer on the downside and has a better upside." *Supersession flag:* the optimism that cost would stay "close to our present level" was overtaken within months — the General Re acquisition (announced June 1998) drove Berkshire's float to a meaningfully *positive* cost for several years (1999–2002) before discipline restored it. See [float](../concepts/float.md), [cost-of-float](../concepts/cost-of-float.md).

**M28 · "And then what?" on Japan dumping Treasurys.** *Buffett:* a net exporter to the U.S. must receive *some* U.S. asset in exchange — "they can't get out of the system." The one question to always ask in economics is "And then what?" because there are two sides to every transaction. *Munger:* "If I owned Japan, I would want a large holding of U.S. Treasurys." (Macro, but a durable second-order-thinking lesson.)

**M29 · Social Security in stocks.** *Buffett:* wouldn't drop the safety net below ~5/6, and wouldn't "turn an army of salespeople loose" on a mandatory 2%. *Munger:* "I don't think we need to get the government into the equity market" (citing Japan's postal-savings equity buying). (Period/policy.)

**★ M30 · "Cost of capital" is just the dollar test.** *Munger:* the business-school teaching is "incoherent"; a "kind of a stupid question … when we keep drowning in this torrent of cash." *Buffett:* return capital the moment you can't make a retained dollar worth more than a dollar; past that, "look around for the thing that you feel the surest about." See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md).

**M31 · Prefer individual shareholders.** *Buffett:* wants holders who "figure out for themselves why they … want to buy Berkshire," who will "stick around"; individuals are "often less rational holders … with individuals" reversed — money made "translates into changes in people's lives" not a quarter's performance figure. *Munger:* institutions avoid it because it's "perceived as hard to buy in quantity." See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**★ M32 · "The earthquake doesn't know the premium you receive."** *Buffett:* super-cat policies can be mispriced at half their value — even at a *dollar* a year you'd "think you made money 70-odd percent of the time," because rare events hide mispricing for years. Catastrophe bonds and hedge-fund capacity (manager takes 20% of the no-hurricane years, limited partners eat the loss) will cut Berkshire's volume but never its prices — the questioner's line that you "can never be smarter than your dumbest competitor," which Buffett endorsed ("You've got it"), bites on volume, not price. See [super-cat-insurance](../concepts/super-cat-insurance.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**★ M33 · What Buffett wants in an annual report.** *Buffett:* a report that tells owners "what we would want to know if we owned a hundred percent" in candid, plain language; "public relations gobbledygook … lots of pictures and no facts" is a negative on the business itself. Berkshire bought Coke purely on its annual report, "not based on any conversation … with top management." *Munger:* what you seldom see is "This is a very serious problem and we haven't quite figured out yet how to handle it." See [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md), [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**M35 · Ignore asset gains at Coke; two variables drive value.** *Buffett:* bottling-transaction gains are "incidental" — "the two important elements in Coke are unit case sales and shares outstanding." Take asset gains "out of the picture." See [coca-cola](../entities/coca-cola.md), [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md).

**★ M36 · Stock options quietly degrade earnings quality.** *Buffett:* for some companies, true earnings are "maybe 10 percent less per year per share than reported" once option cost is charged — "dramatically poorer" quality. The cure is businesses you can compute simply: Coke is earnings-per-case × cases ÷ shares; GEICO is policies-in-force × underwriting-per-policy, "and that is exactly the way … we pay people there." See [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md), [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md), [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).

**★ M37 · How Buffett would teach valuation.** *Buffett:* "one valuation study after another," identifying the key variables and *first* judging how predictable they are — "if something is not very predictable, forget it." The dozen-odd big decisions worked because "they just weren't that complicated … we knew we were focusing on the right variables." *Munger:* trying to appraise *any* company like real estate, future versus price, "you're attempting the impossible." *Buffett:* he'd flunk any student who put a value on an internet company. See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**M38 · Wesco is a "historical accident."** *Munger:* per unit of book value, Wesco is worth "way less than Berkshire … it is not a clone." *Buffett:* anyone wanting a tax-free merger comes to Berkshire, and the big ideas fit Berkshire; Wesco gets the rare exception (Kansas Bankers Surety). See [wesco-financial](../entities/wesco-financial.md).

**M39 · Buy Berkshire, or the stocks it owns?** *Buffett:* "you have to really make up your own mind"; Berkshire makes no portfolio recommendations and gives you the information to value each subsidiary yourself (M40). *Munger:* "Amazingly, we hate it when people … follow us around buying what we buy." See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**M41 · Three factors driving the bull market.** *Buffett:* improved ROE and falling rates (both fundamental) plus self-feeding momentum; mutual-fund money pours in on "very unrealistic expectations." *Munger:* "you won't have 18 more years of 17 percent or 18 percent per annum. That I think we can virtually guarantee." See [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md).

**M43 · Disney/Cap Cities accounting (vs. Abe Briloff).** *Buffett:* disagrees with Briloff on amortization of intangibles — adding back Disney's ~$400M of (non-deductible) intangible amortization, "the economic earnings of Disney … might well be more than the reported earnings." *Munger:* "I don't think you can criticize Disney's accounting." See [disney](../entities/disney.md), [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md).

**M44 · Wants the pooling-vs-purchase distinction abolished.** *Buffett:* identical economics should not produce different reported numbers; managements doing "all kinds of acrobatics … to get pooling accounting" is "nuts," and "if accounting is pushing people to doing things that are nuts … it's time for accounting to look at itself." See [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md).

**★ M45 · "I'll never know enough to buy tech stocks."** *Buffett:* admires Grove and Gates but "I don't know what that world will look like in 10 years … that is a seven or eight foot bar that I can't clear." A lot of money made by others "doesn't bother me … it's much better for us to swing at the easy pitches." *Munger:* "Whatever you think you know about technology, I think I know less." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**M46 · "Missed the boat" on Berkshire buybacks.** *Buffett:* "a valid criticism" that Berkshire didn't repurchase when it should have — but at those times the money usually bought something else that "did very well," and "we've never wanted to leverage up." *Munger:* "I would agree with both comments." *Supersession flag:* Berkshire would not adopt a formal repurchase program until 2011. See [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md).

**M48 · Why Buffett bought silver.** *Buffett:* annual demand (~800M oz) outruns mine-plus-reclaimed supply (~650M oz), and depleted above-ground bullion means "the present price … does not produce an equilibrium" — with inelastic byproduct supply, the eventual price change "would [not] necessarily be minor." *Munger:* "this whole episode will have about as much impact … as Warren's bridge playing" — discipline to wait "three or four decades, waiting for a chance to employ 2 percent of your assets." *Supersession flag:* a rare case where Buffett's directional thesis was right but early — Berkshire sold its silver around 2005–06, near cost, before the post-2008 run-up. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**M50 · If forced to split, keep the operating businesses.** *Buffett:* would give up the marketable securities "because it's more fun … I like being involved with real people." *Munger:* "I'll be in a hell of a fix if I am not in the same arena." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**M51 · Fine even if the top 25 dropped dead.** *Buffett:* the directors hold a letter naming successors for both the operating and capital-allocation roles, which "can change over time"; 99%-plus of his estate stays in Berkshire stock "and it doesn't bother me in the least." *Munger:* "this place would have very respectable prospects if the top 25 managers all dropped dead at once … it's been lovingly put together to have a certain margin of safety." See [berkshire-hathaway](../entities/berkshire-hathaway.md).

**★ M53 · Intrinsic value: "easy to say and impossible to figure."** *Buffett:* "the present value of the stream of cash … between now and doomsday" — easier to estimate for Coke than Intel ("Andy Grove may be better at figuring out Intel than Coke"). Berkshire's own value is harder because "we have no business that naturally employs all of the capital that flows to us," so it depends on "the opportunities available and the ingenuity used when that cash pours in." See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [intrinsic-value-vs-book-value](../concepts/intrinsic-value-vs-book-value.md).

**★ M54 · "Multiple models is the game."** *Munger:* "the Munger system for dealing with reality is to have multiple models in the head, and then run reality against multiple models … To the man with only a hammer, every problem looks pretty much like a nail." See [model-risk](../concepts/model-risk.md).

**M55 · Taxes don't drive activity.** *Buffett:* a tax-free Berkshire "wouldn't make a difference in causing us to trade actively" — "we own the businesses we want to own … not because taxes have restrained us." Expects to pay $1B in tax this year and could defer most of it by "doing nothing," but "it doesn't bother me a lot to be paying taxes." See [tax-deferral](../concepts/tax-deferral.md).

**★ M56 · Buy people who love the business, not money.** *Buffett:* three sellers in four are already rich, so "we have to decide … whether they love the business or love money … it's very important for me to know which." Berkshire's only job is to "avoid anything … that diminishes that love of the business." *Munger:* what enterprises need is "integrity, intelligence, and experience, and dedication." See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).

## Q&A digest — Afternoon session

**A1 · When to buy a house.** *Buffett:* bought his own only when the down payment was ~10% of net worth, treating a home as "a 7 or 8 percent investment, implicitly." *Munger:* "the time to buy a house is when you need one … [when] your wife wants one." (Personal-finance aside.)

**★ A2 · Big money for mediocrity is the obscenity.** *Buffett:* "the most exorbitant are not necessarily the biggest numbers … what really bothers me is when companies pay a lot of money for mediocrity." The system "feeds on itself" — proxy comparisons, the refusal to be "below average," consultants ratcheting rewards — because "the people who have their hands on the switch are the beneficiaries." *Munger:* a "widespread perception that … corporate salaries in America are too high … is not a good thing for a civilization"; comp consultants — "prostitution would be a step up for them." See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md), [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

**A3 · Class B stock worked out.** *Buffett:* created to "step hard on" a disreputable unit-trust scheme with heavy fees and likely misrepresentation; sold so as to attract long-term holders "quite similar in perspective" to the existing base. *Munger:* "we wanted to step hard on what we regarded as a disreputable financial scheme, and that we did." See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**A4 · The investing minimum, and size.** *Buffett:* the ~$750M reporting cutoff sits "more or less at the point where we think it's material"; Berkshire needs to deploy $500M+ at a time, so small caps "can't be material." "If Berkshire were exactly 1/100th of its present size … our prospects would be better." See [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md).

**A5 · Sandy Weill.** *Buffett:* "a very, very good manager" who built "a terrific company in businesses that themselves aren't necessarily so terrific, so it's required real management skill." (Period-specific on Travelers/Citi.)

**★ A6 · "We stupidly blew" pharmaceuticals.** *Buffett:* identifying the *industry* as a high-ROE group "was within our circle of competence … some sort of a group purchase might've made sense"; picking a single company was not. They recognized it and "didn't do anything about it." *Munger:* "we stupidly blew that one." A clean statement of the *buy-the-basket-when-you-can't-pick-the-winner* idea, and of omission as the costly error. See [mistakes-of-omission](../concepts/mistakes-of-omission.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ A7 · Decentralization "just short of total abdication."** *Buffett:* no operating plans submitted, no demanded meetings — "talent is the scarce commodity … we want them to do it their way." With Al Ueltschi at FlightSafety (spending ~$100M/yr on simulators), spending hours "couldn't add 1/100th of 1 percent to his knowledge"; with Ajit Jain he talks reinsurance "virtually every night," but only because "it's an interesting game," not to improve the decisions. *Munger:* "we have decentralized power … to a point just short of total abdication." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).

**★ A8 · The Frozen Corporation and the EBITDA fallacy.** *Buffett:* discount at the long Treasury rate "just to have a standard of measurement"; credit only *net* cash, never gross. Graham's Frozen Corporation (forbidden ever to pay out, liquidate, or sell) is worthless because "investing is the art … of laying out cash now to get a whole lot more cash later on, and something at some point better deliver cash." "The one figure we regard as utter nonsense is the so-called EBITDA." *Munger:* there's "a class of businesses where the eventual cash back … tends to be an illusion"; speaking in EBITDA is "not creditable intellectual performance." See [owner-earnings](../concepts/owner-earnings.md), [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md).

**★ A9 · The selection checklist: simple, not easy.** *Buffett:* understand the business → judge whether its 5–15-year earning power is good-and-improving or poor-and-worsening → judge the people → judge the price. "Knowing what you don't know is important … the stock is nothing other than a piece of a business" — exactly what you'd weigh buying a local service station. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md).

**★ A10 · Teach from the easy cases — National Cash Register.** *Munger:* finance should be taught "from cases where the investment decision is easy" — Patterson's NCR ("the pharmaceuticals of a former age"), where "an idiot could see that this was a talented fanatic … favorably located," and the 1904 report tells you both why the product is worth 20× its price and "that you're an idiot if you want to go in competition with him." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**A11 · A "Norman Rockwell frame of mind."** *Buffett:* Berkshire's kind of business has "a homey … character" whose builders "expect to spend the rest of their lives there rather than … stick it on their resume," and who keep running them well "even though they have no financial consequence to themselves." See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**A13 · Advice to teenagers.** *Buffett:* "learn all the accounting you can … accounting is the language of business" (and its limitations); work in many businesses; read constantly; save early — "any dollar … before you … start having a family is probably worth $10 later." *Munger:* "the little matter of underspending your income year after year after year." See [owner-earnings](../concepts/owner-earnings.md).

**A14 · Not reluctant to invest abroad.** *Buffett:* "if we find such companies … at a price that's half attractive we're perfectly willing to buy them" — but a worldwide bull market means "we're not finding bargains as we look around the world." The three biggest holdings (Amex, Gillette, Coke) already earn heavily abroad. See [look-through-earnings](../concepts/look-through-earnings.md).

**A15 · Charitable share sales won't move the price.** *Buffett:* a foundation giving away 5% a year (≈2% of Berkshire's cap) wouldn't materially move a stock whose value rests on "the prospects of the business" — "if there isn't demand for 500 shares a week … then the price probably is artificially wrong." See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**A16 · Best book on the philosophy.** *Buffett:* Larry Cunningham's organized collection of the Berkshire writings "did the best job … of sort of reconstructing the various things … into the best-organized presentation of our philosophy." (*Buffettology* "gets at the investment philosophy" but isn't the book he'd have written.)

**★ A17 · Selling Disney was a "huge mistake" — and do postmortems.** *Buffett:* "everything we've ever sold has gone up subsequently" — selling Disney in the '60s and the ad agencies in the '70s were mistakes; "the most successful investors, if they sell at all, will be selling things that end up going a lot higher." Companies happily forecast acquisitions but "don't like to do postmortems." *Munger:* "we kind of mentally rub our own noses in our own mistakes. And that is a very good mental habit." See [mistakes-of-omission](../concepts/mistakes-of-omission.md), [disney](../entities/disney.md).

**A19 · The end of the Cold War isn't an investing factor.** *Buffett:* "very difficult to isolate any single variable in a complex economic equation"; he wouldn't factor it into business valuation. *Munger:* but rising Third-World prosperity "is likely to [help] the Cokes and Gillettes" specifically. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**A20 · Subsidiary managers are never second-guessed.** *Buffett:* Borsheims takes American Express, the Furniture Mart doesn't — and headquarters won't override it, because "once we start making decisions for our managers … then we become responsible for the operation, and they are no longer." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**A21 · Tobacco settlement.** *Buffett:* "it smelled like trouble … when they were bound and you had another side that was not bound" — like a labor negotiation where management commits and the union still votes. (Period-specific.)

**A22 · Inheritance: "enough to do anything, not enough to do nothing."** *Buffett:* quoting Kay Graham's father; he'd tax inheritance heavily as "a form of consumption," disliking that "womb selection" should "enable many generations … to do nothing." *Munger:* "I see very few people that I regard as ruined by money. Many of the people that I see ruined who have money would have been ruined without money." (Values, not investing — but a load-bearing temperament note.)

**★ A24 · Two conditions for a market that isn't overvalued.** *Buffett:* not overvalued *only if* interest rates stay at/below present levels *and* corporate profitability stays near its "virtually unprecedented" levels — "a couple of big ifs … if either … is breached in an important way … it will turn out to be overvalued." See [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md).

**A25 · No formula for retaining earnings — but checks.** *Buffett:* every retained dollar to date has produced more than a dollar of market value, but "almost any management that wants to retain money is going to rationalize it" — so impose checks; "if after three or four years … the dollars … hasn't created more … in value, then the presumption becomes very strong … we should start paying out." See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md).

**A26 · Berkshire price tracks intrinsic value "better than most."** *Buffett:* over the ~33-year span book value has tracked intrinsic value, and "our market price has tracked intrinsic value more closely than … 90 percent of the companies that we view, probably 90 percent" — though "it's far from perfect," and "it's not getting more perfect, in our view." See [intrinsic-value-vs-book-value](../concepts/intrinsic-value-vs-book-value.md), [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**A27 · KKR's Gillette sale "means nothing to us."** *Buffett:* the Duracell-derived shares sit in a fund scheduled to disband, so the sale carries no signal — "we form our ideas of valuation independent of anybody else's." (The Mach 3 "is terrific … Henry did not decide to sell that stock based on the Mach 3.") See [gillette](../entities/gillette.md), [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md).

**A28 · No split → a "better class of shareholders."** *Buffett:* anyone wanting a 30-for-1 split "can have a 30-for-1 split any morning" by converting an A to Bs; the no-split policy "has benefitted the company and shareholders" by attracting a better long-term base. The underlying mechanics (A12): an A converts one-way into 30 Bs, the economic value of a B is exactly 1/30 of an A, and arbitrage caps the B near 1/30 of the A, so "you can treat the A and B as very equivalent investment choices." See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**★ A29 · Book value is not a factor — earnings determine value.** *Buffett:* Japanese stocks sell at low price-to-book "simply because Japanese companies are earning far less on book"; a 5%-on-book business isn't worth book. "If anything, we are less likely to look at something that sells at a low relationship to book … the chances are we're looking at a poor business." *Munger:* foreign corporate culture matters — own a company that will protect shareholders over "some steam boiler company." See [intrinsic-value-vs-book-value](../concepts/intrinsic-value-vs-book-value.md), [return-on-equity-yardstick](../concepts/return-on-equity-yardstick.md).

**A30 · Airlines: lend, never buy the equity.** *Buffett:* "we've never bought an airline common stock that I can remember" — USAir was a 10-year loan with a conversion privilege, "a terrible mistake … but we got bailed out" by Steve Wolf's rescue. *Munger:* "the net worth just melted … a very unpleasant experience … we're very slow learners." See [usair](../entities/usair.md), [commodity-business-economics](../concepts/commodity-business-economics.md).

**★ A32 · "We're only interested in price and value."** *Buffett:* declines to compute a correlation coefficient against the S&P — historical relative performance (his best decade, the 1950s, "40-plus points per year") "has no relevance to today." "We don't pay any attention to beta … any kind of market movements … don't mean anything. What does count is where it is 10 years from now." *Munger:* "If the correlation isn't so extreme you can see it with the naked eye, he doesn't compute it." See [risk-vs-volatility](../concepts/risk-vs-volatility.md), [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md).

**★ A33 · Beware businesses that must "spend money like crazy."** *Buffett:* depreciation is usually "a pretty good … proxy for required capital expenditures," so reported earnings plus intangible amortization approximate earning power. The trap is the airline/textile business where you "spend money like crazy … whether it's attractive [or] unattractive" with no conviction it returns cash — versus See's, where capital can't be productively added, or FlightSafety, where it can. *Munger:* the good business "just throws up one easy decision after another"; the bad one hands you "the management bloopers time after time." Buffett's Coke-board contrast: every Coke project has an ROI but "almost any decision … that solidifies and extends the dominance of Coke … is going to be right," while at USAir "you don't have any real conviction that it's going to translate … into real money." See [owner-earnings](../concepts/owner-earnings.md), [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md), [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md).

**A34 · Built to last.** *Buffett:* "about as well-prepared as any company can be for adversity … net, we would benefit … by having some periods of terrible markets." *Munger:* "we are not going to ever sell everything and go to cash and wait for the crash … a lot of turmoil in the next 20 years will help us." See [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md), [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

**★ A37 · "We wait indefinitely" — the discipline of inactivity.** *Buffett:* having found nothing in equities for many months, "the question of how long we wait, we wait indefinitely. We are not going to buy anything just to buy something" — and 5–10% cheaper "wouldn't change anything materially." When something makes sense Berkshire acts "very fast, very big," but "you don't get paid for activity, you only get paid for being right." *Munger:* "An occasional dull stretch for new buying, this is no great tragedy in an investment lifetime … it isn't like we have a quiver with only one arrow." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

**A38 · A "certified record of failure" in real estate.** *Munger:* "we have been demonstrably foolish in almost every operation that had to do with real estate we've ever touched." *Buffett:* "and the funny thing is, we understand real estate" — *Munger:* "and we're good at it. … But we understand other things better." A lesson in declining even a competence when the returns aren't there. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**A39 · Nike.** *Buffett:* "Phil Knight is a terrific operator … but in terms of what we think of the stock … we keep all of those views to ourselves." (Declines to opine.)

**★ A42 · Phil Fisher's "scuttlebutt" method.** *Buffett:* a strong hypothesis should be "80 percent" of the case — like a coach spotting a seven-footer; scuttlebutt (competitors, customers, suppliers, ex-employees) is "the last 20 percent," used "to possibly reject your original hypothesis." It reinforced his American Express buying in the '60s; one five-minute call to Hertz's Frank Olson in 1994 (a heavy Amex payer) beat "hours … of roaming around"; and the 1951 GEICO visit to Lorimer Davidson "changed my life in a major way." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [american-express](../entities/american-express.md).

**A43 · The real test is the gain in intrinsic value.** *Buffett:* "the final bottom line GAAP reported earnings mean absolutely nothing"; realized capital gains can be reported "in any number that we wanted to." Look-through earnings have "some meaning" (adjusted for super-cats); the real test "is the gain in intrinsic value … and there's no hard number for that … Charlie and I judge it satisfactory, but … non-repeatable." See [look-through-earnings](../concepts/look-through-earnings.md), [intrinsic-value-vs-book-value](../concepts/intrinsic-value-vs-book-value.md).

**★ A44 · "We prefer what other people call risk."** *Buffett:* risk is "a go/no-go valve" on certainty, not a discount-rate add-on — "if we think we simply don't know what's going to happen … that's risky *for us*." For a business he's certain about, "we would prefer that it have high volatility than low volatility" — the Washington Post's 50% fall was "the best thing that could've happened … a nonvolatile business [but] a volatile stock … a great combination." *Munger:* "as long as the odds are in our favor and we're not risking the whole company … we don't mind volatility … We figure the volatility, over time, will take care of itself." See [risk-vs-volatility](../concepts/risk-vs-volatility.md), [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

**A45 · Want shareholders who see it the way we do.** *Buffett:* "our idea of a high-caliber group is one that is just like us" — holders who understand the business, the objectives, the time horizon, producing "a more consistent relationship between price and intrinsic value." *Munger:* under "trustee capitalism … wouldn't you rather feel an obligation to people you liked?" See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**★ A46 · "Get more quality than you're paying for."** *Buffett:* a three-to-five-year horizon "leans toward the bigger fool theory" — "the best way to look at any investment is, how will I feel if I own it forever?" Quality means "the certainty that the business will perform as you expect … so the range of possible performance is fairly narrow." *Munger:* "the trick is to get more quality than you're paying for in the price. It's just that simple." *Buffett:* "But not easy." See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [margin-of-safety](../concepts/margin-of-safety.md).

**★ A47 · No interest in spinoffs.** *Buffett:* even where a piece might fetch a higher multiple alone, spinning it off "just doesn't mean anything to us." *Munger:* "it would add a lot of frictional costs and overheads"; Berkshire's after-tax overhead is "a half a basis point of capital value" versus 125 for many mutual funds. See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [investment-friction](../concepts/investment-friction.md).

**A50 · The internet could help Borsheims and GEICO.** *Buffett:* Borsheims runs "15 to 20 percentage points" below public jewelers' costs, and the internet plus the Berkshire name can spread that "terrific deal" nationally — same logic as GEICO's low-cost auto insurance. *Munger:* "all that said … the CD-ROM and the personal computer combined to clobber World Book for us" — *Buffett:* "we paid our entry fee." See [borsheims](../entities/borsheims.md), [geico](../entities/geico.md), [direct-distribution-moat](../concepts/direct-distribution-moat.md).

**A51 · McDonald's vs. Dairy Queen economic models.** *Buffett:* McDonald's owns/operates much of its ~23,000 locations (large capital, good returns, plus rentals); Dairy Queen takes ~4% royalties on ~6,000 franchisee-funded outlets (little capital). "You want the franchise operator to … create a capital asset that's worth more than he's put in it. That's the goal." See [franchise-vs-business](../concepts/franchise-vs-business.md).

**A52 · The Byrne family and GEICO at 2 1/8.** *Buffett:* after a three-hour Wednesday-night meeting with Jack Byrne in 1976 with GEICO near bankruptcy, "the next day I went out and bought 500-some-thousand shares … at 2 1/8" — later worth the equivalent of $70. Now three Byrne sons run or build Berkshire operations. See [geico](../entities/geico.md), [jack-byrne](../people/jack-byrne.md).

**★ A54 · You end up with the reputation you deserve.** *Buffett:* judging honesty needs "some evidence of behavior" — Tom Murphy "bending over backwards … to make sure that you get the better end of the deal." Quoting Agnelli: "When you get older, you have the reputation you deserve." *Munger:* "people leave track records in life … if you start early, trying to have a perfect record in some simple thing like honesty, you're well on the way … the best way to get what you want is to deserve what you want." See [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md), [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**★ A57 · Berkshire is "poorly" structured for owning securities.** *Buffett:* a corporation between you and the stock means double tax — selling Coke would trigger ~$5B of capital-gains tax, then a second tax when reflected in Berkshire's value; "if you have the option of owning them directly or through a partnership — corporate ownership is disadvantageous." Mitigants: the float that helped acquire them, and that "to the extent we have very long holding periods … the real mathematical disadvantage shrinks" (Munger). See [tax-deferral](../concepts/tax-deferral.md), [float](../concepts/float.md).

**★ A58 · Due diligence is boilerplate — people and economics are 99%.** *Buffett:* bought Star Furniture after reviewing financials briefly and meeting Melvyn Wolff for two hours — no lease checks, no employment contracts. Conventional due diligence is "really sort of boilerplate … they're ignoring … evaluating the people they're getting in with, and evaluating the economics of the business. That's 99 percent of the deal." Across 30-plus years he "cannot think of anything that traditional due diligence has had a thing to do with"; the people he deals with (Abegg at Illinois National) "told us the bad things first and good things after we made the deal." See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

## Connections to other sources

- The **"important and knowable"** filter (M23) and the seven-foot-bar refusal of tech (M45) are the spoken extension of the circle-of-competence discipline first stated on the floor in [berkshire-1994-meeting](berkshire-1994-meeting.md) (Nike/Reebok, M38) and [berkshire-1995-meeting](berkshire-1995-meeting.md) (Microsoft/"can I understand it?", M33/A24) — here pushed further into a positive *what to think about* heuristic.
- **Float at negative cost** (M27) builds directly on 1995's "the float page is probably as important a page as there is" ([berkshire-1995-meeting](berkshire-1995-meeting.md) A22); but its optimism about holding the cost down is, within weeks, overtaken by the General Re acquisition introduced in [berkshire-1998-letter](berkshire-1998-letter.md) — the clearest supersession on this page.
