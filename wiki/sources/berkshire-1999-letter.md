# Berkshire Hathaway 1999 Annual Letter

Dated March 1, 2000. Per-share book value rose just 0.5% — Buffett's worst absolute year and worst relative year versus the S&P in his tenure — while the Nasdaq tripled on dot-com mania. Berkshire's largest investees (Coca-Cola, Gillette, American Express, Washington Post, Wells Fargo) badly lagged the market. The letter's lasting contributions are not the year's results: it delivers the most complete articulation of circle-of-competence discipline in the entire letter series, the definitive post-bubble critique of share buyback abuse, a substantive accounting-reform proposal on goodwill that anticipated SFAS 142, and the first clear structural lesson from General Re's expensive inaugural year. Both 1999 acquisitions — Jordan's Furniture and a major stake in MidAmerican Energy — were done for cash, no Berkshire shares issued.

## Key themes

**Circle of competence: the only honest answer to the tech question.** Berkshire owned no technology stocks in 1999 while the Nasdaq tripled. Buffett's explanation is not contrarianism or macro prediction — it is disciplined honesty about the limits of his analytical model. "Our problem — which we can't solve by studying up — is that we have no insights into which participants in the tech field possess a truly durable competitive advantage." The principle generalizes beyond tech: "If we have a strength, it is in recognizing when we are operating well within our circle of competence and when we are approaching the perimeter." Staying inside the circle is the *discipline*; identifying the perimeter is the *skill*. Buffett does not claim to know tech is overvalued — he claims only that he cannot confidently identify winners, and that uncertainty alone is sufficient reason to abstain. The same logic applies to patents, manufacturing processes, geological prospects: competence is domain-specific, not general. If the choice is between a questionable business at a comfortable price and a comfortable business at a questionable price, Berkshire prefers the latter — but what really gets Buffett's attention is "a comfortable business at a comfortable price." This is the 1999 letter's most durable lesson: the circle-of-competence discipline published at peak mania, when abstention was most visible and most mocked. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**Share repurchases: the discipline and the abuse.** The correct two-part test for buybacks: (1) excess capital beyond near-term business needs, and (2) stock selling below intrinsic value, conservatively calculated — with full disclosure so insiders cannot extract value from uninformed partners. By 1999, repurchases had become "all the rage," but the dominant motivation had shifted to price support and option-dilution offsetting. Companies were issuing shares at low option-strike prices and buying back the same shares at market highs — a "buy high, sell low" pattern that "many unfortunate investors have employed — but never intentionally! Managements, however, seem to follow this perverse activity very cheerfully." "Buying dollar bills for $1.10 is not good business for those who stick around." The essay distinguishes clearly: repurchases that benefit continuing shareholders require a stock trading *below* intrinsic value; above intrinsic value, buybacks transfer wealth from continuing shareholders to departing ones. Berkshire considered repurchasing when A shares fell below $45,000 but deferred pending shareholder review of the letter. This essay is the template Buffett returns to in subsequent letters as buyback abuse compounds across the market. See [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md).

**Acquisition accounting: two fictions in collision.** FASB's proposed elimination of pooling accounting triggered Buffett's clearest acquisition-accounting statement. He agrees pooling is fiction: "there is indisputably an acquirer and an acquiree" in every merger — ask the severed employees who was conquered. But mandatory goodwill amortization is equally fictitious in great businesses: "In contrast, economic goodwill does not, in many cases, diminish. Indeed, in a great many instances — perhaps most — it actually grows in value over time. In character, economic goodwill is much like land." See's Candies, 78 years old by 1999, is the case. Buffett's proposed reform: record purchase price at fair value; carry goodwill as an asset without mandatory amortization; write down only on impairment, like any other asset. (FASB adopted approximately this approach in SFAS 142, 2001.) The practical corollary on tax structure: a cash purchase of *assets* (as in the Jordan's deal) permits 15-year goodwill amortization for tax purposes, materially increasing after-tax cash. A cash purchase of *stock* does not. A stock-for-stock deal is worst of all: "From the economic standpoint of the acquiring company, the worst deal of all is a stock-for-stock acquisition" — no step-up in the tax basis of either the acquiree's stock or its assets, leaving the full capital-gains exposure on any future sale. See [economic-goodwill](../concepts/economic-goodwill.md) and [stock-as-acquisition-currency](../concepts/stock-as-acquisition-currency.md).

**Float: the first expensive year, and what it taught.** General Re's first full year under Berkshire produced a $1.4 billion underwriting loss and float costing 5.8% — the first materially positive-cost year in Berkshire's insurance history. Buffett distinguishes two components: $400M of the loss was "welcomed" because it funds long-duration float from retroactive reinsurance contracts that will deliver exceptional float over the next decade; the rest was "decidedly unwelcome" — General Re had been "extremely underpriced, both domestically and internationally." The structural lesson: "Growth of float is important — but its cost is what's vital." Float that grows fast at high cost is not a bargain. The positive contrast is Ajit Jain's reinsurance operation, which by 1999 had built $6.3B of float from scratch since 1986 *with cumulative underwriting profit* — meaning Berkshire was paid to hold it. The four-trait portrait: "In Ajit, we have an underwriter equipped with the intelligence to properly rate most risks; the realism to forget about those he can't evaluate; the courage to write huge policies when the premium is appropriate; and the discipline to reject even the smallest risk when the premium is inadequate. It is rare to find a person possessing any one of these talents. For one person to have them all is remarkable." These four traits identify precisely what keeps float cheap. See [float](../concepts/float.md) and [general-re](../entities/general-re.md).

**Manager alignment: no contracts, no budgets, one ask.** The R.C. Willey / Bill Child story is the 1999 exemplar of Berkshire's management compact. Bill Child insisted on personally funding the $9M Boise store — absorbing the full downside if the no-Sunday policy failed in a new market — then transferred the property to Berkshire at his cost after success, refusing two years' interest on the tied-up capital. Buffett frames it not as a remarkable individual act but as evidence of a selection principle: independently wealthy operators who love their businesses act as owner-principals, not agents. "We simply ask our managers to run their companies as if these are the sole asset of their families and will remain so for the next century." No contracts, no headquarters presentations, no budget approvals. The result — a manager who voluntarily takes on personal financial risk to protect a partnership with Berkshire — cannot be mandated; it can only be attracted and retained by a culture that treats managers as true co-owners. See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**MidAmerican Energy: a capital-deployment platform begins.** Introduced at a Sun Valley–style conference by Walter Scott (Berkshire director), MidAmerican required a non-standard ownership structure to comply with the Public Utility Holding Company Act of 1935: an 11% fixed-income security plus common and exchangeable preferred giving Berkshire just under 10% of voting power but ~76% of equity interest, for a total ~$2B investment. Buffett flags the platform potential: "Though there are many regulatory constraints in the utility industry, it's possible that we will make additional commitments in the field. If we do, the amounts involved could be large." David Sokol, MidAmerican's CEO, is assessed before the deal closes — Buffett's standard of evaluating the operator alongside the asset. This is the origin of what becomes Berkshire Hathaway Energy. See [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md).

## Notable quotes

> Our problem — which we can't solve by studying up — is that we have no insights into which participants in the tech field possess a truly durable competitive advantage.

> If we have a strength, it is in recognizing when we are operating well within our circle of competence and when we are approaching the perimeter. Predicting the long-term economics of companies that operate in fast-changing industries is simply far beyond our perimeter. If others claim predictive skill in those industries — and seem to have their claims validated by the behavior of the stock market — we neither envy nor emulate them. Instead, we just stick with what we understand.

> Growth of float is important — but its cost is what's vital.

> In Ajit, we have an underwriter equipped with the intelligence to properly rate most risks; the realism to forget about those he can't evaluate; the courage to write huge policies when the premium is appropriate; and the discipline to reject even the smallest risk when the premium is inadequate. It is rare to find a person possessing any one of these talents. For one person to have them all is remarkable.

> In contrast, economic goodwill does not, in many cases, diminish. Indeed, in a great many instances — perhaps most — it actually grows in value over time. In character, economic goodwill is much like land: The value of both assets is sure to fluctuate, but the direction in which value is going to go is in no way ordained. At See's, for example, economic goodwill has grown, in an irregular but very substantial manner, for 78 years.

> From the economic standpoint of the acquiring company, the worst deal of all is a stock-for-stock acquisition.

> There is only one combination of facts that makes it advisable for a company to repurchase its shares: First, the company has available funds — cash plus sensible borrowing capacity — beyond the near-term needs of the business and, second, finds its stock selling in the market below its intrinsic value, conservatively-calculated.

> Buying dollar bills for $1.10 is not good business for those who stick around.

> We simply ask our managers to run their companies as if these are the sole asset of their families and will remain so for the next century.
