# Berkshire Hathaway 2000 Annual Letter

The 2000 letter makes three durable contributions against the backdrop of the dot-com collapse and an eight-company acquisition spree: (1) the clearest statement of Buffett's valuation framework in any letter — Aesop's three questions reduce all asset pricing to a single equation, and the bubble was a mass failure to apply it; (2) a structural explanation for why Berkshire prefers owning businesses outright, rooted in the tax code rather than sentiment; and (3) an important amendment to the float story — retroactive reinsurance amortization creates a permanent structural floor on float cost, something the earlier float essays did not contemplate. Net worth gained $3.96 billion; book value per share rose 6.5% to $40,442.

## Key themes

### Valuation has always been Aesop's three questions

Buffett uses the dot-com collapse as the occasion for the most explicit statement of his valuation framework: "a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush" is the complete theory. Three questions are all that remain: How certain are there birds in the bush? When will they emerge and how many? What is the risk-free rate? Answering those three gives the maximum value of any asset — "farms, oil royalties, bonds, stocks, lottery tickets, and manufacturing plants." The Internet, like electricity and the automobile before it, changed nothing in the formula.

This framework disposes of the growth-vs-value debate in one sentence: growth is "simply a component — usually a plus, sometimes a minus — in the value equation." Growth requiring cash inputs that exceed the discounted value of what those inputs will produce *destroys* value; growth compounding retained earnings above the cost of capital creates it. Calling them contrasting approaches "reveals ignorance, not sophistication."

The dot-com bubble was a collective refusal to ask the three questions. A Paine Webber-Gallup December 1999 survey found investors expecting 19% annual returns over the next decade — the birds simply were not there. Bubble markets enabled bubble companies: IPOs as the goal, not profits; promoters transferring wealth from investors to themselves by merchandising "birdless bushes"; investment bankers as "eager postmen" for an "old-fashioned chain letter." The lesson that endures: "a pin lies in wait for every bubble." Rationality sedated by effortless money always produces this outcome; "clocks have no hands" only until they do.

### Owning 80% beats owning 10%: the tax structure explains why

Berkshire prefers acquiring whole businesses to buying minority stock positions not for sentimental reasons but because the tax code eliminates two layers of government partnership at 80%-or-more ownership. When a wholly-owned subsidiary earns $1 million after tax, the full dollar flows to Berkshire — no 14% dividend tax on distributions, no 35%+ capital-gains tax on retained earnings when eventually sold. Owning 10% subjects the same dollar to both. "The government is our 'partner' twice when we own part of a business through a stock investment, but only once when we own at least 80%."

This explains the 2000 acquisition spree: eight businesses ($13 billion in aggregate sales, 58,000 employees) funded with $8 billion, 97% in cash, with only 0.3% share dilution. Whole-business ownership at a fair price compounds better than minority positions even at premium valuations, because the compounding runs at the pre-government-partnership rate.

### Seller character as quality signal

Berkshire's growing competitive advantage in acquisitions is partly reputational: it is the "buyer of choice" for founders who care where their business lands. That preference carries information. Emotional attachment by a long-time owner signals honest accounting, pride of product, respect for customers, and a loyal workforce. The reverse holds equally: when owners auction their business showing no interest in what follows, it has often been "dressed up for sale." Seller disposition is a leading indicator of business quality.

The 2000 acquisitions — including Justin Industries (boots + brick), Shaw Industries (world's largest carpet maker at ~$4B sales), Benjamin Moore (paint, 117-year history), and Johns Manville (industrial insulation, acquired days after an LBO buyer failed to finance) — arrived because Berkshire can make unconditional promises: headquarters stays in Fort Worth, the jeweler won't be merged with a competitor. "We'll leave the auctions to others."

### Float's new structural cost: retroactive reinsurance amortization

Float grew from $25.3 billion to $27.9 billion in 2000, but its cost rose to 6% on an underwriting loss of $1.6 billion — well above the near-zero long-run average. Two structures now embedded at Berkshire, rare elsewhere, drove this and are worth distinguishing.

First, "pain-today, gain-tomorrow" policies: insurers with large current losses offload them to Berkshire in exchange for multi-year float. $482 million of the 2000 underwriting loss (34.4%) came from this category — a deliberate trade of first-year cost for years of investable float with no further charges.

Second and more structurally consequential: *retroactive* reinsurance — Berkshire assumes the *past* losses of companies that want their liabilities behind them. Under GAAP, a "deferred charges applicable to assumed reinsurance" asset is set up and amortized by making annual charges to earnings, sometimes for decades. Ajit Jain sourced the U.K. retroactive policy generating $2.4 billion in premium (perhaps the largest reinsurance premium in history), along with disability cover for Alex Rodriguez and a $170M-present-value sweepstakes policy for Grab.com.

The implication Buffett stated plainly: float will "rarely return to a 'no-cost' mode because of the annual charge that retroactive reinsurance will lay on us." The earlier float essays (1990, 1995) described float as potentially free; this letter adds a permanent structural qualifier unique to Berkshire's retroactive book.

### The GEICO stumble: distinguish execution stumble from moat impairment

For the first time since the 1996 acquisition, new GEICO policies declined — from 1,648,095 in 1999 to 1,472,853 in 2000. Buffett's post-mortem identified four causes: (1) advertising frequency reached diminishing returns; (2) the pool of direct-purchase converts had been largely tapped; (3) stricter underwriting and rate increases lowered conversion rates; (4) State Farm, with 19% market share, was absorbing an 18%-of-premium underwriting loss rather than raising prices — a competitive distortion only a mutual without equity-capital pressure could sustain.

The key distinction: all four causes are temporary or structural-to-State-Farm, not structural-to-GEICO. The moat — lowest operating costs in a commodity-like product — remained intact. "When a company is selling a product with commodity-like economic characteristics, being the low-cost producer is all-important." An investor's job in any execution stumble is to determine whether the underlying competitive advantage has changed; in this case it had not.

The post-mortem also delivered a principle about corporate culture: failures get renamed "restructuring charges" and their origins are never traced. "When it comes to corporate blunders, CEOs invoke the concept of the Virgin Birth."

### Dexter: paying in stock compounds a mistake

"I clearly made a mistake in paying what I did for Dexter in 1993. Furthermore, I compounded that mistake in a huge way by using Berkshire shares in payment." All remaining accounting goodwill from Dexter was charged off. The compounding mechanism is precise: when an acquirer pays with shares worth more than what it receives in exchange, a bad underlying purchase doubles in destructiveness. The principle generalizes to any acquisition partly in stock — the acquirer must evaluate whether the shares given up are worth more than the business received, then apply *that* ratio to the purchase price.

### Growth-rate predictions corrode CEO behavior

Of the 200 highest-earning companies of 1970 or 1980, only a handful sustained 15% EPS growth for two decades. Fewer than 10 of the 200 most profitable companies of 2000 would likely achieve it over the next 20 years. The danger in public growth commitments is not just false optimism but behavioral cascade: CEOs who announce growth targets are forced first into uneconomic operating maneuvers, then accounting games, then fraud. The EBITDA critique is the corollary: a metric that ignores depreciation pretends capital expenditures are free. Both patterns — guidance games and EBITDA promotion — are symptoms of the same underlying incentive: make reported numbers look better than economic reality. "More money has been stolen with the point of a pen than at the point of a gun."

## Notable quotes

> The oracle was Aesop and his enduring, though somewhat incomplete, investment insight was "a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush." To flesh out this principle, you must answer only three questions. How certain are you that there are indeed birds in the bush? When will they emerge and how many will there be? What is the risk-free interest rate (which we consider to be the yield on long-term U.S. bonds)?

> And, of course, don't literally think birds. Think dollars.

> Market commentators and investment managers who glibly refer to "growth" and "value" styles as contrasting approaches to investment are displaying their ignorance, not their sophistication. Growth is simply a component — usually a plus, sometimes a minus — in the value equation.

> Nothing sedates rationality like large doses of effortless money.

> They are dancing in a room in which the clocks have no hands.

> When it comes to corporate blunders, CEOs invoke the concept of the Virgin Birth.

> I clearly made a mistake in paying what I did for Dexter in 1993. Furthermore, I compounded that mistake in a huge way by using Berkshire shares in payment.

> References to EBITDA make us shudder — does management think the tooth fairy pays for capital expenditures?

> More money, it has been noted, has been stolen with the point of a pen than at the point of a gun.
