# Berkshire Hathaway 2000 Annual Letter

Dated February 28, 2001. Net worth gain of $3.96 billion; per-share book value up 6.5% to $40,442. The defining 2000 events: an **eight-business acquisition spree** ($8 billion deployed, 97% cash, only 0.3% share dilution) — MidAmerican Energy, CORT, U.S. Liability, Ben Bridge Jeweler, Justin Industries, Shaw Industries, Benjamin Moore, and Johns Manville. Plus the **GEICO stumble** (advertising spend overran returns; policy growth halted); the **Dexter Shoe goodwill writeoff** (acknowledging the 1993 mistake compounded by paying in stock); the **Aesop "bird in the hand" essay** as the canonical valuation reframing against the dot-com bubble; Ralph Schey's retirement from Scott Fetzer after 15 years; and Ben Graham's 50th anniversary.

## Key themes

- **Eight-business acquisition spree.** $8 billion deployed across MidAmerican Energy (76%), CORT Business Services, U.S. Liability, Ben Bridge Jeweler, Justin Industries (boots + Acme Brick), Shaw Industries (carpet), Benjamin Moore (paint), and Johns Manville (insulation). 97% paid in cash; share count up only 0.3%. Two macro tailwinds: (1) sellers fearing near-term slowdowns; (2) junk-bond market drying up, making LBO buyers less competitive. The standing competitive advantage: Berkshire as the "buyer of choice" for owners who care where their business lands. "We'll leave the auctions to others."
- **The Aesop bird-in-the-hand essay.** The canonical reframing of valuation: "a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush" — and "don't literally think birds. Think dollars." Three questions: how certain that birds are in the bush; when they'll emerge and how many; the risk-free rate. The framework applies universally — "farms, oil royalties, bonds, stocks, lottery tickets, and manufacturing plants" — and "the advent of the steam engine, the harnessing of electricity nor the creation of the automobile changed the formula one iota — nor will the Internet." Growth is just one variable in the equation; growth/value as "contrasting approaches" reveals "ignorance, not sophistication." See [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md) and [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md).
- **Dot-com bubble critique.** Cinderella-at-the-ball metaphor: speculators "are dancing in a room in which the clocks have no hands." "Bubble market" enabled "bubble companies" — IPOs as the goal, not profits; "old-fashioned chain letter" with "fee-hungry investment bankers" as postmen. Wealth transfer, not value creation, when promoters merchandised "birdless bushes." The Paine Webber-Gallup December 1999 survey: investors expected 19% annual returns over the coming decade — "irrational."
- **GEICO advertising stumble (post-mortem).** Buffett's frank admission: "I told you last year that we would get our money's worth from stepped-up advertising at GEICO in 2000, but I was wrong." Four causes: (1) ad-frequency diminishing returns; (2) "low-hanging fruit" of direct-purchase converts already picked; (3) stricter underwriting and rate increases lowering conversion; (4) State Farm absorbing 18%-of-premium underwriting losses, distorting industry pricing. Despite the stumble, GEICO's structural advantage (low-cost producer in a commodity-like product) is unchanged. Buffett uses the post-mortem to attack the corporate practice of waving off failed decisions as "nonrecurring" or "restructuring charges" — "When it comes to corporate blunders, CEOs invoke the concept of the Virgin Birth." See [geico](../entities/geico.md).
- **Retroactive reinsurance and the cost-of-float essay.** Two unusual structures now material at Berkshire: (1) "pain-today, gain-tomorrow" policies that book a first-year underwriting loss in exchange for many years of float; (2) "retroactive" insurance assuming past losses of others, recorded under GAAP as a "deferred charges applicable to assumed reinsurance" asset amortized for many years. Ajit Jain's $2.4B reinsurance premium (a U.K. retroactive policy, "perhaps the largest in history"). 2000 cost of float: 6% — float of $27.87 billion, $1.6 billion underwriting loss. Expectation: float cost falls in 2001 but unlikely to return to "no-cost" because of the retroactive amortization drag. See [float](../concepts/float.md).
- **Dexter Shoe writedown — the compounded mistake.** "I clearly made a mistake in paying what I did for Dexter in 1993. Furthermore, I compounded that mistake in a huge way by using Berkshire shares in payment." All remaining accounting goodwill from Dexter charged off. Continuing the [stock-as-acquisition-currency](../concepts/stock-as-acquisition-currency.md) discipline: paying with undervalued stock makes a bad purchase doubly bad.
- **State Farm as counter-evidence and the GEICO moat.** State Farm as "one of America's greatest business stories" — built from 1922 by a 45-year-old semi-retired Illinois farmer to compete with "haughty institutions in New York, Philadelphia and Hartford." A mutual: no stock options, no equity capital markets, no lavish salaries. By 1999 had "tangible net worth exceeding that of all but four American businesses." Buffett urges studying it at business schools — "Studying counter-evidence is a highly useful activity, though not one always greeted with enthusiasm at citadels of learning." But GEICO has "much the better business model, one that embodies significantly lower operating costs."
- **Full and fair reporting essay.** Refusal to participate in "selective disclosure" or to make growth-rate predictions. The Arthur Levitt SEC reform applauded. EBITDA shudder: "does management think the tooth fairy pays for capital expenditures?" Annual report posted online Friday-after-close to give all 300,000 owners simultaneous access. The "test" on growth-rate predictions: of the 200 highest-earning companies of 1970 or 1980, only a handful sustained 15% EPS growth; same likely for the 200 most profitable of 2000. Predictions of high growth "corrode CEO behavior" — operating maneuvers, then accounting games, then fraud. "More money has been stolen with the point of a pen than at the point of a gun."
- **Owners who care vs. financial owners.** The acquisition essay: emotional attachment of a long-time owner is the signal of "honest accounting, pride of product, respect for customers, and a loyal group of associates." Auctions and "financial owners" carry the opposite signal — "dressed up for sale." Berkshire's structural advantage: "When we tell John Justin that his business will remain headquartered in Fort Worth, or assure the Bridge family that its operation will not be merged with another jeweler, these sellers can take those promises to the bank."
- **Ralph Schey retirement at year-end 2000.** Fifteen years running Scott Fetzer. $1.03 billion distributed to Berkshire against the $230 million net purchase price; contribution to Berkshire's present value "well into the billions of dollars." Inducted into "Berkshire's Hall of Fame." See [ralph-schey](../people/ralph-schey.md) and [scott-fetzer](../entities/scott-fetzer.md).
- **Ben Graham 50th anniversary.** "It was exactly 50 years ago that I entered Ben Graham's class at Columbia." Personal results "no better than average" in the decade before; "Beginning in 1951 my performance improved … a few hours spent at the feet of the master proved far more valuable to me than had ten years of supposedly original thinking." See [ben-graham](../people/ben-graham.md).
- **Wall Street Journal Conseco false report.** A long aside attacking the unattributed-rumor practice of major news organizations: "Today's Rumors" should be the honest column heading. December 29 front-page item claimed Berkshire was buying Conseco bonds; Berkshire never owned a share or bond of Conseco. Story moved the stock to ninth most-active on the NYSE. "Journalism's First Principle should require that the media be scrupulous in deciding what that will be."
- **WSJ Conseco story aside is a window into Buffett's stance on media noise** — connects to the same epistemic discipline that produces "rumors are what these organizations often publish."

## Notable quotes

> I will detail our purchases in the next section of the report. But I will tell you now that we have embraced the 21st century by entering such cutting-edge industries as brick, carpet, insulation and paint. Try to control your excitement.

> Our acquisition technique at Berkshire is simplicity itself: We answer the phone.

> I can't resist pointing out that Berkshire — whose top management has long been mired in the 19th century — is now one of the very few authentic "clicks-and-bricks" businesses around. We went into 2000 with GEICO doing significant business on the Internet, and then we added Acme. You can bet this move by Berkshire is making them sweat in Silicon Valley.

> When a business masterpiece has been created by a lifetime — or several lifetimes — of unstinting care and exceptional talent, it should be important to the owner what corporation is entrusted to carry on its history.

> How much better it is for the "painter" of a business Rembrandt to personally select its permanent home than to have a trust officer or uninterested heirs auction it off.

> We'll leave the auctions to others.

> A caution is appropriate here: Because loss costs must be estimated, insurers have enormous latitude in figuring their underwriting results, and that makes it very difficult for investors to calculate a company's true cost of float.

> At Berkshire, we strive to be both consistent and conservative in our reserving. But we will make mistakes. And we warn you that there is nothing symmetrical about surprises in the insurance business: They almost always are unpleasant.

> Our retroactive business is almost single-handedly the work of Ajit Jain, whose praises I sing annually. It is impossible to overstate how valuable Ajit is to Berkshire. Don't worry about my health; worry about his.

> Agonizing over errors is a mistake. But acknowledging and analyzing them can be useful, though that practice is rare in corporate boardrooms.

> When it comes to corporate blunders, CEOs invoke the concept of the Virgin Birth.

> To steal a line from a competitor, we are in good hands with Tony. [On Tony Nicely at GEICO.]

> The oracle was Aesop and his enduring, though somewhat incomplete, investment insight was "a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush." To flesh out this principle, you must answer only three questions. How certain are you that there are indeed birds in the bush? When will they emerge and how many will there be? What is the risk-free interest rate (which we consider to be the yield on long-term U.S. bonds)?

> And, of course, don't literally think birds. Think dollars.

> Aesop's investment axiom, thus expanded and converted into dollars, is immutable. It applies to outlays for farms, oil royalties, bonds, stocks, lottery tickets, and manufacturing plants. And neither the advent of the steam engine, the harnessing of electricity nor the creation of the automobile changed the formula one iota — nor will the Internet.

> Market commentators and investment managers who glibly refer to "growth" and "value" styles as contrasting approaches to investment are displaying their ignorance, not their sophistication. Growth is simply a component — usually a plus, sometimes a minus — in the value equation.

> Now, speculation — in which the focus is not on what an asset will produce but rather on what the next fellow will pay for it — is neither illegal, immoral nor un-American. But it is not a game in which Charlie and I wish to play. We bring nothing to the party, so why should we expect to take anything home?

> Nothing sedates rationality like large doses of effortless money.

> They know that overstaying the festivities — that is, continuing to speculate in companies that have gigantic valuations relative to the cash they are likely to generate in the future — will eventually bring on pumpkins and mice. But they nevertheless hate to miss a single minute of what is one helluva party. Therefore, the giddy participants all plan to leave just seconds before midnight. There's a problem, though: They are dancing in a room in which the clocks have no hands.

> But value is destroyed, not created, by any business that loses money over its lifetime, no matter how high its interim valuation may get.

> The fact is that a bubble market has allowed the creation of bubble companies, entities designed more with an eye to making money off investors rather than for them. Too often, an IPO, not profits, was the primary goal of a company's promoters. At bottom, the "business model" for these companies has been the old-fashioned chain letter, for which many fee-hungry investment bankers acted as eager postmen.

> But a pin lies in wait for every bubble. And when the two eventually meet, a new wave of investors learns some very old lessons: First, many in Wall Street — a community in which quality control is not prized — will sell investors anything they will buy. Second, speculation is most dangerous when it looks easiest.

> Even so, we make many mistakes: I'm the fellow, remember, who thought he understood the future economics of trading stamps, textiles, shoes and second-tier department stores.

> I clearly made a mistake in paying what I did for Dexter in 1993. Furthermore, I compounded that mistake in a huge way by using Berkshire shares in payment.

> References to EBITDA make us shudder — does management think the tooth fairy pays for capital expenditures?

> More money, it has been noted, has been stolen with the point of a pen than at the point of a gun.

> Charlie and I tend to be leery of companies run by CEOs who woo investors with fancy predictions. A few of these managers will prove prophetic — but others will turn out to be congenital optimists, or even charlatans. Unfortunately, it's not easy for investors to know in advance which species they are dealing with.

> The Constitution's First Amendment allows the media to print or say almost anything. Journalism's First Principle should require that the media be scrupulous in deciding what that will be.

> Beginning in 1951 my performance improved. No, I hadn't changed my diet or taken up exercise. The only new ingredient was Ben's ideas. Quite simply, a few hours spent at the feet of the master proved far more valuable to me than had ten years of supposedly original thinking.

> A small leak can sink a great ship. [Buffett quoting Ben Franklin on the home-office headcount.]

## Tax-driven preference for whole-business ownership

The structural reason Berkshire prefers buying entire businesses to stock positions: 80%-or-more ownership eliminates dividend tax (14%) and capital-gains tax (35%+) on retained earnings rolled into the cost basis. "The government is our 'partner' twice when we own part of a business through a stock investment, but only once when we own at least 80%." Leaving taxes aside, the valuation formula is identical for both.
