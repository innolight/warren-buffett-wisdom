# Berkshire Hathaway 2011 Annual Letter

Book value per A share grew 4.6% in 2011. The letter's most enduring sections are a landmark essay on investment categories (currency vs. sterile vs. productive assets), a counter-intuitive worked example of the IBM share-repurchase arithmetic, and a careful articulation of insurance underwriting discipline. The five largest non-insurance businesses (BNSF, Iscar, Lubrizol, Marmon, MidAmerican) earned more than $9B pre-tax combined.

## Key themes

- **Three investment categories** — a complete taxonomy of currency-based (bonds, cash), sterile (gold, commodities), and productive assets (businesses, farms, real estate); with the argument that only the third category reliably preserves and grows purchasing power. The gold vs. "pile B" thought experiment is the most vivid illustration in the letters.
- **IBM buyback math** — the counter-intuitive demonstration that a long-term partial owner should *want* share prices low during a buyback period; at lower prices the repurchasing company retires more shares, enlarging each holder's proportional claim on future earnings.
- **Insurance record** — float reached $70.6B, a new record; nine consecutive years of underwriting profit, totaling $17B cumulative. The letter introduces the book-value-distortion framing: float is deducted as a full liability under GAAP but is effectively a revolving fund, understating Berkshire's intrinsic value.
- **Four disciplines of sound underwriting** — the 2011 letter offers the clearest enumeration: understand all exposures, conservatively evaluate likelihood and cost, price for a profit, and walk away if the right premium isn't available. The fourth discipline is what most insurers flunk.
- **BNSF and MidAmerican records** — both set operating earnings records; BNSF moved ~37% of railroad industry ton-miles (~15% of all U.S. inter-city freight); MidAmerican committed $6B to wind generation.
- **Capital deployment** — $8.2B in property, plant and equipment across operating companies (95% in the U.S.); Bank of America $5B preferred + warrants; IBM $10.9B stake.
- **NetJets: market dominance ≠ financial performance** — Jordan Hansell's $227M pre-tax first year closes the eleven-year object lesson that industry leadership (5× nearest competitor throughout) does not guarantee financial results; cost structure is a wholly independent variable from market position.

## Notable quotes

> Investing is forgoing consumption now in order to have the ability to consume more at a later date.

> The riskiness of an investment is not measured by beta... but rather by the probability — the reasoned probability — of that investment causing its owner a loss of purchasing-power over his contemplated holding period.

> Today the world's gold stock is about 170,000 metric tons... at $1,750 per ounce — gold's price as I write this — its value would be $9.6 trillion. Call this cube pile A. Let's now create a pile B costing an equal amount. For that, we could buy all U.S. cropland (400 million acres with output of about $200 billion annually), plus 16 Exxon Mobils... After these purchases, we would have about $1 trillion left over for walking-around money.

> The first law of capital allocation — whether the money is slated for acquisitions or share repurchases — is that what is smart at one price is dumb at another.

> Bonds promoted as offering risk-free returns are now priced to deliver return-free risk. — quoting Shelby Cullom Davis

> It is no exaggeration to characterize railroads as the circulatory system of our economy. Your railroad is the largest artery.

> From a standing start in 1985, Ajit has created an insurance business with float of $34 billion and significant underwriting profits, a feat that no CEO of any other insurer has come close to matching.
