# Berkshire Hathaway 1994 Annual Meeting

The 1994 meeting is, unusually, a meeting that out-teaches the year's letter. Across ~60 unscripted Q&A exchanges Buffett and Munger restate — often in their sharpest, most quotable form — the load-bearing ideas of the whole Berkshire system: that risk is the probability of permanent loss over your holding period and not price volatility; that you need no opinion on the market, interest rates, or the economy to value a business; that capital allocation is the CEO's core, non-delegable job and most CEOs are untrained for it; and that genuine alignment is symmetric. Because it is a transcript, this page keeps the wiki's two-layer convention: a short distilled top of the durable themes, then a **comprehensive Q&A digest** that captures every value-bearing exchange as the home for detail.

## How to read the Q&A digest

- **★** marks a timeless, load-bearing exchange worth citing in the future; unmarked entries still carry investor value but are secondary or period-specific.
- Answers are attributed separately to **Buffett** and **Munger** — a Munger line appears only when he added a distinct lesson (he answered "Nothing to add" to most). This keeps the wisdom fairly credited to both.
- **M#/A#** map to the numbered items in the raw morning/afternoon transcript for traceability.

## Key themes

### Risk is the probability of permanent loss over your horizon — not volatility

Buffett's cleanest-ever statement of his risk definition lands here: risk "is inextricably wound up in your time horizon for holding an asset." Buying Coca-Cola to hold for decades is "so close to nil" in risk; buying it this morning to sell tomorrow is "a very risky transaction." Beta and volatility are not risk — "something whose return varies from year to year between plus-20 percent and plus-80 percent is riskier, as defined, than something whose return is 5 percent a year every year," which is backwards. The super-cat book proves the point: certain to lose money in some years, yet low-risk over a decade. Munger's gloss: think "like Fermat and Pascal as if they'd never heard of modern finance theory," much of which "can only be described as disgusting." See [risk-vs-volatility](../concepts/risk-vs-volatility.md).

### You need no macro view — price, don't time, and let cash be a residue

"Charlie and I never have an opinion about the market because it wouldn't be any good and it might interfere with the opinions we have that are good." The discipline generalizes: "We think about what will happen. … It's impossible, in our view, to figure out when." Macro fear is the buyer's friend — "if you're going to be buyers of groceries over time, you like grocery prices to go down" — so a falling market is good news for a net buyer. Cash is therefore never an asset-allocation call; it "piles up" only when they fail to find things to buy, and a large cash balance is "an index of failure on the part of your management." See [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

### Capital allocation is the CEO's non-delegable job — and most can't do it

The meeting's most vivid management lesson: a CEO promoted for operating skill who must now allocate capital is "like somebody playing the piano all their life, and then getting to Carnegie Hall and they hand him a violin." Over a ten-year tenure a CEO retaining 8% of equity a year allocates an enormous multiple of the company's net worth, yet "relatively few chief executives are either trained for, or are selected on, the basis of their ability to allocate capital." Delegating it to staff or consultants is "a terrible mistake." Buffett and Munger keep "practically nobody at headquarters" precisely because allocation is the one thing they will not hand off. See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md).

### Intrinsic value is discounted future cash — and certainty beats cheapness

Value "is the present value, the appropriate interest rate, of all the future streams of cash going in or out of the business" — and "there are no answers in the financial statements; there are guidelines to enable you to figure out the answer." Berkshire weights *confidence* in the cash stream over apparent cheapness: it will discount a very certain 30-year stream at a lower rate than an uncertain one, requiring "at least a 10 percent rate" in a 7% long-bond world. Crucially, growth is not value: Berkshire will "buy companies that aren't going to grow at all," and a no-growth business that throws off cash can beat a fast grower that consumes capital — "a huge difference … financial analysts do not give adequate weight to." See [intrinsic-value-vs-book-value](../concepts/intrinsic-value-vs-book-value.md) and [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md).

### Stay inside the circle; ordinary acts produce extraordinary results

Asked about Nike and Reebok, Buffett declines not from a negative view but because "I don't understand their competitive position … as well as I think I understand the position of Brown and Dexter." The corollary: "you don't have to do exceptional things to get exceptional results." He would "rather multiply by three than by pi," likes "haystacks, not needles," and rejects seller projections wholesale — Munger: "A mine is a hole in the ground owned by a liar," and "Don't ask the barber whether you need a haircut." The whole edifice is built to "avoid being wrong." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

### Alignment is symmetric and unit-specific; owners are cultivated, not pandered to

On compensation: pay big only for big *owner* results. The Salomon CEO's $24M bonus target is fine because it is "hellishly hard to hit" — "Babe Ruth squared" — while typical option grants reward managers who merely "tread water." On ownership: Berkshire refuses to split its stock ("carving ownerships … into little, tiny $20 pieces is almost insane" — Munger) and refuses to publish an intrinsic-value estimate, because the goal is to attract long-horizon owners and not "encourage" traders. The stop-loss order that sells at 15,500 what the holder wouldn't sell at 16,000 is the anti-pattern: "the lower it went, the better they liked it." See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md) and [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

## Q&A digest — Morning session

**★ M11 · Derivatives.** *Buffett:* they invite enormous leverage and are often not understood by those using them; "any time you combine ignorance and borrowed money, you can get some pretty interesting consequences" (P&G's interest-rate swaps that became puts on bonds). See [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md), [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md).

**M12 · "Permanent" holdings can still tender.** *Buffett:* selling 1M Cap Cities shares into a buyback doesn't break the permanent-holding stance — Berkshire participated in Post and GEICO repurchases too, and remains the largest holder. Repurchases at a fair price are good use of an investee's cash. (See also M42.) See [capital-cities](../entities/capital-cities.md), [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md).

**★ M13 · Minimum cash-flow yield / discount rate.** *Buffett:* would conceivably buy a business with *no* current cash flow if its future were tremendous (cf. buying GEICO while it was losing money); discounts future after-tax streams at "at least a 10 percent rate" in a 7% bond world, and plays closer the more certain the business. *Munger:* "We don't care what we report in the first year or two of … buying anything." See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md).

**M14 · Insurance intrinsic value far exceeds book.** *Buffett:* the float table — growth from ~$20M to ~$3B at attractive cost — shows the insurance business's excess of intrinsic over carrying value is the largest of any Berkshire business, in *absolute dollars* (clarified in A18). See [float](../concepts/float.md), [national-indemnity](../entities/national-indemnity.md).

**★ M15 · Why no stock split.** *Munger:* "the idea of carving ownerships … into little, tiny $20 pieces is almost insane" — servicing tiny accounts is inefficient and there should be a minimum to join. *Buffett:* policies that attract the wrong holders only displace this ideal constituency — "part of our eugenics program here at Berkshire." (See M25.) See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**★ M18 · Two yardsticks for judging management.** *Buffett:* (1) how well they run the business — judged by capital allocation over time, versus competitors, given the hand they were dealt; (2) how well they treat owners — read the proxy, compare how they treat themselves vs. shareholders. The two usually go together; you only need a few correct judgments. See [buffett-management-criteria](../synthesis/buffett-management-criteria.md).

**★ M19 · Keeping great managers.** *Buffett:* "find the .400 hitters and then not tell them how to swing"; compensate on their own unit's results, never on Berkshire-wide performance; most are already financially independent, so make the work fun. No company-wide comp plan, no consultants. *Munger:* treat the other fellow as you'd want to be treated if roles were reversed — "we like our relationships to be more than a business relationship." See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).

**M20 · Guinness / scotch.** *Buffett:* well-run, but worldwide scotch volume "is nothing to write home about" — don't count on physical growth; a currency (pound) loss since purchase. Honest assessment of a holding's limits without overclaiming. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**M21 · Berkshire after Buffett.** *Buffett:* the ownership structure doesn't change if he dies tonight; managers run themselves and capital could "do worse than just adding it to positions we already had." *Munger:* prospects "obviously diminished … but it would still be one hell of a company."

**M24 · Blue Chip Stamps' 99.5% decline.** *Buffett & Munger:* a self-deprecating joke — "we have presided over a decline of 99½ percent" — that carries the float lesson: the trading-stamp business "only works because of the float." See [blue-chip-stamps](../entities/blue-chip-stamps.md), [float](../concepts/float.md).

**★ M27 · Don't price by what others say.** *Buffett:* on a Barron's valuation — "you really should not make decisions in securities based on what other people think… A public opinion poll will not get you rich on Wall Street. You cannot get rich with a weather vane." See [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md).

**M28 · Bank buybacks, case-by-case.** *Buffett:* repurchasing at an attractive price is a fine use of capital — a bank "probably knew more about their own bank than some other bank they were going to buy." Judge per bank, not as a rule. See [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md).

**★ M29 · Economics over accounting.** *Buffett:* "it absolutely makes no difference to us what accounting treatment we get… We are interested in the economics." Berkshire mentally recasts every business to a standardized form (pooling vs. purchase); a management "preoccupied with accounting considerations" is a negative. *Munger:* published figures "are a place to start economic analysis… frequently quite silly on a functional basis." See [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md), [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md).

**M30 · Salomon leverage in good hands.** *Buffett:* a 30-to-1 levered business is tolerable only with the right operators; recounts how Maughan, Denham, and Macfarlane held the firm together through the 1991 funding crisis. Leverage magnifies the quality (or absence) of the people controlling it. See [salomon](../entities/salomon.md), [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md).

**★ M31 · Change your mind; vote with your feet.** *Munger:* on exiting Wesco's small S&L as regulation tightened — quotes Keynes ("when he got new facts or new insights … he changed his mind") and adds "There's a time to vote with your feet." Exiting a sound-but-shrinking position is rational, not inconsistent. See [wesco-financial](../entities/wesco-financial.md).

**★ M32 · Great managers vs. a tough industry.** *Buffett:* the shoe business "hasn't been too good" except with operators like Rooney, Issler, and Lunder; "whenever we've got talent we like to … give them as big a domain as we can," hinting at expansion. *Hindsight flag:* the industry won — Dexter became one of Buffett's worst deals, a caution that great managers rarely rescue bad economics (contrast the USAir lesson he states in M40). See [dexter-shoe](../entities/dexter-shoe.md), [commodity-business-economics](../concepts/commodity-business-economics.md).

**M33 · Tobacco.** *Buffett:* the economics "may be fine, but that doesn't mean it has a great future" — the unknowable variable is societal attitude and liability, on which "you can form an opinion just as well as I could." A risk that sits outside the circle. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**M34 · Product utility — "hard to argue with the market."** *Buffett:* judge a long-available product's utility by the market's revealed vote (Coke's 750M daily servings, Dexter's repeat buyers) rather than an independent guess. *Munger:* taken on average across the portfolio.

**★ M36 · Super-cats: the industry's "head in the sand."** *Buffett:* the LA quake was "peanuts on that scale"; a worst-case Long Island hurricane could cost Berkshire $400–700M and leave major insurers "in significant trouble." The industry "vastly underestimated" PMLs until Andrew and the quake were a "wake-up call." See [super-cat-insurance](../concepts/super-cat-insurance.md).

**M37 · Reading list.** *Buffett:* Janet Lowe's forthcoming Ben Graham biography; Geoff Cowan's *The People v. Clarence Darrow*. *Munger:* Connie Bruck's *Master of the Game* (Steve Ross) and Van Doren's *Benjamin Franklin* — Franklin "believed in compound interest, too."

**★ M38 · Stay inside the circle (Nike/Reebok).** *Buffett:* declines to assess them — not negative, but "I don't understand their competitive position … as well as I … understand Brown and Dexter. They may be easier for other people." Abstaining without a verdict is the discipline. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ M39 · Ordinary acts, extraordinary results.** *Buffett:* "you don't have to do exceptional things to get exceptional results"; would "rather multiply by three than by pi"; "the big thing to do is avoid being wrong"; refuses seller projections — "we almost paid 2 million not to look at it." *Munger:* hiring a label to do something intrinsically hard is "one of the most dangerous ideas"; "A mine is a hole in the ground owned by a liar." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ M40 · USAir — don't buy businesses that need such fixes.** *Buffett:* a non-viable cost structure; "the answer is not to get into businesses that need to solve problems like that." Even an able CEO (Schofield) may not extract hundreds of millions in costs. *Munger:* the labor side "is in the lap of the gods." See [usair](../entities/usair.md), [commodity-business-economics](../concepts/commodity-business-economics.md).

**M44 · Won't comment on holdings (Wrigley).** *Buffett:* won't discuss what Berkshire might buy or sell, but "you can understand the Wrigley Company just as well as I can." *Munger:* "I'm good at not being forthcoming."

**M45 · No regional mandate.** *Buffett:* "no specific desire" to be in or avoid any region — only "businesses that we understand, run by people that we like, and priced attractively." Coke and Gillette already deliver large look-through international earnings. See [look-through-earnings](../concepts/look-through-earnings.md).

**★ M47 · No market opinion — what will happen, not when.** *Buffett:* "We think about what will happen… It's impossible to figure out when." Bought his first stock in April 1942 amid a losing war; the company that became Berkshire sold for ~$2,000 in 1890 versus ~$50B now — waiting for the all-clear was never worth it. *Munger:* being "agnostic about those macro factors" is "a way more efficient way to behave." See [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

**★ M48 · Whole companies vs. market bargains.** *Buffett:* prefers buying whole businesses (tax + temperament), but "the market is far more inefficient in pricing businesses than is the negotiated market" — you never buy a bargain from an intelligent seller. *Munger:* corporate and LBO buyers wield "somebody else's money" and have "the upside, but not the downside," so they bid up whole businesses; "animal spirits" are the competition. See [cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses](../concepts/cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses.md).

**M49 · Why not publish intrinsic value (the Wesco "quirk").** *Munger:* disclosed Wesco's ~$100/share estimate only because buyers "had gone a little crazy" at ~$130 — "a one-time quirk," not a policy change; Berkshire won't, because its insurance value alone could be estimated "three times" differently by reasonable people. (See M60.) See [intrinsic-value-vs-book-value](../concepts/intrinsic-value-vs-book-value.md).

**★ M50 · Risk is permanent loss over the horizon, not volatility.** *Buffett:* risk is "the possibility of harm or injury… inextricably wound up in your time horizon." Willing to lose on any single arbitrage or policy, never on the *expectancy* of a set of independent bets; would stake a meaningful sum on a favorable coin. *Munger:* "think like Fermat and Pascal as if they'd never heard of modern finance theory"; much of it "can only be described as disgusting." See [risk-vs-volatility](../concepts/risk-vs-volatility.md).

**M52 · Not a "rich man's mutual fund."** *Buffett:* it's a collection of businesses they'd ideally own outright — "the same thing Jack Welch is trying to do at General Electric," only more hands-off. See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**★ M53 · Interest-rate sensitivity vs. needing a rate view.** *Buffett:* every asset's valuation is "100 percent sensitive to interest rates," because investing is trading cash now for a future stream. But whether a Wells Fargo or Freddie Mac does better or worse *operationally* under higher rates is hard to figure — so "I do not have to have a view on interest rates … to make a decision." See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md).

**★ M55 · "Swimming naked when the tide goes out."** *Buffett:* reinsurance is a business where "some very stupid things are done en masse periodically… you don't find out who's been swimming naked until the tide goes out." Hidden errors surface only under stress. See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**★ M56 · Cash piles up by failure, not choice.** *Buffett:* "we would never have an asset allocation meeting"; cash accumulates only when they can't find things to buy, and "you can look at that as an index of failure." A sharp behavioral note: "the best purchases are usually made when you have to sell something to raise the money," because it raises the bar. See [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

**M57 · Good business ≠ good investment (Fannie/Freddie).** *Buffett:* their market share is "essentially certain to go up" given unmatched low-cost intermediation — "that doesn't mean those are wonderful businesses to buy." Separates a strong competitive position from an attractive price. See [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md).

**★ M58 · Speed of information is irrelevant.** *Buffett:* performs "about like … 30 or 40 years ago" — what matters is processing and judgment, not fast data; "we like haystacks, not needles… we want it to shout at us." You could invest fine "where the mails were delayed three weeks." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**M59 · Why buybacks are unlikely.** *Buffett:* conditions cheap enough to make a Berkshire repurchase smart will usually make outside businesses even cheaper, because Berkshire's holders are more rational; doesn't rule it out, but the case rarely arises. See [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md).

**M60 · Don't publish intrinsic value — give the inputs.** *Buffett:* "we try to give you all of the numbers that we would use ourselves… then we want you to make the calculation." *Munger:* the firms that "constantly told their shareholders what the intrinsic value was were the real estate holding companies" — Webb & Knapp announced it "to eight decimal places … right till the day they filed for Chapter 11." See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**M61 · Peter Lynch.** *Buffett:* admiring — "there's more than one way to get to heaven… Peter's got one, and we've got one." Lynch diversifies far more; each would do worse trying to run the other's method. See [concentration-vs-diversification](../concepts/concentration-vs-diversification.md).

**★ M62 · Price insurance to exposure, not experience.** *Buffett:* "We price to what we think is exposure. We don't price to experience" — a quiet hurricane year shouldn't cut next year's rates. Capital-allocation flexibility lets Berkshire decline underpriced business "with a certain equanimity that most managements can't," because they have alternatives. "You cannot let the market think for you." *Munger:* Berkshire is "a very old-fashioned place" that disciplines itself to stay with "the eternal verities." See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

## Q&A digest — Afternoon session

**A2 · World Book's slide — "I wish I knew the answer."** *Buffett:* candidly can't fully explain falling unit sales (electronic competition? sales-force recruiting?), praises Schey's profit defense, makes no prediction. Intellectual honesty about a secular decline he doesn't understand. See [scott-fetzer](../entities/scott-fetzer.md).

**A3 · Breaking up Berkshire wouldn't help.** *Buffett:* "Berkshire Two, Three, Four" solves nothing — "there's $10 billion to invest" regardless. *Munger:* operations are "incredibly decentralized," securities "incredibly centralized"; the size penalty hasn't bitten yet, but "eventually we will." See [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md).

**A4 · General Dynamics.** *Buffett:* won't discuss buying/selling, but its management did "an absolutely sensational job"; "it isn't the kind of business … that we have a 20-year view on." A holding bought for a specific re-rating, not forever.

**★ A5 · Stop-loss orders and the wrong owners.** *Buffett:* a holder's stop-loss to sell at 15,500 what they wouldn't sell at 16,000 is irrational — "the lower it went, the better they liked it"; like telling your broker to sell your $100,000 house if anyone offers 90. Berkshire courts long-horizon owners and "will do nothing to encourage" traders. See [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md), [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**A6 · Berkshire benefits when the market falls.** *Buffett:* as a net cash generator it keeps buying; "if you asked us … whether Berkshire would be better off if the whole stock market were down 50 percent or where it is now, we would be better off if it was down 50 percent." (Reinforces M47/M56.) See [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

**A7 · Salomon comp "far from ideal."** *Buffett:* better pay would have produced better results — "the trick is to pay … only when you're getting very big results for the owners"; the old system "wasn't totally off the mark … but far from ideal." See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).

**A8 · Coke puts.** *Buffett:* wrote puts on ~5M Coke shares for a $7.5M premium, but "we're better off … buying the security itself" — broker position limits and scale make it a poor use of time. See [coca-cola](../entities/coca-cola.md).

**A9 · Don't believe press on what they're buying.** *Buffett:* "well over 50 percent" of reports of Berkshire purchases are wrong; they never correct them (correcting the false ones would identify the true ones) — and the noise is "probably useful to us."

**A10 · Unlikely to be a Dow/S&P 500 stock.** *Buffett:* a thought experiment — if Berkshire were indexed and 60% of the market indexed while he and Munger never sold, the funds could never replicate the weight (a "Northern Pacific Corner"). An early flag on concentrated ownership vs. mechanical indexing. See [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md).

**★ A11 · Concentrate if you understand; diversify if you don't.** *Buffett:* ran his partnership with up to 40% in one stock and would again with small capital; "if you don't understand businesses, then you're better off … widely diversifying." *Munger:* the heir who kept a two-stock estate against a trust officer's diversification advice — "if my father believed the way you do, he might have been a trust officer in a bank instead of leaving this large estate." See [concentration-vs-diversification](../concepts/concentration-vs-diversification.md).

**A13 · Grading their own capital decisions.** *Buffett:* the Allianz convertible was "maybe a tiny minus"; *Munger:* "close to a wash" — then volunteers USAir as "one we would have been well to duck." Candor about mistakes as a governance norm. See [mistakes-of-omission](../concepts/mistakes-of-omission.md).

**★ A14 · Size is a disadvantage.** *Buffett:* market impact when selling large illiquid holdings "can be very significant"; "Size is a disadvantage … both in buying and in selling. And we don't know any way around that." They allow for it in required return. See [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md).

**A15 · No key-man insurance.** *Buffett:* none on him or Munger — it "wouldn't be material," and besides "the math of intelligently selling insurance is better than the math of intelligently buying insurance."

**★ A17 · Berkshire doesn't hedge currencies.** *Buffett:* owning Coke is owning "a bunch of foreign bonds with coupons … denominated in local currencies, that go on forever" — and "we don't think our opinion on currencies is any good… we do not know more than the market." Hedging a to-Judgment-Day stream is costly and futile; they'd slightly prefer dollars but "don't lose sleep" over the mix. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**A20 · The $24M bonus "hellishly hard to hit."** *Munger:* Maughan's target (30% on equity, 10 points above peers) is "Babe Ruth … doing 150 home runs in a season." *Buffett:* "if that happens, you'll be very glad to pay the money." Big pay is fine only for outlier owner results. See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).

**A21 · Easier to understand ≠ better buy.** *Buffett:* Coke's future is easier to read than Salomon's, "but that doesn't mean it's a better buy"; current holdings reflect "historical accident … of when we bought." Investment-banking and commercial-banking both carry inescapable systemic risk.

**★ A22 · How to calculate intrinsic value.** *Buffett:* present value of all future cash at the appropriate rate; "there are no answers in the financial statements — there are guidelines." Prioritizes certainty over the apparent cheapest number; if you'll price a stock today but can't estimate its 20-year cash, "you've got cognitive dissonance." See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [intrinsic-value-vs-book-value](../concepts/intrinsic-value-vs-book-value.md).

**★ A23 · Growth alone doesn't make a good investment.** *Buffett:* willing to "buy companies that aren't going to grow at all"; would you rather own a savings account paying 10% flat or 2% growing 10%? "There's a huge difference in the business that grows and requires a lot of capital … and the business that grows and doesn't require capital." Berkshire's best wholly-owned businesses don't grow but throw off cash to redeploy. See [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md), [return-on-equity-yardstick](../concepts/return-on-equity-yardstick.md).

**★ A24 · Capital allocation can't be delegated.** *Buffett:* "mail all the money to Omaha" — they allocate it themselves because it's "one of two or three key functions at … 80 or 90 percent of all companies," yet CEOs "get there through other routes": the piano player handed a violin at Carnegie Hall. *Munger:* "practically nobody at headquarters … this makes Southwest Airlines look like they don't understand cost accounting." See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md), [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**A25 · Keynes and Graham.** *Buffett:* read the markets chapter of Keynes's *General Theory* (he guesses ch. 8; it is ch. 12) alongside Graham's chapters 8 and 20 of *The Intelligent Investor* — "you'll get as much wisdom … as anything written in investments." Keynes began by predicting cycles, then "shifted to fundamental analysis … and did extremely well." See [ben-graham](../people/ben-graham.md), [margin-of-safety](../concepts/margin-of-safety.md).

## Connections to other sources

- "Don't ask the barber whether you need a haircut" is the meeting-floor cousin of the "interior decorator … $50,000 rug" line in [berkshire-1994-letter](berkshire-1994-letter.md) — both attack advisor-driven decisions; the letter aims at acquisition advisors, the meeting at seller projections.
- The USAir post-mortem (M40, A13) is the same admission that culminates in [berkshire-1994-letter](berkshire-1994-letter.md)'s $269M writedown and the commodity-cost diagnosis; the meeting adds the forward rule — "don't get into businesses that need to solve problems like that."
- The Dexter Shoe praise (M32) reads as a supersession warning when set against [berkshire-1994-letter](berkshire-1994-letter.md)'s franchise-vs-business framework: the managers were superb, but the industry economics (later) won — the negative case that USAir was already proving in the same meeting.
- The capital-allocation and "growth needs capital" themes extend the owner-earnings and ROE-yardstick reasoning developed across [berkshire-1992-letter](berkshire-1992-letter.md) and earlier letters, here compressed into the savings-account analogy.
