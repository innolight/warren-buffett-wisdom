# Berkshire Hathaway 2004 Annual Meeting

The 2004 meeting, the first held at the new Qwest Center (19,500 attendees — a record), sits in the middle of a cash-pile problem Buffett and Munger can't yet solve, a derivatives-risk warning they've been repeating since 2002 and are about to be proved right about, and a governance eruption — ISS and CalPERS trying to push Buffett off Coca-Cola's board — that gives Buffett his sharpest riff of the decade on the difference between checklists and thinking. The dominant analytical currents: a $30B-plus cash hoard awaiting elephant-sized acquisitions that aren't coming; a renewed and increasingly specific derivatives warning ("I would predict that sometime in the next 10 years you will have some very big problems"); an extended National Indemnity no-layoff lesson that is the most complete statement Buffett has ever given of how incentive design produces underwriting discipline; and the compensation philosophy in its bluntest form — Munger offering to throw a viper down his shirtfront before hiring a consultant. Per the wiki's two-layer convention, a short distilled thematic top precedes the comprehensive Q&A digest.

## How to read the Q&A digest

- **★** marks a timeless, load-bearing exchange worth citing in the future; unmarked entries carry value but are secondary or period-specific.
- Answers are attributed separately to **Buffett** and **Munger** — a Munger line appears only when he added a distinct lesson.
- **M#/A#** map to the numbered items in the raw morning/afternoon transcript for traceability. Items M1–M7 are formal business; Q&A opens at M8.

## Key themes

### 1. Checklists are no substitute for thinking — the Coke board controversy

ISS and CalPERS recommended shareholders vote against Buffett's re-election to Coca-Cola's board on the grounds that his ties to Berkshire subsidiaries that do business with Coke created a conflict. Buffett's response is the meeting's most concentrated governance lesson: "checklists are no substitute for thinking." The conflict argument is arithmetically absurd — Berkshire holds $10 billion of Coke stock and receives roughly $100,000 per year in director fees; treating the $100,000 as the alignment test while ignoring the $10,000,000,000 gets the proportionality exactly backwards. Munger sharpens it: activist reform is a legitimate cause, but "the cause of reform is hurt, not helped, when an activist makes an idiotic suggestion." The governance lesson generalizes: mechanical criteria — independence boxes, conflict checklists — can produce outcomes that undermine the very owner-alignment they are meant to protect. See [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md). *(M8)*

### 2. Derivatives: "big problems" in the next 10 years — a specific prediction

Buffett issues a named, time-bound prediction: "I would predict that sometime in the next 10 years, that you will have some very big problems that will either be caused by, or accentuated in a big way, by people's activities in derivatives." The mechanism had been stated at the 2002 and 2003 meetings — risk concentration, not dispersion — but 2004 adds two new specifics. First: Freddie Mac's $6 billion restatement, achieved despite triple-A directors, dozens of covering analysts, and a dedicated oversight office, is proof that no amount of external scrutiny substitutes for honest management; derivatives facilitated the whole mischief. Second: at Salomon on a Sunday in 1991, Berkshire was within hours of watching $1.2 trillion in notional derivative contracts land in bankruptcy court — "a judge eating popcorn watching baseball." The model Munger adds is that collateral-trigger clauses — meant to protect counterparties — are precisely what turns a local difficulty into a cascade: "when you add lying to the process, it's a Mad Hatter's Tea-Party." See [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md), [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md).

> **Supersession flag:** Validated emphatically in 2007–2009. AIG wrote credit default swaps it could not pay; Lehman Brothers' derivatives book froze global markets; Freddie Mac and Fannie Mae entered U.S. government conservatorship in September 2008 — exactly within Buffett's ten-year window.

### 3. Cash is an index of failure — the elephant problem

Berkshire exits 2003 with $30-odd billion in cash and cannot deploy it. Buffett is unambiguous that this is a management failure, not a strategic posture: "Cash may pile up faster than we can use it, in which case we'll have to rethink the whole game." The problem is scale — they now need elephants. A shareholder's detailed DCF of Berkshire's intrinsic value prompts Buffett to warn that his own growth projections would be lower than the shareholder's precisely because of size: "It gets harder all the time to deploy the kind of funds that keep flowing into Omaha." The structural constraint is the same one raised at the 2003 and earlier meetings, but by 2004 the cash pile is large enough to be a standalone concern. See [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md), [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md). *(A28)*

### 4. Underwriting discipline: never lay off a soul, never write a bad policy

The most extended business-process discussion of any 2004 topic is Buffett's slide-illustrated history of National Indemnity's premium volume — from $16M at acquisition to $366M at the 1986 hard-market peak, then down to $55M over years of declining rates, then back up to nearly $600M. No comparable public company could have survived a volume decline like that without layoffs. Berkshire not only survived it but profited almost every year — because the explicit message to every National Indemnity employee was: "If you write no business, your job is not in jeopardy." The behavioral logic is airtight: an unspoken threat of layoffs forces underwriters to write business at any price. Once that culture takes root, it is almost impossible to dislodge. High expense ratios during the lean years (peaking at 41% in 1999) are a cheaper price to pay than writing bad business at any expense ratio. Munger: "Practically nobody else does it. And yet to me it's obvious it's the way to go." See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md), [national-indemnity](../entities/national-indemnity.md). *(A12)*

### 5. Compensation: simple, unit-specific, and never delegated to consultants

The 2004 meeting gives the Berkshire compensation philosophy its most vivid expressions. Buffett designs comp plans in minutes on a yellow pad (MidAmerican's arrangement took three minutes to sketch), never brings in consultants ("I would rather throw a viper down my shirtfront" — Munger), keeps them simple enough to fit in a paragraph, and matches them to each business's own economics rather than applying a company-wide formula. The structural insight: comp committees don't put Dobermans on them, they put "Chihuahuas that have been sedated" — and consultants never say the CEO is worth less than they're paid. The result is a ratchet: every CEO thinks they're in the top quartile, and the pie just keeps growing. The counter-model at Berkshire is GEICO's two-variable plan (growth and profitability) that has run unchanged since Berkshire took control. See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md). *(M11, A20)*

### 6. Leverage is the only thing that forces you off a winning hand

The meeting revisits leverage from a new angle: the single reason smart people with correct analysis get wiped out. Buffett uses the 2002 junk bond episode — securities that went from 35–40% yields to 6% in twelve months — to make the point clean: "you could have thought junk bonds were wonderful at 15 percent... But if you owed a lot of money against them in between, you wouldn't have been around for the party at the end." The principle generalizes: "The only way smart people can get clobbered, really, is through leverage. If you can hold them, you have no real problems." Berkshire's structural commitment is to be the last institution standing in a financial cataclysm rather than the first casualty — and the commitment is architectural, not aspirational: no leverage, always liquid, always ready to buy when others are forced to sell. See [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md), [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md). *(M32, A33)*

### 7. Low-cost producers win — always and in any era

Buffett traces the evolution of auto insurance distribution — casualty affiliates of fire companies → State Farm's captive agency force (1920s) → USAA/GEICO's direct model (1936+) → Progressive's recent pivot toward direct — to make the general case: "It's always a good idea to go with a low-cost producer over time. I mean, you could mess it up in other ways, but being a low-cost producer of something that's essential to people, it's going to be a very good business usually." The same model works at Costco and Walmart. Dell's supply-chain-light PC manufacturing is the analogy for manufactured goods. The structural advantage is durable because lower costs enable lower prices, which expand the customer base, which funds further scale — a self-reinforcing loop that pure product differentiation cannot easily undercut. See [moat](../concepts/moat.md), [geico](../entities/geico.md). *(A16)*

## Q&A digest — Morning session

**★ M8 · ISS checklists vs. thinking — the Coke board controversy.** *Buffett:* ISS and CalPERS wanted him off Coca-Cola's board due to alleged conflicts (FlightSafety training revenues, Dairy Queen purchases of Coke product). Rebuttal: "checklists are no substitute for thinking." With $10 billion of Coke stock, Berkshire is on exactly the same side as every other shareholder; the director who receives $100K as their *entire* income is "100 percent dependent" while Berkshire — receiving the same $100K against $10 billion of aligned ownership — is treated as "not independent." A person pulled from a welfare line would be deemed independent; Berkshire is not. *Munger:* "The cause of reform is hurt, not helped, when an activist makes an idiotic suggestion." See [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md), [coca-cola](../entities/coca-cola.md).

**M9 · Inflation-resistant businesses.** *Buffett:* best defense against inflation is a business that can price in inflationary terms without requiring proportional capital reinvestment — See's Candy is the canonical example. Most businesses are forced to put more and more dollars into the business just to hold the same real volume. TIPS are not a bad alternative for investors worried about inflation heating up. *Munger:* "most people are going to get a very small real return from investment after considering inflation and taxes. I think that's an iron law of the world." See [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md).

**M10 · No analyst subgroup meetings.** *Buffett:* resists holding special meetings for institutional investors — giving any subgroup private access violates the egalitarian information policy. Equally important: one of Berkshire's promises to managers is that they never have to talk to analysts; they spend 100% of their time running the business. *Munger:* "One of Berkshire's strengths has been that we don't spend time in that way." See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**★ M11 · Compensation: Chihuahuas, vipers, and three-minute plans.** *Buffett:* MidAmerican's comp plan was sketched in three minutes on a yellow pad; the See's Candy arrangement set in 1972 is still in force; Fruit of the Loom's plan fits in a paragraph. "No two businesses that have the same arrangement." Comp committees put "Chihuahuas that have been sedated" on them, not Dobermans — and consultants never say the CEO is overpaid. *Munger:* "I would rather throw a viper down my shirtfront than hire a compensation consultant." See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).

**★ M12 · No investment categories — junk bonds and currencies as examples.** *Buffett:* "We don't think about the categories by themselves... we don't go to the office in the morning thinking what category." In summer/fall 2002 junk bonds appeared screaming "buy, buy, buy" at 30–35–40% yields-to-maturity; twelve months later some went to 6%. Also active in currencies (significant positions not yet fully disclosed). Cash is what remains when nothing crosses the threshold — not an asset-allocation call. *Munger:* "We have all this cash because we don't much like any of those fields at the moment. Spending all the time thinking about orders of precedency among things you clearly are not going to do is pretty fruitless." See [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md), [investment-categories](../concepts/investment-categories.md).

**M13 · Danger of projecting high growth rates.** *Buffett:* If a growth rate exceeds the discount rate in a DCF, the answer is infinity — "and there are a lot of managements around who like to think their stocks are worth infinity, but we haven't found one yet." The "St. Petersburg Paradox" (Durand, ~1970s) addresses this. The Fortune 500 going back 50 years shows very few companies sustaining above 10% growth for extended periods; above 15% "you're in the atmosphere." Projecting high growth "has caused investors to lose very, very large sums of money." See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md).

**M14 · NYSE specialist system.** *Munger:* generally thinks it has worked well — "there may have been a few troubles lately, but averaged out, it's worked pretty well for a long time." Buffett reveals that Munger and Jack Wheeler ran a specialist operation in General Motors on the Pacific Coast Stock Exchange for about 13 years.

**★ M15 · Derivatives: "big problems" prediction — Freddie Mac and Salomon.** *Buffett:* Freddie Mac misstated earnings by $6 billion, facilitated substantially by derivatives, despite "two of the smartest and highest-grade people in fixed income" on the board plus a dedicated oversight office plus dozens of Wall Street analysts. Gen Re Securities "had its mind around" what was happening — "we tried to get our mind around it. We couldn't do it." Salomon in 1991 was hours from filing bankruptcy papers that would have left a Manhattan judge "watching baseball, eating popcorn" responsible for $1.2 trillion of notional derivative contracts. "I would predict that sometime, in the next 10 years, that you will have some very big problems that will either be caused by, or accentuated in a big way, by people's activities in derivatives." *Munger:* "In engineering, people put big margins of safety into systems. In derivatives, it's as though nobody gave a damn about safety. Somebody has to have the common sense to say, 'We're just not going there.'" *Supersession flag: validated by AIG/Lehman/Fannie/Freddie in 2007–09.* See [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md).

**M16 · Succession: four internal candidates.** *Buffett:* Bill Gates "could do my job very well" but "we have at least four people within the Berkshire organization that, in many respects, could do my job better than I do." More blessed in this than at any point in Berkshire's history. "We will have a leader that succeeds me that comes from within Berkshire." The cultural advantage of an internal successor: they've lived the culture; Berkshire has seen their pluses and minuses. See [berkshire-hathaway](../entities/berkshire-hathaway.md).

**M17 · Reading, temperament, and the "business mind."** *Buffett:* spent more time looking at companies when younger; if doing it over, same approach — "we would look at everything in sight that we thought we could understand." There is nothing different about analyzing securities now versus 50 years ago except there are more companies. *Munger:* "I don't know anybody who is wise who doesn't read a lot. On the other hand, that alone won't do it. You have to have a temperament that grabs the correct ideas and does something with them." *Buffett:* Phil Carret called it a "money mind" — "It's not a business that requires extraordinary intellect. It does require extraordinary discipline." Book recommendations: Munger recommends *Deep Simplicity* by John Gribbin; Buffett is reading *A Short History of Nearly Everything*. Isaac Newton lost a fortune in the South Sea Bubble. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**M18 · Tax shelters: institutional corruption.** *Buffett:* Pamela Olson of the Treasury has been "cracking down on tax shelters and some things that Charlie and I think shouldn't exist" — the tax-shelter arrangements sold by the biggest accounting firms (for contingent fees, in proprietary form, structured through ~20 offshore entities to make the aggregate invisible) are "absolutely disgusting." They effectively transfer taxes from the corporations using them to the middle class. *Munger:* "One of them actually explained to me that they were an ethical seller of fraudulent tax shelters... We just sold them to our 20 most important clients so they were more likely to stay secret." See [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md).

**★ M19 · Numeracy is necessary; advanced math is counterproductive.** *Munger:* "If you're innumerate, you're going to be a klutz." The good news for business: "you don't have to know any high math." In fact, knowing advanced math is likely a disadvantage — it creates a tendency to look for opportunities to use the complicated tool when the simple analysis is correct. *Buffett:* "When my mother sang me songs about compound interest, there really wasn't any need to go further." See [model-risk](../concepts/model-risk.md).

**★ M20 · The $10 billion Walmart mistake — anchoring to initial price.** *Buffett:* tried to buy 100 million Walmart shares (pre-split) at ~$23; after buying some it moved up a fraction and he stopped, waiting for it to come back. "I cost us about 10 billion." The behavioral error: getting "anchored, in effect, to some initial price when I could have paid more subsequently and it really was inconsequential." *Munger:* "At least we are constantly thinking about the past occasions when we blew opportunities. Since those don't hit financial reports, most people don't bother thinking about them." See [mistakes-of-omission](../concepts/mistakes-of-omission.md), [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md).

**★ M21 · Finding honest investment managers is very hard.** *Buffett:* when winding down his partnership in 1969, with partners to redirect, he could only identify two people he trusted — Sandy Gottesman and Bill Ruane — after years of active involvement in the investment world. "The promotional types going around to solicit institutional investors are very unlikely to meet any long-term tests of ability, and sometimes, integrity." *Munger:* a significant fraction of mutual fund managers "accepted propositions to take bribes for betraying their own shareholders — it was as if a man came to you and said, 'I have a wonderful proposition. Why don't I kill your mother and we'll split the insurance money?' And a significant number of the people said, 'Gee, I would like some insurance money.'" See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md), [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md).

**★ M22 · Asset allocation models: "pure nonsense."** *Buffett:* "We think the best way to minimize risk is to think." The 60/40-to-65/35 "strategic rebalancing" announced by Wall Street strategists "has to be pure nonsense." The right default position is always short-term instruments; move into whatever crosses the threshold on a given day. *Munger:* "There's always been a market for people who purported to know the future based on their expertise... It's just as crazy as when the king was hiring the forecaster who looked at the sheep guts." The real cost: mutual fund investors shuffled from fund to fund by commission-hungry brokers end up 3–4 percentage points below even the already-below-market average fund performance. See [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md), [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md).

**M24 · PUHCA repeal: no bonanza.** *Buffett:* the Public Utility Holding Company Act of 1935 is long outmoded, but its repeal wouldn't necessarily benefit Berkshire — competitors currently blocked by it would also be freed. "It will be no bonanza for us at all if it goes away. It may make life simpler on some very large transaction." *Munger:* if a wonderful opportunity arose under PUHCA, they'd find a way to do it anyway, probably through MidAmerican. See [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md).

**M25 · HomeServices real estate will grow.** *Buffett:* HomeServices currently handles ~$50B of transactions annually across ~15–16 local firms (Southern California, Minnesota, Iowa, Omaha, Lincoln). "We will end up — unquestionably, in my view — we'll end up buying either a few or a whole lot of additional companies over the next 10 years." Model: full local autonomy, no national brand identity (unlike Cendant), comparable to the broader Berkshire subsidiary model. See [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md).

**M27 · Succession: family + managers as culture guardians.** *Buffett:* all Berkshire stock eventually goes to the foundation; the family members on the board are "guardians of the culture — not there to profit themselves." The Walmart analogy: the Walton family has "done a magnificent job" selecting successors who reinforced rather than diluted Sam Walton's culture. *Munger:* having known the Buffett family for decades — "Don't worry about it. You should be so lucky." See [berkshire-hathaway](../entities/berkshire-hathaway.md), [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**★ M28 · Technology won't raise profits as a % of GDP — the tiptoes analogy.** *Buffett:* "technology is just as likely to reduce profits as to increase profits." In the textile business, every new machine promising a 40% IRR and eliminating 43 employees produced zero net benefit because competitors adopted the same machines — "like everybody at a parade stands up on tiptoes and 10 seconds later everybody is up on tiptoes and they're not seeing any better and their legs hurt." In a competitive system, improvements flow to consumers, not to aggregate profits. Technology, EVA, and better management will not move after-tax profits as a share of GDP materially from their historical 4–6.5% band. See [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md), [commodity-business-economics](../concepts/commodity-business-economics.md).

**M29 · Margin of safety: conservative on each variable, then add the margin.** *Buffett:* approach each variable — discount rate, growth, reserves — as realistically as possible with errors on the conservative side, then apply a margin of safety at the end rather than compounding conservatism at every step. Uses earthquake-underwriting as the analogy: 26 California 6.0+ earthquakes in the past century; assume ~30–32 in the next; price for that number; then charge more than the fair premium to build in the margin. Munger adds that earthquake-size distributions follow a known power law. See [margin-of-safety](../concepts/margin-of-safety.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**M30 · Regulation and moats — no one-size formula.** *Buffett:* regulatory impact varies enormously by business. For furniture retail, barely a factor; for insurance and energy, potentially decisive. *Munger:* they over-estimated regulatory risk early in their careers — avoided television stations for years because licenses could be revoked, but the system evolved so that "almost never happened." "Tom Murphy figured that one out before we did." See [moat](../concepts/moat.md), [capital-cities](../entities/capital-cities.md).

**★ M31 · Berkshire or low-cost index fund?** *Buffett:* without recommending buying or selling Berkshire, endorses a "very low-cost index fund" accumulated over 10 years with regular amounts as a path that will "probably do better than 90 percent of the people around you that take up investing at a similar time." *Munger:* "The stockbroking fraternity, in toto, can be guaranteed to do so poorly that the index fund is a better option." See [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md).

**★ M32 · Leverage is the one thing that prevents you from playing out a winning hand.** *Buffett:* smart people with correct analysis still go broke when leveraged — "if you owed a lot of money against them in between, you wouldn't have been around for the party at the end." The anti-leverage rule is architectural: Berkshire holds enormous cash, avoids debt, remains liquid precisely so it can hold through whatever "absolute chaos" a financial market produces and still be standing to buy. "The only way smart people can get clobbered, really, is through leverage." See [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md), [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md).

## Q&A digest — Afternoon session

**A1 · Corporate ethics: very hard to detect, even for the sophisticated.** *Buffett:* suspicious when companies display their stock price prominently in the lobby — "things like that make us nervous." But the honest admission is that Royal Dutch Shell would have been near the top of Munger's list for sound long-term culture — "and to have the oil reserves phonied for years, with internal reports of people who were tired of lying." *Munger:* "If it can happen at Royal Dutch, believe me, it can happen a lot of other places." Situational ethics: "people sink faster to a lower prevailing morality than they rise to a higher prevailing morality." See [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

**★ A2 · Congress legislating accounting: dishonor, not stupidity.** *Buffett:* the 1993 Senate vote (88–9) to override FASB's stock-option-expensing requirement was "a disgrace" and "accelerated the 'anything goes' mentality of the 1990s." Uses the Indiana legislature's 1897 attempt to set pi = 3.2 (or 3.20) by statute as the analogy: Indiana was stupid, but at least the legislators didn't know they were wrong. *Munger:* "The current members of Congress that want to retain the former abusive accounting are way worse than the people who wanted to round pi to an even number. Those people were stupid. These people are mostly not stupid, but dishonorable." Now all four Big Four firms endorse expensing, where in 1993 all five (then Big Five) endorsed the opposite. See [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md), [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md).

**A3 · No fund management company: too many conflicts.** *Buffett:* could easily sell it, but "managing so much of our own money at Berkshire, to take on the responsibility for managing another group of people to whom we would owe our best efforts" creates an unsolvable conflict — prorating purchases between Berkshire and a managed fund would be ethically untenable. *Munger:* "that's why we don't do it. But I must say it's an attitude that doesn't seem to bother many other people." See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**★ A5 · "I'm the only one that can double-cross you" — Berkshire as permanent owner.** *Buffett:* private equity is real competition for whole-business acquisitions. But Berkshire offers something PE cannot: a seller who built a family business "lovingly over decades and decades" can run it exactly as before, indefinitely, without consultants coming in to restructure, without leveraging and reselling. "It's kind of silly to auction off your daughter to whatever man is willing to pay the most for her." Promises to sellers are not contractual — "I'm the only one that can double-cross you" — but no management committee or Wall Street firm can override Buffett's word. See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**A6 · MidAmerican environmental record.** David Sokol (present at the meeting) responds to a New York Times article that criticized him as a "Ranger" in the 2002 Bush campaign, not MidAmerican's emissions. MidAmerican was ranked #2 nationally and #1 in the Midwest (out of 55 utilities) for environmental reliability, availability, and customer satisfaction. Secondary; logged for accuracy. See [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md).

**★ A8 · Why no stock split: shareholder self-selection.** *Buffett:* no religious opposition, but splitting would attract shareholders who prefer a $9 stock over a $90,000 one — and "as a group" those shareholders are less long-term, less informed, less in sync with Berkshire's policies. The high price is "a symptom of people with a somewhat different attitude toward the stocks they own." Berkshire's Class A share turnover is the lowest of any large American corporation. "You can't reconstruct it if you destroy it." See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md), [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md).

**★ A9 · Munger: liquidity is "mostly twaddle" as a contributor to capitalism.** *Munger:* "The notion... that liquidity of tradable common stock is a great contributor to capitalism — I think that is mostly twaddle. The GNP of the United States grew at very good rates long before we had highly-liquid markets for common stock." After the South Sea Bubble, England banned tradable common stocks for decades and "did fine." Liquidity creates crazy booms as well as virtues. Berkshire itself trades ~$50M per day — "We're trying to create more people who have this big liquidity problem, because they own so much stock." See [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md), [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md).

**A10 · Moral distinction: owning stock vs. directing a company.** *Buffett:* would own stock in companies whose activities he wouldn't personally endorse as an owner/operator. "I do not have a problem buying stock in companies... that engage in activities that I wouldn't probably endorse myself." But Berkshire passed on an outright acquisition because "we went down in the lobby of the hotel and just decided that in the end we didn't want to be involved in that" — while adding he'd buy the stock. *Munger:* Berkshire has "a huge area of things which is perfectly legal to do, that we think beneath us. So we won't do them."

**★ A12 · National Indemnity: never a layoff because volume doesn't mandate writing.** *Buffett:* (with slides) National Indemnity premium volume: $16M at acquisition (1967) → $79M (1980) → $366M (1986 hard market) → $55M (1999 soft market) → nearly $600M (2003+). Through the entire decline from $366M to $55M, not one employee was laid off — all attrition. The reason: "the absolutely unequivocal message to the people that are associated with us, that they will never be laid off because of lack of volume, and therefore, we don't want them to write one bit of bad business." Expense ratios rose to 41% as volume fell — "We would rather suffer of having too much overhead than we would want to teach our employees that to retain their jobs, they needed to write any damn thing that came along, because that's a very hard habit to get rid of once you get hooked on it." *Munger:* "Practically nobody else does it. And yet to me it's obvious it's the way to go." See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md), [national-indemnity](../entities/national-indemnity.md).

**★ A13 · PetroChina: cheap enough to absorb country risk.** *Buffett:* bought PetroChina not because it's in China but "because it was very, very cheap in relation to earnings, in relation to reserves, in relation to daily oil production, and refining capacity." Produces 80–85% as much crude daily as Exxon; paid out 45% of earnings as dividends (explicitly committed); no unusual leverage; annual report probably more transparent than major Western oil companies. Valuation was "well under half — or maybe a third — of what comparable oil companies are selling for." *Munger:* "If a thing is cheap enough, obviously you can afford a little more country risk, or regulatory risk, or whatever. This is not complicated." *Supersession flag: Berkshire sold PetroChina in 2007 at roughly 5x cost, generating over $3B profit.* See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md).

**★ A15 · Dividends vs. buybacks: the right framework.** *Buffett:* the equation is simple but practice often doesn't follow logic. Buybacks when the stock trades below intrinsic value are "probably the best use of cash" — gives remaining shareholders more per share and gives sellers a slightly better exit price. Dividends create an "expectational situation" — once set, hard to cut without a signal. *Wrong* use of buybacks: companies buying back at full price to prop up stock, hoping it delays declines; "often done at prices that don't really make a lot of sense for continuing shareholders." *Munger:* the total frictional costs of investment advice and trading in the U.S. are roughly equal to the total dividends paid — "so the net dividends that come to the shareholders are approximately zero." See [dividend-policy](../concepts/dividend-policy.md), [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md), [sees-candies](../entities/sees-candies.md).

**★ A16 · GEICO and Dell: the low-cost producer wins.** *Buffett:* traces auto insurance distribution from casualty affiliates of fire companies → State Farm's captive agency (1920s) → USAA/GEICO's direct model (1936+) → Progressive's recent pivot to direct. Each stage was a better mousetrap; the low-cost mousetrap won. "Every American family wants a car; they don't want insurance, but they can't drive without it." Cost therefore dominates the purchase decision. "It's always a good idea to go with a low-cost producer over time... being a low-cost producer of something that's essential to people, it's going to be a very good business usually." *Munger:* "you've chosen a wonderful field. And if you fail in it, it's your own fault." See [geico](../entities/geico.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md), [direct-distribution-moat](../concepts/direct-distribution-moat.md).

**A17 · Google's owner's manual: the right communication model.** *Buffett:* pleased that Google's co-founders were "inspired" by Berkshire's Owner's Manual. "If you buy into Google, having read their owner's manual, you'll know the kind of people you're associating with." An owner's manual written the way partners would speak to each other is the right communication model. *Munger:* "Those two guys who created Google are two of the smartest young men in the whole country. It's much more fun to be copied by people that smart." See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**A19 · Phil Fisher: concentration, temperament, and reading.** *Buffett:* Fisher died a month prior, well into his 90s; his first book (*Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits*, 1958) and its sequel were "terrific." Met Fisher once (Russ Building, San Francisco) but "you didn't need to meet them personally. They were such good writers, and their thoughts were so clear." Charlie Munger had been "preaching the Fisher doctrine" to Buffett from 1959 onward. *Munger:* "the basic idea that it was hard to find good stocks... and therefore you just find a few of them that you knew a lot about, and concentrate on those — it seemed to me such an obviously good idea. And yet, 98 percent of the investing world doesn't follow it." See [concentration-vs-diversification](../concepts/concentration-vs-diversification.md), [cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses](../concepts/cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses.md).

**A20 · Compensation: no single formula, understand the business.** *Buffett:* ROE as a comp yardstick makes no sense if the manager inherited a terrible goodwill overhang from an acquisition price they had nothing to do with. A network TV station can earn huge returns on equity with an "idiot nephew" running it. The comp system must reflect the *true economics* of the specific business. Book recommendation from Munger: *Pride in Excellence* by Les Schwab — "he made a huge fortune in one of the world's really difficult businesses [tire shops] by having shrewd systems." See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).

**★ A21 · Auction markets offer more bargains than IPOs.** *Buffett:* "It is more of a negotiated sale [in an IPO]. And negotiated transactions are very hard to get bargains." IPO sellers choose *when* to come to market — not in your favor. An auction market can price similar things at wild discounts because *everyone* is a seller at once; junk bonds at 35–40% yields in 2002 couldn't happen in a negotiated transaction. "You're way more likely to get incredible bargains in an auction market. It's just the nature of things." *Munger:* "The average person buying IPOs is going to get creamed. So if you're talented enough, sure, that will work." See [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md), [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md).

**A23 · American business has never let investors down.** *Buffett:* the Dow went from 66 to 10,000+ through the 20th century — two world wars, nuclear bombs, flu epidemics, the Cold War. "At any given point in history, including when stocks were their cheapest, you could find an equally impressive number of negative factors." The underlying premise: "this country will do very well, and in particularly it will do well for business. In this country the opportunities have won out over the problems over time." "It won't be the American economy that does in investors over a five, or 10, or 20-year period. It will be the investors themselves." See [american-tailwind](../concepts/american-tailwind.md), [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md).

**★ A24 · Derivatives vs. insurance: "a demented system."** *Buffett:* Gen Re's derivative contracts "bore very little relation to the insurance businesses we see." Insurance transfers genuine risks people cannot or will not bear themselves. Much of derivatives activity was speculative — "created transactions without much economic necessity." The trader at bank A and trader at bank B both "record a profit" on the same zero-sum trade; "I've never seen one where they both put on a loss that day." *Munger:* "They're radically different. The derivatives business is chock full of clauses saying that if one party's credit gets downgraded... they have to start posting collateral. That's just like a margin account... In attempting to protect themselves, they've introduced this enormous instability into the system... It's a demented system. And you don't get properly paid in most cases for playing the game." Collateral triggers meant to protect each party are exactly what turns a local credit event into a systemic cascade. See [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**★ A25 · Salomon and Robert Maxwell — incentives override judgment.** *Munger:* "Salomon was begging for new investment banking business from Maxwell. And his nickname was 'The Bouncing Czech.'" Shortly after Salomon solicited him, Maxwell committed suicide after massive embezzlements of pension funds. Three Berkshire-affiliated directors (Buffett, Munger, Lou Simpson) — the largest shareholder block — all argued against the transaction. The underwriting committee had approved it. "I mean, here we are — with all three of us on the board, the biggest shareholder, and we can't even stop one stupid little underwriting." The deeper principle: "it is very hard to control people when their income depends on bringing in dubious people into the door." See [salomon](../entities/salomon.md), [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md), [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

**★ A26 · Hedge funds: a fad with extraordinary fees.** *Buffett:* "The hedge fund right now is in the midst of a fad. It's distinguished not by the ability to make more money. It's distinguished by the extraordinary amount of fees that are collected." With $600B in hedge fund assets paying 2% management fees plus profit participations, "it is not going to be a great experience, in aggregate, for investors." Fund-of-funds adds another layer. *Munger:* "Why would you want to invest with a guy whose basic thought process runs something like this: 'If a second layer of fees on top of a first layer of substantial fees is good for an investor, then a third layer of fees must be better yet?'" See [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md), [investment-friction](../concepts/investment-friction.md).

**★ A27 · "Uncommon sense" and the exclusion principle.** *Munger:* an old Omaha friend's insight: "There is no common sense. When people say common sense, they mean uncommon sense." The key discipline is not superior analytical speed but "being able to tune out folly as distinguished from recognizing wisdom... if you just got whole categories of things you just bat away, so your brain isn't cluttered with them." *Buffett:* uses the Fischer-Spassky chess match as analogy — neither player outthinks a computer by brute speed, but both eliminate "99.99 percent of the possibilities without even thinking about it," leaving only the few moves worth evaluating. "Don't spend 10 minutes on something you know in the first sentence isn't a great, wonderful idea." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [inversion](../concepts/inversion.md).

**★ A28 · Intrinsic value exercise — and the cash pile problem.** *Buffett:* endorses a shareholder's DCF approach (2003 operating earnings + look-through earnings discounted at 7% over 20 years plus terminal value): "The line of thinking is correct, it just depends on what variables you plug in." Would expect CAPEX to exceed depreciation on average; would apply lower growth assumptions given Berkshire's size ("very large, and it gets harder all the time to deploy the kind of funds that keep flowing into Omaha"). *Munger:* "The interesting thing about Berkshire's present valuation is how much cash, and cash equivalents it has to do something. That is a very interesting question. How well are we going to do with this massive amount of investable cash?" "Cash may pile up faster than we can use it, in which case we'll have to rethink the whole game." See [intrinsic-value-vs-book-value](../concepts/intrinsic-value-vs-book-value.md), [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md).

**A30 · Advice to young people: habits, debt, and better companions.** *Buffett:* would emphasize habits above almost anything else — they are very difficult to change at 45 and very easy to form young. Single most important financial advice: don't get into debt. "The game plays a lot easier if you're a little bit ahead of the game than if you're behind." Many financial disasters he hears about are debt-related, not income-related. *Munger:* "All the trite stuff is what works... avoid doing the really dumb things... avoid evil or seriously irrational people, particularly if they are attractive members of the opposite sex." *Buffett:* "It's better to hang out with people better than you... you'll drift in that direction." See [warren-buffett](../people/warren-buffett.md).

**A31 · Silver: no current position comment; commodity markets not rigged.** *Buffett:* declines to confirm or deny position in silver (previously bought 129.5M oz; disclosed at Bank of England's request). Rejects the conspiracy theory that the COMEX is rigged: "I find most of the people that write on gold and silver tend to have various conspiratorial theories... there's nothing flawed, in my view, about the market for silver, or copper, or gold, or really any commodity that trades in real quantity." *Munger:* "you're asking for the opinions of people who have not particularly distinguished themselves in this arena."

**A32 · Wells Fargo not a "big player" in derivatives.** *Buffett:* JPMorgan Chase's derivative book vastly exceeds Wells Fargo's; does not regard Wells as a meaningful derivatives player. Admires Dick Kovacevich's management ("an absolutely terrific record") while disagreeing with him on stock-option expensing. Voted Berkshire shares for option expensing at Wells' annual meeting, where 57% of shareholders agreed. See [wells-fargo](../entities/wells-fargo.md), [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md).

**★ A33 · Leverage: the biggest danger — Berkshire as "last man standing."** *Buffett:* in a financial cataclysm, "really the only way a smart person that's reasonably disciplined in how they look at investments can get in trouble is through leverage — if somebody else can pull the plug on you during the worst moment... you go broke." Absent leverage, "the world won't hurt you over time in securities." Berkshire is "in an extraordinarily strong position in respect to any kind of financial cataclysm. I think we would be definitely the last man standing, and then some." The junk-bond episode in 2002 was "absolute chaos" at the bottom — "that isn't as much chaos as you could have." Having cash, having courage, and having no forced sellers is the structural position Berkshire is designed to occupy. See [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md), [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md), [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).
