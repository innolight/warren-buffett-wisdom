# Berkshire Hathaway 2013 Annual Meeting

Held on May 4, 2013, at the CenturyLink Center in Omaha with an estimated 35,000–45,000 attendees — the first year the audience surpassed 40,000 — the 2013 meeting is notable on three fronts: it was the first annual meeting to include a self-described short seller on the analyst panel (Doug Kass of Seabreeze Partners), it introduced the H.J. Heinz acquisition-with-3G Capital as a new structural template for Berkshire, and it produced the most extended public discussion of Berkshire's commercial insurance build-out and the post-Buffett institutional franchise. The durable teaching clusters around five ideas: that capital advantage and operational expertise are separable, and a structure that explicitly rewards both can beat forcing one party to do everything; that Berkshire's crisis-lending franchise is institutional rather than personal and will compound in value after any single manager departs; that cumulative knowledge compresses due diligence legitimately without diminishing intensity; that rational insurance underwriting requires structural independence from quarterly earnings pressure; and that interest rates act as a gravitational constant on all asset prices with systematic distributional consequences when held near zero for extended periods. This page keeps the wiki's two-layer convention: a distilled thematic top, then a comprehensive ★-marked Q&A digest as the home for detail.

## How to read the Q&A digest

- **★** marks a timeless, load-bearing exchange worth citing in the future; unmarked entries still carry investor value but are secondary or period-specific.
- Answers are attributed separately to **Buffett** and **Munger** — a Munger line appears only when he added a distinct lesson (he said "Nothing to add" to most).

## Key themes

### The 3G partnership: capital advantage meets operational expertise

The Heinz deal introduced a structural model new to Berkshire's public history: pairing patient capital (Berkshire's preferred plus common equity, less leveraged) with an operating partner (3G Capital, more leveraged) who brings intensive operational expertise. Buffett's precise formulation: "we have more money than operating ability at the parent company level, and they have lots of operating ability and wanted to maximize their return on 4 billion." The design is explicit — if Heinz does well, 3G's levered 4.1 billion earns higher percentage returns than Berkshire's 12 billion; but Berkshire captures a fair return on both the equity-like slice and the preferred-like leverage it provided. Buffett stretched the price slightly because he thought Jorge Paulo Lemann and his associates are "extraordinarily good managers." The lesson generalizes: capital advantage and operational advantage are different skills, and a structure that explicitly separates, rewards, and combines them can outperform forcing a single acquirer to do both. See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md). *(M12)*

### Berkshire as the 800-number — the institutional franchise outlasts any individual

The "lender of last resort" exchange is the clearest statement yet that Berkshire's crisis-capital franchise is structural rather than personal. After Buffett, the successor will still have "unusual capital at times when... the ability to say 'yes' very quickly with very large sums sets you apart from virtually anybody in the investing universe." Munger sharpens the argument: in the early days, Buffett competed in fields of intense competition (small-cap value); he then "gotten into a field, being a good home for big companies that don't want to be controlled in meticulous detail by headquarters, where there isn't much competition." Crucially, this franchise compounds — "as we get even bigger, the area we occupy becomes more and more our own." The institutional brand, not the individual's reputation, is the durable asset. See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md). *(M25, M27)*

### Cumulative knowledge — intensity never dims, just concentrates

Responding to the suggestion that Buffett's research style had shifted from "sleuth-like" to less intense, his rebuttal is fundamental: "I have every bit of the intensity — not manifested exactly the same way — but it's there every minute." What looks like a quick idea (Bank of America in the bathtub) is actually the surface expression of 50+ years of following banking. The first American Express investment required exhaustive channel checks through restaurants and banks; the second required a single golf game with Frank Olson, who revealed he couldn't get rid of American Express's fees no matter how he tried. Munger's summary principle: "The game of life is a game of everlasting learning. At least it is if you want to win." The corollary: deep expertise compresses due diligence legitimately — but only when the foundation was built properly over decades. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md). *(A2, A12)*

### Rational underwriting requires structural independence from quarterly pressure

The 2013 meeting produces the clearest explanation of why Berkshire's insurance culture resists what virtually every other publicly-listed insurer cannot: "we have controlling shareholders, so we did not have outside influences that pushed us in directions we didn't want to go." National Indemnity contracted its traditional volume by roughly 80% when pricing became inadequate. Buffett is direct: "I'm not sure any manager of a public company answering to quarterly earnings calls could've really stood up to the kind of pressure they would receive if they followed a similar policy." When hedge funds entered reinsurance as a tax-efficient beard, Berkshire simply watched — "we know what we think the prices should be, and we will do insurance business where we think the odds favor us earning an underwriting profit. And if we can't do it, we'll watch for a while." The structural independence that enables Berkshire's acquistions enables its underwriting. See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md). *(M40, M41)*

### Build at book value rather than buy at a premium — when talent is recruitable

When asked why Berkshire didn't simply acquire a commercial insurer, Buffett encodes a general principle: "if you can find the right people with the right mindset... it's really better to build than buy." Building from the AIG executives who sought out Ajit Jain costs book value and avoids inheriting culture, pricing errors, and legacy practices. Acquiring a comparable operation would cost multiples of book and package all of these alongside. The lesson: when specific talent is recruitable and the culture can be set from scratch, building beats buying at a premium — particularly in industries where discipline and underwriting culture are the primary competitive advantages. See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md). *(M22)*

### Conviction is a spectrum — IBM vs. BNSF

The IBM discussion yields the meeting's clearest statement of how conviction calibrates position-sizing and confidence language: "I do not understand the moat around an IBM as well as I understand the moat around a Coca-Cola... I could think of some things that could go wrong with IBM. I can't think of anything that could go wrong with BNSF." This is not a binary in-or-out filter but a calibrated spectrum — a lower-conviction position still warrants a "considerable amount of money" at the right price; maximum conviction (BNSF, GEICO) warrants near-certainty language. Munger's corollary: "We think that the Burlington Northern will have a competitive advantage 15 years from now with a high degree of confidence. We would never have that degree of confidence about Apple, no matter what their financial statements showed." The lesson: position size and language precision should both track conviction honestly, not just reflect a yes/no pass. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md). *(A22)*

### Interest rates as gravity — a structural lens for any rate environment

The Federal Reserve discussion yields the meeting's most durable metaphor: "interest rates are to asset prices, you know, sort of like gravity is to the apple. And when there are very low interest rates, there's a very small gravitational pull on asset prices." The implication is systematic: every asset class simultaneously reports stronger marks at low rates, creating an illusion of prosperity that reverses symmetrically when the rate environment shifts. The distributional consequence is equally structural: "they had to hurt somebody, and the savers were convenient." Low rates transfer wealth from fixed-income savers to borrowers and asset holders — a deliberate redistribution with real victims who "don't have much of a voice." The framework transfers to any rate era: identify what rate level is embedded in current valuations and trace the winners and losers of any shift. *Supersession flag:* the Fed funds rate rose from effectively zero to 5.25–5.50% in 2022–2023, the most aggressive tightening cycle since Volcker; asset prices fell sharply across 2022 as gravity reasserted. See [bonds-and-inflation](../concepts/bonds-and-inflation.md), [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md). *(M21, A21)*

## Q&A digest — Morning session

**M2 · Q1 earnings: GEICO closure/persistency records, BNSF outperforming.** *Buffett:* GEICO's closure rate (people who get a quote and buy a policy) at "incredible levels," persistency (renewal rate) also up — a million new policies this year is plausible, worth $1.5 billion in intrinsic value not visible on any financial statement. BNSF car loadings up 3.8% in first 17 weeks vs. 0.4% for other four major Class I railroads — partially driven by Bakken oil proximity. Berkshire now the fifth most valuable company in the world. See [geico](../entities/geico.md), [bnsf](../entities/bnsf.md).

**★ M8 · Book value as S&P proxy — and why book understates intrinsic.** *Buffett:* the 2013 first five-year period may mark the first time book value per share growth falls short of the S&P (including dividends); Berkshire is structurally better in down years. More importantly: book value is a tracking device, not intrinsic value — GEICO's million-policy gain adds $1.5 billion to intrinsic value and zero to book; the ISCAR buyout reduced book value by ~$1 billion the day it closed. "In the end, we have to do better for you than you would do in an index fund. And if we don't, we aren't earning our pay." *Munger:* Berkshire's momentum and compounding base remain intact even as growth rates slow from "almost unbelievable" early levels. See [berkshire-hathaway](../entities/berkshire-hathaway.md), [intrinsic-value-vs-book-value](../concepts/intrinsic-value-vs-book-value.md).

**M10 · ISCAR vs. Sandvik — passion and relentless improvement.** *Buffett:* Sandvik is a fabulous company; ISCAR is better. The competitive explanation is not location, raw materials, or proximity to customers — "the only answer you can give when you see that result" is extraordinary talent that never stopped working, never stopped improving the product, never stopped making customers happy. *Munger:* "It's the only place I was ever in where I saw nothing but robots and engineers working computers." See [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**M11 · Preserving culture after Buffett — it will be "rejected like foreign tissue."** *Buffett:* culture is the durable asset; the trains keep running and people keep calling GEICO the day after he dies. The most striking formulation: "I think that anything that came in — any foreign-type behavior — would be cast out because people have self-selected into this group... it would be rejected like a foreign tissue if we got the wrong sort of person in there." The board is devoted to Berkshire not because it's paid hugely but because it believes in the mission; the manager-partners have brought their companies to Berkshire because they want to be part of it. *Munger:* "Don't be so stupid as to sell these shares." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**★ M12 · Heinz with 3G Capital — capital meets operational expertise.** *Buffett:* the deal structure reflects what each party does best: "we have more money than operating ability at the parent company level, and they have lots of operating ability and wanted to maximize their return on 4 billion." Berkshire has a less-leveraged position, providing debt-like stability plus equity upside; 3G's more-leveraged common equity earns higher percentage returns if the business does well. Buffett stretched the price because Lemann and his associates are "extraordinarily good managers — both classy and unusually good." The arrangement is designed so that over five to ten years, 3G will probably earn a higher rate of return on its investment, but Berkshire's larger dollar total still produces very attractive returns. *Munger:* "The report [suggesting otherwise] was totally wrong." See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), [stock-as-acquisition-currency](../concepts/stock-as-acquisition-currency.md).

**M13 · Commercial insurance expansion — Aon deal and AIG executives.** *Buffett:* two moves: a 7.5% participation in the entire London market (the Aon arrangement), giving Berkshire a cross-section of commercial reinsurance; and hiring four senior AIG insurance executives to build a primary commercial operation from scratch. "I think you'll see us become a very significant factor, worldwide, in the commercial insurance business... it could be business that reaches into the billions." Capital advantage plus willingness to commit with certainty are the structural edge. *Munger:* "Our reinsurance business under Ajit is very peculiar, and other people who think it's easy are going to find out that it isn't." See [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**M14 · GEICO vs. Progressive Snapshot — competing selection methods.** *Buffett:* GEICO is gaining a hugely disproportionate share of new policyholders while maintaining strong underwriting results; Snapshot is Progressive's bet, but "our systems have been developed over many decades" with a huge credible policyholder base. "I would say that our ability to sell insurance at a price considerably lower than most of our competitors, evidenced by the fact that when people call us they shift to us, and at the same time earn a significant underwriting profit, indicates that our selection process is working quite well." Politely invites a comparative results check in two to three years. *Munger:* "Obviously, we're not going to immediately copy the oddball thing that every single competitor does, particularly when we've got an operation that's working so well." See [geico](../entities/geico.md).

**M15 · Business Wire and the primacy of simultaneous disclosure.** *Buffett:* the SEC's allowance of social-media disclosure for material announcements is a mistake — "the key to disclosure is accuracy and simultaneity." Business Wire's track record of getting information to every part of the globe simultaneously is irreplaceable. Berkshire will not change its own disclosure practices; important releases come via Business Wire after the market closes so shareholders have time to digest rather than react to a one-page summary. "I do not want to have to keep hitting up to their webpage... hoping that I'm not 10 seconds behind someone else."

**★ M16 · Buying pricier businesses — the size penalty acknowledged.** *Buffett:* Doug Kass is right that size is a headwind and that Berkshire has paid up more for recent acquisitions; "we've paid up for good businesses more than we would have 30 or 40 years ago — partly at Charlie's urging." But that reflects learning: "paying up for an extraordinary business is not a mistake." The genuinely challenging claim is whether Berkshire will do better than the "giants of the past" who found scale more constraining than expected. *Munger:* "If you look at the oil companies that got really big... the record is not all that good. Rockefeller's Standard Oil is practically the only one that, after it got monstrous, continued to do monstrously well." See [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md), [cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses](../concepts/cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses.md).

**★ M19 · Decentralization to the point of abdication — and why it scales.** *Buffett:* his successor will modestly rearrange reporting lines but will never abandon the principle that CEO-managers run their businesses in virtually all important ways except capital allocation. The real money is in the large businesses; small changes in reporting structure are immaterial. *Munger:* "If your system is decentralization, almost to the point of abdication, what difference does it make how many subsidiaries you have?" Twenty years ago it would have seemed ridiculous that a company this size could function with ten people in a little Omaha office — but it works. See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md).

**M20 · Fed stimulus and uncharted monetary territory.** *Buffett:* the Fed's $3.4 trillion balance sheet and the bank reserves sitting at the Fed are unprecedented; "it's like watching a good movie, because I do not know the end." The unwind will be harder than the buying — "it's a lot easier to buy things than it is to sell them." Not necessarily inflationary yet, but the potential is clear. When the market gets any signal that buying might end, "it's likely to be the shot heard around the world." *Munger:* "The economists ought to be a little more cautious in believing they know exactly how to stay out of trouble when they print money in massive amounts." Japan's two decades of stasis after using "all the tricks in the economist's bag" is the cautionary case.

**★ M21 · Interest rates as gravity — and savers as victims.** *Buffett:* "interest rates are to asset prices, you know, sort of like gravity is to the apple. And when there are very low interest rates, there's a very small gravitational pull on asset prices." Berkshire held ~$48 billion in short-term securities at the end of Q1 earning "basically nothing" — if short rates returned to 5%, that would represent ~$2 billion of additional annual pretax earnings. But near-zero rates also harm millions of savers who "live off their income" and face staggering purchasing-power losses. *Munger:* "They had to hurt somebody, and the savers were convenient." *Supersession flag:* the Fed raised rates from 0–0.25% to 5.25–5.50% over 2022–2023; the gravity metaphor proved accurate as asset prices fell sharply in 2022. See [bonds-and-inflation](../concepts/bonds-and-inflation.md), [float](../concepts/float.md).

**M22 · Build vs. buy — commercial insurance at book value.** *Buffett:* "if you can find the right people with the right mindset... it's really better to build than buy." Berkshire is building its commercial insurance operation at book value and will not inherit bad habits, bad culture, or bad reserves from an acquired entity. Any comparable established operation would cost far more than book, and the premium would fund the very cultural problems that make discipline hard. "We will build a very large commercial operation, and essentially we've built it at book value." *Munger:* "Nothing to add." See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**M23 · Bitcoin — Munger's verdict.** *Munger:* "I have no confidence whatsoever in bitcoin being any kind of a big universal currency." *Buffett:* "Of our 49 billion, we haven't moved any to bitcoin." Acknowledged as outside either's circle; wouldn't discuss it further. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**M24 · Multilevel marketing — the end-user test.** *Buffett:* the key distinction in multilevel marketing is whether the money is made by selling to end users or by "loading up" would-be distributors with product they never sell. Pampered Chef is "a million miles away" from the latter — thousands of weekly parties where actual users buy from someone in-person. If he were regulating the industry, he would look hard at operations where the primary income to participants came from recruiting, not from selling to genuine end users. *Munger:* "There's likely to be more flimflam in selling magic potions than pots and pans."

**★ M25 · Berkshire as the 800-number — capital plus willingness in a panic.** *(Doug Kass)* *Buffett:* "Berkshire is the 800 number when there's really, sort of, panic in markets, and for one reason or another, people need significant capital." His successor will have unusual capital at precisely those moments when most institutions are frozen. The brand strengthens over time — when it happens after Buffett is gone, "it becomes even more the Berkshire brand and not anything attached to a single individual." *Munger:* Buffett shifted from small-cap value (intense competition) to "being a good home for big companies that don't want to be controlled in meticulous detail by headquarters, where there isn't much competition." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md).

**★ M27 · Berkshire's acquisition edge — staying sane and the reluctant-seller story.** *Munger:* Berkshire's competitive advantage explained to a 13-year-old: "We've always tried to stay sane, and other people, a lot of them, like to go crazy. That's a competitive advantage." Second: treating subsidiaries the way you'd want to be treated if you were in them — "a very rare attitude in corporate America." Third: being a good partner to people who need a partner with more money. *Buffett:* illustrates with the reluctant-seller: a 60-something owner who didn't want to retire but worried about what would happen to the business and the employees after he died. He ruled out competitors ("Attila the Hun synergies"), ruled out private equity ("load it up with debt, then resell it"), and concluded: "You're the only guy left standing. You're not a competitor, you're not a private equity firm, and I know I will get a permanent home." "Our competitive advantage is we had no competitors." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**M28 · BNSF: coal flat, crude oil rail growing.** *Buffett + Matt Rose:* coal franchise expected to stay roughly flat depending on natural gas prices and EPA; crude-by-rail is a different story. BNSF currently operating ~10 Bakken loading stations with 30 destination stations; negotiating another 30 destinations; handling ~650,000 barrels per day, targeting 750,000 by year-end with a pathway to 1.2–1.4 million. "Oil moves a whole lot faster by rail than it does by pipeline." Bakken plus shale developments open a long-duration opportunity for rail. See [bnsf](../entities/bnsf.md).

**M29 · Crisis lending — Harley-Davidson and the world's proclivity for excess.** *Buffett:* the Harley-Davidson 15%-coupon note matures in 2014 — "what we'd like to do is not answer the mail and let him keep paying 15 percent, but that won't happen." These special-terms transactions were made when the corporate bond market was "basically frozen." "The world is given to excesses, and they have consequences, and we are always willing to act." The key insight: Berkshire didn't think Harley-Davidson was going broke — it just saw an asymmetric opportunity in a frozen market and committed quickly. Similar situations will recur. See [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md), [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

**★ M30 · Todd Combs and Ted Weschler — full autonomy in practice.** *Buffett:* Combs and Weschler don't check with him before buying; he learns what they've purchased on monthly portfolio sheets, sometimes a month after they start. He gave each another $1 billion on March 31 and doesn't know how it was spent. They have "one or two restrictions" (Bank Holding Company law for certain bank stocks) but are otherwise free agents — can put it all in one stock or spread across 50. "When I managed money, I wanted to be a free agent... once they gave me the money, I wanted free reign to do what I wanted." They buy things he wouldn't buy; he buys things they wouldn't. The arrangement requires unusual trust and the willingness to delegate genuinely. See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).

**M33 · Newspaper economics — 10%+ after-tax with declining earnings.** *Buffett:* the newspaper acquisitions (approaching $100 million pretax) are bought at "very, very low prices compared to current earnings" because earnings will decline. The after-tax return is structurally enhanced because most papers were bought from S-corps or partnerships, allowing intangible amortization that doesn't apply to C-corp acquisitions like BNSF. His projection: at least 10% after-tax return, perhaps somewhat higher. "Everything we have seen to date would indicate that we will meet or beat the 10 percent." Small in absolute dollars (the needle doesn't move), but decent returns on capital. Promises to report annually. *Munger:* "What you're saying is that it's an exception, and you like doing it." *Supersession flag:* Berkshire sold its entire newspaper portfolio to Lee Enterprises in January 2020 for a nominal $140 million plus assumption of debt, confirming secular decline. See [media-economics](../concepts/media-economics.md).

**★ M34 · Teledyne and Henry Singleton — admiring but not emulating.** *Buffett:* breaking Berkshire up into separately-traded companies "would produce a poorer result, certainly now, and I believe in the future." *Munger:* Singleton was a genius (blindfolded chess near grandmaster level, perfect math/physics scores) but ran a much more centralized operation, played the stock-issuance game aggressively in the 1960s, and at the end wanted to sell his businesses to Berkshire for Berkshire stock. "We basically said to him, 'Henry, we love you and we'd love to buy your businesses, but we don't want to issue Berkshire stock.'" He did the right thing on buybacks — "managed it on the way down like a great chess player should" — but "to some extent he looked at the shareholder group as somebody to be taken advantage of" in the issuance phase. "I like our system better. We're more avuncular than Teledyne was." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md).

**M38 · Luck, timing, and the baby born today in America.** *Buffett:* Being born in the United States was "a huge, huge, huge advantage." Being born male in 1930 was also a structural advantage — he would not have had the same investment opportunities as a woman. Timing matters at the margin (born five years earlier = more money; born ten to fifteen years later = less) but doesn't foreclose opportunity. "I envy the baby that's being born today in the United States... on a probability basis, that's the luckiest individual that's ever been born." The person with a passion for investing, coming of age twenty years from now, will "do very well." *Munger:* "The competition was very weak in your early days... I don't think the competition is as weak now." But opportunities still appeared in 2008–09 for those with capital and willingness to act. See [american-tailwind](../concepts/american-tailwind.md).

**★ M40 · Rational insurance pricing — structural independence is the difference.** *Buffett:* "we have controlling shareholders, so we did not have outside influences that pushed us in directions we didn't want to go." National Indemnity contracted its traditional book by roughly 80% when business became unattractive. "I'm not sure any manager of a public company answering to quarterly earnings calls... could've really stood up to the kind of pressure they would receive if they followed a similar policy." Insurance is full of opportunities to do stupid things. "You can't afford to go along with the crowd in investment, insurance, or a whole lot of other things." The specific mechanism: "There's a reason why all that stuff is in the Bible. You can't covet your neighbor's ass... it's the one sin there's no fun in." *Munger:* "With our cranky, wait-it-out methods, we probably have ended up with the best large-scale casualty insurance operation in the world." See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md), [institutional-imperative](../concepts/institutional-imperative.md).

**★ M41 · Hedge funds in reinsurance — "dumb competition" and how to respond.** *Buffett:* hedge funds entered reinsurance aggressively partly to obtain a tax-efficient "beard" for Bermuda-based operations and partly because it can be sold to investors as "uncorrelated." "Anything Wall Street can sell, it will sell." The influx may bring down prices and do stupid things; this has happened before. Response: "we know what we think the prices should be, and we will do insurance business where we think the odds favor us earning an underwriting profit. And if we can't do it, we'll watch for a while." The advantage of insurance over, say, a service station: standby costs aren't huge, so you can let volume fall dramatically without back-breaking cost. National Indemnity did exactly that in the 1980s and waited for better days. *Munger:* "With our cranky, wait-it-out methods... why would we change?" See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md), [float](../concepts/float.md).

**M43 · Banking after Dodd-Frank — stronger U.S., Munger on derivatives in banks.** *Buffett:* the U.S. banking system is stronger than any time in the last 25 years — capital dramatically higher, troublesome loans largely gone, new loan vintages far better. Higher capital ratios reduce return on equity but "it could be something else" causing the next bubble (not housing again). Wells Fargo, U.S. Bank, M&T: "very strong banks pursuing sound practices." *Munger:* "I would like to see something more extreme in terms of limiting bank activities. I do not see why massive derivative books should be mixed up with insured deposits." *Buffett:* "I'm with Charlie on that." See [wells-fargo](../entities/wells-fargo.md), [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md), [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md).

**M44 · Hedge fund bet update — zero-coupons swapped for Berkshire stock.** *Buffett:* at the halfway point (year 5 of 10), interest rates fell so far that the zero-coupon bond used to fund the $1 million prize appreciated rapidly to ~$950,000 — leaving very little further upside to maturity. The bet managers agreed to swap into Berkshire stock with Buffett's guarantee that it would be worth $1 million; currently worth ~$1.2 million. Hedge funds led by 13 points after year one but the S&P 500 has led the last four years. "The investment world has been very good at extracting a very significant percentage of the returns that investors get for themselves." See [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md), [investment-friction](../concepts/investment-friction.md).

## Q&A digest — Afternoon session

**★ A2 · Intensity is cumulative — the bathtub is not the research.** *(Doug Kass)* *Buffett:* the Bank of America idea appearing in a bathtub is the surface expression of 50+ years of following banking — "the bathtub really was not the key factor." The first American Express purchase required weeks of channel checks through restaurants and banks. The second purchase required only a golf game with Frank Olson of Hertz, who revealed that "there was no way in the world that he could get rid of American Express or even get them to cut their fees." The research was still deep; it just built on a foundation laid over decades. "I have every bit of the intensity — not manifested exactly the same way — but it's there every minute." *Munger:* "The first one was hard, and the second was easy... it's cumulative, eventually." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [american-express](../entities/american-express.md).

**★ A3 · Investment analysis is business analysis — no screens, no ratios alone.** *Buffett:* "we're looking at the aspects of a business," not a stock. No computerized screens; different numbers matter for different industries (a bank vs. ISCAR vs. a brand). "It is because I have some idea of what the company might look like in five or ten years, and I have a reasonable amount of confidence in that judgment, and there's a disparity in price and value, and it's big." *Munger:* "We don't know how to buy stocks just by looking at financial figures and making judgments based on the ratios... you really have to understand the company and its competitive position, and the reasons why its competitive position is what it is, and that is often not disclosed by the math." *Buffett:* "People with very high IQs who are good at math naturally look for a system where they can just look at the math... it's not that easy." *Munger:* "You'd do it poorly." See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**A4 · Buffett and Munger split on "new normal" — the rare public disagreement.** *Buffett:* "Charlie and I, in 54 years, I can't think of a time when we made a decision on a stock or a company where a macro discussion came up." The thing to know with high certainty is BNSF's competitive position in 10–20 years — not GDP or interest rates. Owning good businesses at reasonable prices will produce fine results. *Munger:* "I kind of agree with Bill Gross" (that returns will be lower than historical). "Less than we've enjoyed in our lifetimes, the new normal." *Buffett:* "What have we enjoyed in the last 10 years? It hasn't been that bad." *Munger:* "Not nearly as good as it was in the first 30." A rare on-record disagreement: Buffett optimistic about future business returns; Munger more cautious. *Munger:* advice to a surgeon near retirement: "Work an extra couple of years." See [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md), [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md).

**A5 · Fruit of the Loom vs. Gildan — commodity branding has limits.** *Buffett:* Gildan's non-branded wholesale screen-print business has taken real share; Fruit must keep costs down and constantly build brand recognition. Fruit turns out "first-quality, low-priced underwear with strong brand recognition." Market share in men's/boys' underwear should hold; not Coca-Cola in franchise power but not unbranded either. The risk is real ("it's not a business you can coast on") but the brand position at the lower end of priced goods is defensible. *Munger:* "We're not going to win every skirmish." See [fruit-of-the-loom](../entities/fruit-of-the-loom.md), [commodity-business-economics](../concepts/commodity-business-economics.md).

**A6 · Books that shaped the philosophy — Graham gave the bedrock.** *Buffett:* "The Intelligent Investor changed my life... Graham taught me how to think about a stock, how to think about the stock market, and that the market was there not to instruct me but to serve me." The Mr. Market framework, thinking about stocks as pieces of businesses rather than ticker symbols — that philosophy "has carried me along" and "I have not found any aspect of that bedrock philosophy that has flaws in it." Phil Fisher later taught the same thing Charlie Munger taught: "it's very important to get into a business with fundamentally good economics, and one you could ride with for decades rather than go from flower to flower every day." No single list of other ten books — "my mind is a blend of so many books I can't sort it out anymore" (Munger). See [ben-graham](../people/ben-graham.md), [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md).

**★ A7 · Airlines — still skeptical, but acknowledging the rail parallel.** *Buffett:* the industry has consolidated to four carriers controlling ~90% of traffic, and "if it ever gets down to where there's one airline and there's no regulation, it will be a wonderful business." Skepticism remains because: very low incremental costs per seat with enormous fixed costs, temptation to sell the last seat at any price, easy to create new entrants ("sexy" to people; you can go out and raise money for a new airline). "As I've said, if there had been a capitalist at Kitty Hawk, he should have shot down Orville and done us all a favor." *Munger:* the railroads did exactly what Bill Miller suggests for airlines — consolidated, controlled labor costs — and became wonderful. "What did we do? We missed it. We stumbled in very late to the party." Munger acknowledges airlines "go into my too-hard pile — but he could be right." *Supersession flag:* Berkshire built ~$9 billion stakes in American, United, Delta, and Southwest in 2016 — then sold all four positions in Q2 2020 at a loss amid COVID, confirming ongoing skepticism. See [commodity-business-economics](../concepts/commodity-business-economics.md), [bnsf](../entities/bnsf.md).

**A8 · Share repurchases — mixed emotions and the 1.2× book threshold.** *Buffett:* raising the buyback threshold from 1.1× to 1.2× book — but the important point is that book value has little correlation with intrinsic value across most companies; it has reasonable tracking utility at Berkshire specifically. "We don't really like the idea of running a company where it makes most of its money by buying its partners out at a discount, but if partners want to sell out at a discount, we also like the idea of making sure money that way." Berkshire will buy "a whole lot" if price falls significantly below intrinsic and cash balance allows. *Munger:* "Some people buy their own stock back regardless of price. That's not our system." See [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md), [intrinsic-value-vs-book-value](../concepts/intrinsic-value-vs-book-value.md).

**A11 · Short selling — "trading agony for money."** *Munger:* declines Doug Kass's challenge to seed his short fund: "The answer is no." Followed by the sharpest characterization: "We don't like trading agony for money." *Buffett:* both he and Munger attempted short selling in the past and "failed at it." He has identified frauds with certainty but making money shorting consistently "still is not a game that appeals to us over a long period of time." The asymmetry and the psychological toll of being right early and wrong before being right eventually — not suited to either temperament. See [risk-vs-volatility](../concepts/risk-vs-volatility.md).

**★ A12 · Pricing wonderful businesses — "we gag and we get there."** *Buffett:* "we usually feel we're paying too much... but we find the business so compelling, the management, our associates, so compelling, that we gag and we get there on the price." No perfect mathematical formula. "If you get a chance to buy a wonderful business — one that has economic characteristics that lead you to believe, with a high degree of certainty, that they will be earning unusual returns on capital over time — unusually high — and better yet, if they get the chance to employ more capital at again at high rates of return — that's the best of all businesses. And you probably should stretch a little." The See's Candy example: "Charlie was the one that said, 'For God's sakes, Warren, write the check.'" *Munger:* "It almost always happens... Modern prices are not cheap." And the summary principle: "The game of life is a game of everlasting learning. At least it is if you want to win." See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), [sees-candies](../entities/sees-candies.md).

**★ A14 · Benjamin Moore — the dealer pledge as a commitment device.** *Buffett:* when Berkshire bought Benjamin Moore, he made a video pledging to maintain the dealer distribution system; dealers had "invested their savings and passed on from generation to generation" dealerships built on that commitment. Home Depot and Lowe's repeatedly offered to take Benjamin Moore — which would produce a big short-term volume jump — but would "essentially gut" the dealer network and "double-cross a network of dealers that trusted us." The management that was moving toward violating that pledge was changed. Benjamin Moore's strategy stays focused on the high end with dealers. "It doesn't mean Sherwin-Williams won't do well... we are in a different segment." *Munger:* "I always wish we could buy five more like it tomorrow." The broader principle: explicit pre-commitments (including public pledges) are a form of competitive discipline — they constrain future management from opportunism and build seller trust. See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**A18 · Young money manager — develop an audited track record.** *Buffett:* "to attract money, you should deserve money, and you should develop a record over time that — and then you should be able to explain to people why that record is a product of sound thinking rather than simply being in tune with a trend or simply just being lucky." Todd and Ted were hired partly on track record, but record must be understood and believed, not just reported. Orangutan argument: 310 million orangutans flipping coins ten times will leave 300,000 who went ten-for-ten — those orangutans will try to attract capital. The manager's job is to distinguish genuine skill from luck. *Munger:* "Most people start with friends and family, or people whose trust they've already earned in some other way." See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).

**★ A20 · Howard Buffett as nonexecutive chairman — a culture protector, not an operator.** *(Doug Kass)* *Buffett:* Howard's role is not to run the business, allocate capital, or make operating decisions — "he is there as a protector of the culture." The probability of a mistake in picking a CEO is "less than 1 in 100, but not 0 in 100." A nonexecutive chairman who cares enormously about preserving the culture and has no interest in running the business makes it "far easier to make another change" if the CEO choice turns out wrong. Buffett has seen "more than one example" of a CEO who's a six out of ten, perfectly likable, who keeps the job long past when a change should be made because removing a chairman-CEO is very hard. "Blessed are the meek for they shall inherit the earth — but after they inherit the earth, will they stay meek?" The structural point: separating the chairman and CEO roles with an owner-oriented non-executive chair is a genuine governance innovation, not just a formality. *Munger:* "The Mungers are much safer with Howard there." See [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md), [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**★ A21 · Near-zero rates "brutal" for savers — and what that implies for equities.** *Buffett:* "it is brutal" for people who live off their investment income — the loss of purchasing power "is just staggering." A million dollars at a quarter of a percent yields $2,500 a year — "that is not what people anticipated when they were saving over the years." His consistent advice since 2008: own equities. "I feel sorry for people that have clung to fixed-dollar investments... probably 90 percent of the time in my life, owning businesses has made more sense than owning fixed-dollar investments." *Munger:* "They had to hurt somebody, and the savers were convenient." Savers' losses are asset owners' gains — a redistribution that is policy-driven, not market-driven. See [bonds-and-inflation](../concepts/bonds-and-inflation.md), [investment-categories](../concepts/investment-categories.md).

**★ A22 · IBM moat — lower conviction than BNSF, still a substantial position.** *Buffett:* "I do not understand the moat around an IBM as well as I understand the moat around a Coca-Cola... I could think of some things that could go wrong with IBM. I can't think of anything that could go wrong with BNSF." The lower conviction doesn't preclude "a very large position" — it just means less certainty in the language used. IBM also carries a $75–80 billion pension obligation alongside its assets ("a big annuity company on the side... balls can take funny bounces in the annuity field"). *Munger:* "We would never have that degree of confidence about Apple, no matter what their financial statements showed... it's just too hard." *Supersession flag:* Buffett sold virtually all of Berkshire's IBM shares in 2017, telling CNBC he had "reassessed" his views on the competitive position. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**★ A24 · Don't invest by categories — find good businesses at attractive prices.** *Buffett:* "We don't really start out looking to either emerging markets or specific countries or anything of the sort... if you told us we could only invest in the United States the rest of our lives, we would not regard that as a huge hardship." The categories-first approach (country, sector, theme) is how investment advice is sold, not how businesses are evaluated. "The thing to do is just find a good business at an attractive price and buy it." *Munger:* "It's a great way to sell investment advice, to have a whole lot of different categories, lots of commissions, lots of advice, lots of action... When we hear somebody talking concepts... we tend to think they're probably going to do better at selling than at investing." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [investment-friction](../concepts/investment-friction.md).

**★ A25 · Housing bubble post-mortem — fear en masse, confidence one at a time.** *Buffett:* Washington was "a very big part" of the bubble but the account is "not complete" — "the whole country, almost, really kind of went crazy in terms of housing." The bubble dynamic: skeptics look like idiots for three to five years while everyone who jumped on the bandwagon makes easy money. "You've got your neighbor getting richer than you are because he went along and you didn't. And that sort of thing — the bandwagon effect — those things are very hard to resist." Key behavioral observation: "confidence comes back sort of one at a time, but when they get greedy, they get greedy en masse, and when they get scared, they get scared en masse too." Leverage was the fatal amplifier — introduced into housing "in a huge, huge way." *Munger:* "The government could've pulled away the punch bowl before everybody was totally drunk. Instead, the government increased the proof." See [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md), [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

**★ A28 · Fraud tells — accounting and human character.** *Buffett:* can't give a 40-item checklist, but "people give themselves away a lot" — there are "tells" in human character, just as in poker. In insurance specifically, he's seen reserves managed artificially before IPOs (down before the offering, built when buying in stock). *Munger:* the clearest tell was a man offering a fire insurance company who said "it's like taking candy from babies — we only write fire insurance on concrete structures that are underwater." Deduced fraud instantly. On accounting complexity: "the financial statements of big banks are way harder to understand now than they used to be... they've got so many footnotes and there's so much gobbledygook." Gen Re had 23,000 derivative contracts — "Charlie and I could've spent 24 hours a day and had 10 or 20 math PhDs and we still wouldn't have known what was going on." Munger coined the category: "good until reached for." See [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md), [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md).

## Connections to other sources

- The 3G/Heinz partnership model (M12) is structurally new in the meeting corpus — no prior meeting introduced a formal co-acquisition-with-operating-partner structure. It anticipates the evolving role of Berkshire as a capital partner rather than sole owner/operator, a theme that will recur.
- The "800-number" brand argument (M25) extends the crisis-capital theme from [berkshire-2008-meeting](berkshire-2008-meeting.md) and [berkshire-2009-meeting](berkshire-2009-meeting.md), where Berkshire actually deployed the Goldman Sachs, GE, and Bank of America preferred deals; here Buffett explicitly argues that the franchise outlasts any individual.
- The Teledyne discussion (M34) is the sharpest published critique of Henry Singleton's methods from Buffett and Munger; it clarifies what Berkshire's decentralization model deliberately avoids — centralized management, aggressive stock issuance — while crediting Singleton's buyback discipline.
- The airline skepticism (A7) with Munger's concession that "we proved ourselves slow learners" on railroads is a rare self-critique that directly bridges to [berkshire-2012-meeting](berkshire-2012-meeting.md)'s A28 ("we sort of buy barriers; we don't build them") — the rail parallel makes the airline question genuinely open by 2013 even as the verdict remains uncertain. *Superseded*: Berkshire built large airline positions in 2016 and sold them all in 2020.
- The cumulative-knowledge and intensity argument (A2) is the meeting's answer to the "cigar-butt to wonderful business" evolution story told across [berkshire-2012-meeting](berkshire-2012-meeting.md) A26 and multiple letters; here Buffett clarifies that the *method* of research changed (less legwork, more pattern recognition) but the *intensity* and *depth* did not.
- The IBM conviction-vs-BNSF-certainty spectrum (A22) foreshadows the IBM exit Buffett announced in 2017, making this the last annual meeting where the IBM thesis was still intact; the conviction language ("I could think of some things that could go wrong with IBM") reads in retrospect as the early warning.
- The interest-rate-as-gravity metaphor (M21) is the 2013 elaboration of the interest-rate sensitivity argument from [berkshire-1994-meeting](berkshire-1994-meeting.md) M53 ("every asset is 100 percent sensitive to interest rates"), now extended to show the distributional consequences for savers — a consequence that didn't exist in 1994's higher-rate world.
