# Berkshire Hathaway 2010 Annual Meeting

Held in May 2010 with the economy in a "sputtering" recovery, the just-closed BNSF acquisition freshly on the books, and the SEC's Abacus fraud suit against Goldman Sachs barely two weeks old. Buffett opened by defending Goldman and Lloyd Blankfein at length — the meeting's set-piece — and the day returned repeatedly to the crisis's aftermath: derivatives and financial reform, sovereign debt (Greece), inflation, and the moral hazard baked into Wall Street's incentive systems. Underneath the topical questions the durable lessons are unusually clean: judge a transaction by your own analysis, never by who sits across the table; volatility-tolerance is itself a competitive moat; size is the anchor that turns "wonderful" into merely "good"; the circle of competence is defined by its perimeter, not its size; and the right owners, like the right managers, are cultivated by telling the truth about what you are. Because it is a transcript, the page keeps the two-layer convention: a distilled thematic top, then a comprehensive Q&A digest as the home for detail.

## How to read the Q&A digest

- **★** marks a timeless, load-bearing exchange worth citing in the future; unmarked entries still carry investor value but are secondary or period-specific.
- Answers are attributed separately to **Buffett** and **Munger** — a Munger line appears only when he added a distinct lesson (he answered "Nothing to add" to most).
- **M#/A#** map to the numbered items in the raw morning/afternoon transcript for traceability.

## Key themes

### Judge the transaction, not the counterparty

Buffett's long Abacus defense (M6) reduces to one underwriting principle: "It's our job to evaluate the risk of the bonds and to determine the proper premium… If I have to care about who's on the other side of the trade, I should not be insuring bonds." He walks through Berkshire's own municipal-bond insurance — a list of states handed to it by Lehman Brothers, taken as-is for a ~$160M premium — and argues ACA lost on Abacus because it made "a dumb insurance decision," not because Paulson knew more. Whether the other side is hedging, shorting, or long is irrelevant to the price of the risk; a bond insurer that blames the counterparty afterward was never doing its job. Munger's sharper coda (M6) refuses to hide behind legality: "the standards in business should not be what's legal and convenient. The standards should be different." See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

### Volatility-tolerance is itself the moat

Berkshire's insurance edge is not a model or a niche — it is the willingness to accept outcomes no rival can stomach. Munger (M29): "we are deliberately seeking a method of operation which will give us occasional big losses in a single year… And that's a competitive advantage, that willingness to endure fluctuating annual results." Buffett agrees it "comes very close to a permanent and substantial advantage… and it gets wider every year," because competitors "know what we do, they just don't want to do it, or they're unable to." The same posture underwrites the whole company: "Huge amounts of debt are not going to do us in" (A33), and Berkshire is "running this place so that it can stand anything" (M16) — a $250B industry catastrophe would still leave it with positive earnings. Strength is built to be used when others are paralyzed. See [super-cat-insurance](../concepts/super-cat-insurance.md), [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md).

### Size is the anchor — wonderful businesses don't soak up capital

The pivot into capital-intensive businesses (BNSF, MidAmerican) is a concession to scale, stated plainly: the returns will be "good, not brilliant." "If we could double the capital [in See's]… at anything like the returns we receive on the first 40 million… we'd be down there this afternoon with the money. Unfortunately, the wonderful businesses don't soak up capital. That's one of the reasons they're wonderful" (M15). The best businesses on earth run on little or negative capital — magazines, insurance, Blue Chip's float, See's, Apple (A26) — but they can't absorb the tens of billions Berkshire now generates. Buffett's honest terminal forecast (A28): "there will come a time when we cannot intelligently use 100 percent of the capital that we develop internally," and then the money will go back to owners. See [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md), [cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses](../concepts/cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses.md).

### The circle of competence is defined by its perimeter

The most-quotable line of the day (A25): "The biggest thing is not how big your circle of competence is, but knowing where the perimeter is." You need to understand only the businesses you actually buy; a small circle is "not a killer." The Harley-Davidson decision embodies it (M16): "I knew enough to lend them money; I didn't know enough to buy the equity" — a 15% bond was a simple "are they going broke?" question, the equity a hard one about margins and volumes. And the perimeter can move: Munger credits BYD as proof "the old men are continuing to learn" (M25, A28), Berkshire's first bet on developing-edge technology, made only because the company had "won its spurs" before they found it. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [margin-of-safety](../concepts/margin-of-safety.md).

### Own the temperament: the farm has no daily quote

Asked how to have bought in the 2008–09 panic without being scared, Buffett reframes the whole problem (A18): if you buy a farm or an apartment house, "do you get a quote next week?… No." A stock quote is an asset — daily liquidity — that most investors turn into a liability by reacting to it. "When it goes down we love it, because we'll buy more. And if it goes up, it kills us to buy more." The corollary is that fear is not curable by advice: "to expect somebody else to tell you when to buy and therefore get your courage back up… he's a broker's friend, but he's not going to make a lot of money." Munger's antidote (A18): "maybe you should get your feet wet with a little more failure." See [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md), [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md).

### Cultivate the right owners and managers by telling the truth

Berkshire attracts stable owners the way a restaurant does diners — by honest advertising (A23): quoting Phil Fisher, "what you can't do is have hamburgers on the outside on the marquee and deliver French food inside." The annual report is written to two intelligent outsiders — "I start mentally off writing, 'Dear Doris and Bertie'" — so shareholders self-select into sync (A8). The same honesty governs pay: no compensation consultant, no cross-Berkshire formula, and the real target is subjective — "the real thing I really want to pay managers for is widening the moat that separates our business from our competitors' businesses over time" (M26). And the non-negotiable, repeated in the biennial letter to managers (M27): "we won't trade reputation for money" — with a new line added, that "the other guy is doing it" is never a good enough reason. See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md), [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

### "Everyone else is doing it" is the engine of bad behavior

The crisis, in Buffett and Munger's telling, was less malevolence than situational ethics scaled up. Buffett's exhibit (A17): when FASB ruled stock options an expense in the 1990s, 498 of the S&P 500 chose the non-preferred (don't-expense) route — including people he'd trust as executors — because "I can't do it if the other guy isn't doing it." Munger adds that most bad behavior "comes from subconscious poor cognition," and that the cure is structural: "have a system where the people who are making the decisions bear the consequences" — the opposite of a mortgage machine where everyone passed the paper on at a profit and "nobody felt any responsibility." See [institutional-imperative](../concepts/institutional-imperative.md), [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

## Q&A digest — Morning session

**★ M3 · Ignore quarterly EPS — and the people who whisper it.** *Buffett:* Berkshire won't even print quarterly EPS; undue focus on it is "a bad idea for investors" and "a terrible idea for managers." Cites a study that took reported earnings out one extra digit and found a "statistically impossible" shortage of fours — accountants finding a tenth of a cent to round upward. Managing to a whispered penny corrupts the numbers. See [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md), [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md).

**★ M6 · Abacus / Goldman — judge the risk, not the counterparty.** *Buffett:* long-form defense. ACA lost because it was a bond insurer that "drifted" into structured credit it didn't understand ("I was Snow White but I drifted") — Berkshire took Lehman's list of states as-is for ~$160M and would never blame the shorter afterward: "If I have to care about who's on the other side of the trade, I should not be insuring bonds." The Goldman preferred "is paying us $15 a second." *Munger:* would have "voted with the minority two" on the 3–2 SEC decision — but "every business ought to decline a lot of business that's perfectly legal and proper to accept." *Supersession:* Goldman settled the Abacus suit with the SEC for a then-record $550M in July 2010, and the director tied to the Galleon leak (Rajat Gupta) was later convicted of insider trading — the confident dismissal here did not survive the year. See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**M7 · Financial reform — a new Glass-Steagall.** *Buffett:* wouldn't say how he'd vote on the 1,550-page bill. *Munger:* every big investment bank except Goldman "was going to go blooey" under stress; a system that fragile "should be changed so it's less permissive." Would "make Paul Volcker look like a sissy" and wants "a new version of Glass-Steagall that drastically limits" what banks may do — unlimited flexibility plus government-backed credit makes people "go plum crazy." See [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md).

**M8 · Retroactive collateral on old derivative contracts.** *Buffett:* under the current bill, required collateral would be zero unless Berkshire were declared "dangerous to the system" — unlikely with ~250 contracts vs. rivals' millions. Berkshire deliberately forwent perhaps "a billion dollars of extra premiums" by writing un-collateralized contracts; forcing collateral retroactively would be "like… Congress comes along later on and says, 'All houses have to be sold furnished.'" *Munger:* it would be "of dubious constitutionality… both unfair and stupid." See [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md).

**★ M9 · Greece, the euro, and borrowing in your own currency.** *Buffett:* "I don't know how this movie ends… I try not to go to movies like that." The durable distinction: a country borrowing in its own currency (US, Japan) faces "no possibility, none, of default" — it can print — while one forced to borrow in others' currencies "can really put you out of business very quickly." Greece is a sovereign that surrendered its printing press to the euro. Recent events "would make me more bearish on all currencies." *Munger:* published debt understates the problem because "the unfunded promises… are much greater than the government bonds outstanding." See [bonds-and-inflation](../concepts/bonds-and-inflation.md).

**M10 · Wells notice and materiality.** *Buffett & Munger:* neither would have deemed an Abacus-related Wells notice material to a company earning "many, many billions of dollars a year"; "if every company reported every little thing… we'd just have unlimited confusing reports." The bigger the company, the less likely any one notice is material. No contact from the Galleon investigation.

**M12 · Foundation gifts don't pressure the stock.** *Buffett:* his ~1.5% annual gifts to five foundations are trivial against NYSE turnover of "well over 100 percent" of shares; "if 1.5 percent of the outstanding shares… move the price down in a year, it probably deserves to move down." *Munger:* the distribution likely helped by getting Berkshire into the S&P 500. See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**M13 · "I would not run from the United States."** *Buffett:* the biggest real threat is a "massive nuclear, chemical, or biological attack," low over one year but high over 50. Otherwise the US "system is remarkable… it does unleash human potential like has never been seen before"; a rising China and India is "not a zero-sum game." *Munger:* Berkshire has no "global allocation system" — it finds sensible things and concentrates, preferring "the more responsible" countries. See [american-tailwind](../concepts/american-tailwind.md).

**M14 · Succession — CEO ready in 24 hours, CIO can wait.** *Buffett:* a new CEO would be in place "within 24 hours" and the board knows who; the investment role "is really far less urgent" and can be "fairly leisurely." The four investment candidates "didn't distinguish themselves" in 2008 but "did pretty darn well" in 2009. *Munger:* "quite optimistic that the culture of Berkshire will last a long, long time and will outlast, greatly, the life of the founder." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**★ M15 · Capital-intensive means "good, not brilliant."** *Buffett:* would pour more capital into See's "this afternoon" if it could earn like the first $40M — but "the wonderful businesses don't soak up capital. That's one of the reasons they're wonderful." BNSF and MidAmerican make sense — translate retained dollars into "a little something more than a dollar of present value" — but "if anybody expects brilliant returns from this base… we don't know how to do it." See [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md), [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md).

**★ M16 · Debt vs. equity in a distress (Harley-Davidson).** *Buffett:* took Harley's 15% bonds, not the $12 stock, because "I knew enough to lend them money; I didn't know enough to buy the equity" — a simple "are they going broke?" question beats a hard one about margins and volumes. Berkshire's 50-year insurance liabilities also constrain how aggressively it holds stocks. *Munger:* "very often, when you're looking at a distress situation and buy the bonds, you should have bought the stock" — a promising area. *Buffett:* Graham wrote in *Security Analysis* (1934) that junior securities usually do better but "you may sleep better with the senior securities." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [ben-graham](../people/ben-graham.md).

**M17 · Building culture is easier than changing it.** *Buffett:* "a lot easier to build a new organization around a culture than… to change the culture of an existing organization." Berkshire's is so ingrained "the culture would basically reject" an intruder; at Salomon, "I would not grade myself A+." *Munger:* on changing an existing culture, "my failure rate has been 100 percent."

**M18 · Ajit Jain and the float that keeps peaking.** *Buffett:* Ajit "cannot be replaced," but the reinsurance unit has advantages beyond him (a Jesuit-disciplined cadre of ~30). On float: "every year I think our float has peaked"; now over $60B, "we will not organically grow the float… at a fast clip from here… we may fight to stay even." *Supersession:* float went on to roughly double past $100B over the following decade, mostly through further large deals. See [float](../concepts/float.md), [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md).

**M19 · India — opportunity throttled by ownership limits.** *Buffett:* foreign insurers are capped (~24.9%), and "I really hate to take some of our managerial talent and put them to work for something we only own 25 percent of." ISCAR is thriving there. *Munger:* Indian government "paralysis, endless due process" lets China grow faster, though he admires the democracy behind it.

**M20 · Inflation prospects "have increased."** *Buffett:* "if you wanted to bet… I'd bet on higher, and maybe a lot higher." Massive debt was "the medicine," and "weaning ourselves from the medicine" may be harder than the illness; no country running 10%-of-GDP deficits long-term escapes "a significant diminution in the value of their currency." See [bonds-and-inflation](../concepts/bonds-and-inflation.md).

**M21 · Financial habits and McDonald's.** *Buffett:* getting good financial habits early "is enormously important," the aim of the Secret Millionaires Club; elementary-level learning matters more than graduate degrees. *Munger:* praises McDonald's as an educator — it hires marginal people and teaches them "to show up on time… and observe the discipline," launching many to higher jobs.

**M23 · The best inflation hedge is your own talent.** *Munger:* "The best defense… is to contribute as much as you can to the civilization"; trying "to profit from the inflation" by outsmarting others "is a much more dangerous course." *Buffett:* "Your money can be inflated away but your talent can't be inflated away" — the best brain surgeon or painter "will always command your share of the resources."

**M24 · NetJets — a candid post-mortem, and no change of method.** *Buffett:* the $711M loss came from buying planes "at prices that were fictitious" and letting operating costs outrun revenue; credits Dave Sokol with a turnaround (now >$50M quarterly profit) "like nobody could have." Compares it to his own 20 years in textiles. *Munger:* in context — 30 businesses run hands-off, working "95 percent of the time very well" — "it's not a big failure record." *Supersession:* Sokol, lavishly praised here and in M25, resigned under an ethics cloud (the Lubrizol share purchases) within a year, in March 2011. See [dave-sokol](../people/dave-sokol.md), [turnarounds-seldom-turn](../concepts/turnarounds-seldom-turn.md).

**★ M25 · BYD — the old men are still learning.** *Munger:* Berkshire "would not have made an investment in BYD if the opportunity had come along five or 10 years earlier"; it shows "the old men are continuing to learn, and that's absolutely essential." Credits Dave Sokol with helping "the learning process." Berkshire's first move onto the developing edge of technology — expanding, not abandoning, the circle. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ M26 · Why Berkshire will never hire a compensation consultant.** *Buffett:* businesses differ too much (insurance with "minus capital," BNSF with "tons," See's with "very little") for one standard; a consultant's cost-of-capital factor on See's "is nonsense." The real objective is subjective: "the real thing I really want to pay managers for is widening the moat that separates our business from our competitors' businesses over time." *Munger:* our decentralized personnel policy "clearly works best for us"; imperial headquarters "is frequently hated in the field." See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**★ M27 · "We won't trade reputation for money."** *Buffett:* the biennial letter to managers opens "we've got all the money we need… but we don't have a shred more reputation than we need, and we won't trade reputation for money," with a new line: if the best reason for an action "is that the other guy is doing it, it's not good enough." Wants problems surfaced fast via the hotline and internal audit. *Munger:* "we care more about that than business mistakes, way more… we celebrate wealth only when it's been fairly won and wisely used." See [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md), [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**M28 · Regulated railroad returns.** *Buffett:* the Surface Transportation Board's ~10.5% return on invested capital "is not a crazy… standard" — high enough to induce the "much needed investment over the next 10, 20, 30 years," and the country and railroads share an interest in "not earning exorbitant profits." Railroads carry more downside than electric utilities in a recession. *Munger:* the industry has been "essentially totally rebuilt" over 30–40 years — "wise regulation accompanied by wise management." See [bnsf](../entities/bnsf.md).

**★ M29 · Lumpy earnings as competitive advantage.** *Munger:* "we are deliberately seeking a method of operation which will give us occasional big losses in a single year… And that's a competitive advantage, that willingness to endure fluctuating annual results." Everyone else avoids it. *Buffett:* "a huge advantage… it gets wider every year"; a $3B Katrina loss left him feeling "no different the next day." Insurance is the business of "taking the other guys' desire to smooth their earnings, and, in exchange, get… larger, lumpy earnings over time." See [super-cat-insurance](../concepts/super-cat-insurance.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**★ M30 · Derivatives, Keynes, and the casino.** *Munger:* the usefulness of derivatives "has always been overrated"; a world with only responsibly-conducted commodity/currency/metals contracts "would be a better place." *Buffett:* reads Keynes's *General Theory* ch. 12 — "When the capital development of a country becomes a by-product of the activities of a casino, the job is likely to be ill-done" — and recounts his lone 1982 letter to Congressman Dingell opposing the S&P 500 futures contract. Notes the "60/40" tax break for holding an index future "for 30 seconds" as "a tribute to the lobbying power of a rather small group." See [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md).

**M31 · Losing (so far) the bet against hedge funds.** *Buffett:* two years into the ten-year charitable wager with Protégé Partners — funds-of-funds vs. an S&P index fund — "at this point I'm behind." *Supersession:* Buffett won decisively when the bet concluded in 2017, the index fund routing the funds-of-funds. See [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md).

## Q&A digest — Afternoon session

**A1 · Short sellers speaking out is healthy — if held responsible.** *Buffett:* no problem with responsible bullish or bearish speech; attacking "the conventional wisdom" always draws attack because "you're threatening people's positions" (cf. the efficient-market orthodoxy 30 years ago). Bad practices — spreading untruths — have historically been "more on the long side… by some margin." *Munger:* often "the accountants that allowed the lousy accounting are the ones that ought to be held in the dock." See [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md).

**A2 · No forced synergy among subsidiaries.** *Buffett:* ~6,000 Dairy Queens, 99% franchised, so Berkshire can't dictate that they serve Coke; "we do not tell our companies to do business with each other" — synergies happen "at the operational level" when each side earns it. *Munger:* "we really like it that way." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**A3 · Act like an owner and the competition thins.** *Buffett:* he makes very few hiring decisions (managers hire their own people); "you'd be surprised at how little competition you really have if you start thinking like you would if you were an owner of the place, and working like you would."

**★ A4 · The retained-earnings dollar test — and admitting the error.** *Buffett:* his 1984 test (retain only if each dollar creates "at least one dollar of market value") was originally worded badly — a market slump could fail it despite good allocation — so he rewrote the economic principles in the 2009 report. Currently each retained dollar has produced "something over $1.30 of market value"; if that ceases "over time, we should figure out something else to do." *Munger:* "I like people that… find an error and rub my nose in it, particularly when it's your error." See [dividend-policy](../concepts/dividend-policy.md), [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md).

**A5 · A safety net is society's job, not Berkshire's.** *Buffett:* Berkshire hires "when we have something for them to do" (net hiring now as BNSF volumes recover); creative destruction demands "a social safety net," but Berkshire shouldn't be it. *Munger:* make-work "to increase human hope" would "over time… reduce human hope."

**A6 · Why GEICO isn't in China/India.** *Buffett:* the ~24.9% ownership cap makes it not worth diverting managerial talent; GEICO has gone from 2% to 8.5% US share with "plenty to do here," and Berkshire lacks the same competitive advantage abroad. See [geico](../entities/geico.md).

**★ A8 · Write to "Doris and Bertie."** *Buffett:* the annual report is aimed at his two intelligent, non-expert sisters — "I start mentally off writing, 'Dear Doris and Bertie,'" targeting ~12,000 words, because more "obfuscates… rather than illuminates." Look-through earnings get less emphasis now because undistributed earnings of non-controlled holdings are "not more than… 15 percent" of reported earnings. *Munger:* "the details can change as the facts change." See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md), [look-through-earnings](../concepts/look-through-earnings.md).

**★ A10 · Newspapers are losing the fight with the internet.** *Buffett:* three-quarters of newspaper money came from advertising, and papers are "not the only game in town" any more — a "chicken and egg" collapse as advertisers leave when subscribers fall and vice versa. "There was probably nothing that looked more bulletproof than a daily newspaper," but "I cannot make an affirmative decision on newspaper ownership." Recalls Lord Thomson pricing "to make 40 percent pre-tax. Above that, I feel I may be gouging" — "those days are gone." *Munger:* the weakening of the "Fourth Estate" "is not good for the country… I think it's very sad." *Supersession:* within about two years Berkshire reversed course, buying dozens of local papers (Media General, the Omaha World-Herald, BH Media) at low prices — an economics call, not a moat call. See [media-economics](../concepts/media-economics.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**★ A11 · Small money still finds $100 bills.** *Buffett:* fewer than before, but "there will always be opportunities if you're not working with large amounts of money"; the investment-management conflict is that "asset gathering can become a way more important part of your income than asset managing." Example: Munger's Daily Journal sat on cash for years then turned $15M into $45M in ~six months by waiting for things to get "really ridiculous." *Munger* (quoting Buffett's frequent advice): "take the high road. It's far less crowded." See [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md), [passive-return-crossbar](../concepts/passive-return-crossbar.md).

**A12 · Municipal-bond insurers must fear contagion.** *Buffett:* Berkshire isn't writing much muni insurance now because the premiums aren't "appropriate" or "fair" (echoing the "the premiums are wrong" logic from M6); the real danger is "correlation" — if one entity defaults with no consequences, others ask "why should I have a great fiscal reputation when I can have lower taxes?" Every bond insurer except Berkshire "either failed or effectively failed" over structured credit. *Munger:* invest where places are "both prosperous and disciplined" — "It's hard for an empty sack to stand upright." See [lending-discipline](../concepts/lending-discipline.md).

**A13 · Short-term moves are unknowable and irrelevant.** *Buffett:* "I don't know what the stock market's going to do next week, or next month, or next year"; over 10–20 years he'd "much rather own equities… than cash, or… a 10 or 20-year bond," partly because he's "very unenthusiastic about the alternatives" — a "modest positive real return." *Munger:* "people should get accustomed… to doing less well… in real terms." See [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md).

**★ A14 · Don't outsource investment judgment (rating agencies).** *Buffett:* the ratings business is "incredibly wonderful" — no capital, real pricing power, "you can't shop pricing" — but the agencies "succumbed to the same mania" and "couldn't see a world where residential housing, countrywide, would collapse." Berkshire has "never paid any attention to ratings… We don't think we should farm out, outsource, investment judgment. If we can't do it ourselves, we just don't do it." *Munger:* they "drifted with the stupidity of their times," fed by "asininity in American business education." See [model-risk](../concepts/model-risk.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**A15 · The oil "windfall," and innovation.** *Buffett:* oil "contributed in a huge way to the prosperity of the world," but the world won't depend on it "for the next hundred years — don't ever give up on humans' ability to innovate." *Munger:* an advanced civilization "can get ahead without the oil if we have to" (citing Freeman Dyson); relatedly (A19) he is "enormously optimistic" about solar — but as a decision rule declines to install panels now because "they're going to get a lot cheaper and I'd rather wait," and calls corn ethanol "a stunningly stupid idea." See [american-tailwind](../concepts/american-tailwind.md).

**★ A16 · Kraft–Cadbury and the manager's itch for easier ground.** *Buffett:* "I didn't like either the Cadbury decision or the pizza decision… the odds are it was a dumb deal." Dissects the tax-inefficient pizza sale (net ~$2.5B, not the announced $3.7B, for ~$340M of growing pre-tax earnings) and the "very fancy price" for Cadbury; Kraft trades "below the value of its constituent parts." *Munger:* managers "hate the tough competitive conditions in the business they're in, and… yearn for some business where it's less difficult" — the reason "Xerox bought Crum & Forster, one of the dumbest acquisitions in all time." *Buffett:* "an absolute army of lawyers, investment advisors, public relations people" all have "a strong economic interest in having you push ahead on deal, after deal, after deal." See [institutional-imperative](../concepts/institutional-imperative.md), [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**★ A17 · Integrity and "everyone else is doing it."** *Munger:* integrity "is the safest way to make money"; "everybody mouths the integrity, even when it's lacking." *Buffett:* when options-expensing became preferred in the 1990s, 498 of 500 S&P companies chose the non-preferred route — good people who said "I can't do it if the other guy isn't doing it." *Munger:* most bad behavior "comes from subconscious poor cognition"; the cure is "a system where the people who are making the decisions bear the consequences" — mortgage securitization was "deeply immoral" precisely because no one did. See [institutional-imperative](../concepts/institutional-imperative.md), [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

**★ A18 · You can't profit if you get scared when others are scared.** *Buffett:* if your temperament breaks under others' fear, "you are not going to make a lot of money in securities." Buying a farm or apartment house, "do you get a quote next week?… No." "When it goes down we love it, because we'll buy more. And if it goes up, it kills us to buy more." Advice-driven courage is worthless — "he's a broker's friend." *Munger:* "maybe you should get your feet wet with a little more failure." See [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md), [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md).

**A20 · No "exploding bananas" in the portfolio.** *Buffett:* the portfolio's undervaluation "is not dramatic, and… has been exceeded many times in the past"; Berkshire will "do reasonably well over time" but holds nothing "likely to increase dramatically in value from here." A sober counter to shareholder over-optimism. See [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md).

**A21 · Optimism and lowered expectations.** *Munger:* the energy problem has "a solution… on the horizon"; and "There's no better way to be happy than getting your expectations down." *Buffett:* "than getting your results up." *Munger:* "If I can be optimistic when I'm nearly dead, surely the rest of you can handle a little inflation."

**★ A23 · Loyal owners come from honest advertising.** *Buffett:* anyone can buy a public stock, "they elect you," so you must "let them know exactly what sort of institution you plan to run." Quotes Phil Fisher: "what you can't do is have hamburgers on the outside on the marquee and deliver French food inside." "Our goal in life is not to spend our time associating with people who are going to be disappointed with us." *Munger:* the family-like relationship began "by accident" — investing for friends — and "we had enough sense… to keep doing it." See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**A24 · Zero rates punish the fearful.** *Buffett:* a tenth-of-a-percent money-market yield since "Columbus landed" would barely have doubled your money; low rates exert enormous upward "pressure… on the value of everything else" as the frightened get "pushed into other investments" — much of the past year's rally. *Munger:* "the reality of our situation is almost amusingly depressing" — either rates rise (stocks lose their relative edge) or they don't (Japan-style stagnation). See [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md).

**★ A25 · Getting better at valuing businesses.** *Buffett:* "The biggest thing is not how big your circle of competence is, but knowing where the perimeter is." Keep asking questions about the businesses in your own town — which will be around in 20 years, which is hard to compete with — and "always remember that margin of safety." A basket of 20 cheap Korean stocks worked "even if a crook might run one of them." *Munger:* "get up each morning and try and go to bed that night a little wiser"; people who do that "almost never fail utterly." *Buffett:* "It's avoiding the dumb thing." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [margin-of-safety](../concepts/margin-of-safety.md), [concentration-vs-diversification](../concepts/concentration-vs-diversification.md).

**★ A26 · The best businesses need little or negative capital.** *Buffett:* "a good business" is defined by "the capital actually needed in the business," not the price paid — Coca-Cola could run "with no capital." Magazines (People, Sports Illustrated), insurance, and Blue Chip's float run on negative capital — customers pay in advance. "The best ones… are the ones that can get very large while needing no capital," but the great consumer franchises are also what "the rest of the world" wants, so they get bid up. *Munger:* "the formula never changes." See [return-on-equity-yardstick](../concepts/return-on-equity-yardstick.md), [float](../concepts/float.md).

**★ A27 · The phone rarely rings — and that's the moat.** *Buffett:* the ~$75–100M minimum pre-tax "weeds out a lot of phone calls"; three or four serious calls a year "is a good year." Ready to say yes to a $10B deal "on Monday" and "figure out how we do it." *Munger:* "it's human revulsion that has helped us" — the best sellers "are so smart that they're revolted by almost everything else" and won't sell into "some fee-driven buying system." The ISCAR seller and another who came "because you're the only guy left" wanted "a permanent home," not a competitor's dismantling or a private-equity resale. See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), [stock-as-acquisition-currency](../concepts/stock-as-acquisition-currency.md).

**★ A28 · The size question Berkshire asks itself.** *Buffett:* the surprising-himself candor — "there will come a time when we cannot intelligently use 100 percent of the capital that we develop internally," and then whatever is best for shareholders "will be done." BNSF was "better for their shareholders than… ours," but good enough for Berkshire. *Munger:* Berkshire will bring in investors "starting sooner than many of you expect," possibly "with abilities that Warren lacks"; BYD proves the willingness to do "unusual" things that work. See [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md), [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md).

**A29 · Find your passion (Mrs. B).** *Buffett:* successful managers share one factor — "they love what they do." Rose Blumkin built the ~$400M Nebraska Furniture Mart from "$500 of capital" though she "never went to a day of school in her life and couldn't read or write" — the green price tags on her home furniture. "Find your passion, and then don't let anything stop you." See [rose-blumkin](../people/rose-blumkin.md), [nebraska-furniture-mart](../entities/nebraska-furniture-mart.md).

**★ A30 · The fundamental algorithm of life.** *Munger:* asked for a unifying theory behind Berkshire's contrarian practices, he answers "It's pragmatism… we've found through experience that it works better," and distills it: "the fundamental algorithm of life. Repeat what works." See [inversion](../concepts/inversion.md).

**A31 · High-speed passenger rail isn't economic.** *Buffett:* competing with auto and air, it "won't meet the test of private economics" without heavy subsidy — the US lacks "point-to-point density." *Munger:* skeptical for Los Angeles ("a bottomless pit of cost"); it works in Japan/China with "a different calculus." A clean illustration of return-on-capital thinking applied to infrastructure. See [bnsf](../entities/bnsf.md).

**A32 · Berkshire can absorb the worst catastrophe.** *Buffett:* even a $250B industry earthquake or hurricane — Berkshire's share "not much more than 3 percent… call it 4 percent — that'd be $10 billion" — would likely still leave positive annual earnings. "We're in a different class, in terms of even being able to handle them." *Munger:* much earthquake damage is uninsured, unlike fire. See [super-cat-insurance](../concepts/super-cat-insurance.md), [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md).

**★ A33 · "Huge amounts of debt are not going to do us in."** *Buffett:* Berkshire "made the bet in 2008 there would not be" a total meltdown — that the government "could solve the panic… and finally that they would" — and "went all in." Barring "some massive nuclear, chemical, or biological attack," Berkshire "was built… to withstand anything that any corporation can." *Munger:* "I'm not worried about it." See [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md).
