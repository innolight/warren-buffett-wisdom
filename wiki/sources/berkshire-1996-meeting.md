# Berkshire Hathaway 1996 Annual Meeting

The 1996 meeting is the **Class B meeting**: half the morning is given over to an issuance Buffett deliberately engineered to be *unattractive to speculators* — an underwriting spread "about as low as any I've ever seen," an unlimited supply so it "couldn't be a hot stock," and the almost unheard-of admission, printed in the prospectus, that "we do not think it's undervalued." That single act distills the meeting's spine: a manager who treats the *quality of his shareholder base* as something to be cultivated and protected, who wants the stock to track intrinsic value so no cohort of owners profits at another's expense, and who would rather repel a buyer than disappoint one later. Around it, Buffett and Munger restate the durable system in some of its sharpest forms — diversification as "a protection against ignorance," change as the investor's enemy rather than his opportunity, float that must above all be gotten *cheap*, capital that must carry a price, and the parable that you should never reach for an extra eighth of a percent. Per the wiki's two-layer convention, a short distilled top of the durable themes precedes a **comprehensive Q&A digest** that captures every value-bearing exchange.

## How to read the Q&A digest

- **★** marks a timeless, load-bearing exchange worth citing in the future; unmarked entries still carry investor value but are secondary or period-specific.
- Answers are attributed separately to **Buffett** and **Munger** — a Munger line appears only when he added a distinct lesson (he answered "Nothing to add" to most). This keeps the wisdom fairly credited to both.
- **M#/A#** map to the numbered items in the raw morning/afternoon transcript for traceability. The morning's items M3–M24, excluding M16 which is broken out below, are the Class B issuance discussion (folded into one entry); the general Q&A runs M25–M45 and A1–A41.

## Key themes

### Engineer the offering to repel the wrong owner — and want the price *fair*, not high

The Class B issuance exists to defuse promoters' "unit trusts" that would have parceled Berkshire into thousand-dollar pieces and sold a never-repeatable past record to "uninformed purchasers." Buffett's counter is a study in owner-orientation: he set an underwriting spread "about as low as any I've ever seen," promised "we would issue as many shares as people wanted to buy" so there could be no hot-issue scarcity, and stated flatly in the prospectus that the stock was *not* undervalued — because "we would not sell a part of your interest in Berkshire at a price which we did not feel was adequate for the present shareholders." The governing philosophy: "We really feel the fairer, the better," because the goal is for "every shareholder [to] participate in the progress that Berkshire makes … during their holding period" rather than one party getting wealthy off another. The economist's reflex he teaches alongside it — "whenever somebody tells you something … the first question to ask yourself is, 'And then what?'" A high stock price is not an end, because "the next question is, 'And then what?'" See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

### Diversification is a protection against ignorance — three wonderful businesses beat fifty

Asked about portfolio construction, Buffett gives the meeting's most-quoted line: "Diversification is a protection against ignorance. … if you don't know how to analyze businesses … [it] is a perfectly sound approach." But for someone who *can* value businesses, "it's crazy to own 50 stocks," because "there aren't that many wonderful businesses that are understandable to a single human being." The counterintuitive payoff is that concentration is *safer*: "there is less risk in owning three easy-to-identify, wonderful businesses than there is in owning 50 well-known, big businesses." Munger's gloss is unsparing — much of what is taught in modern finance is "twaddle," a "type of dementia I just can't even classify." The deeper point is that fortunes are *built*, not diversified into: "they were built by someone who identified with a wonderful business." See [concentration-vs-diversification](../concepts/concentration-vs-diversification.md).

### Change is the enemy, not the opportunity — buy predictability

Pressed again on great tech franchises, Buffett inverts Wall Street's instinct: "we feel change is likely to work against us. We do not … think we have great ability to predict where change is going to lead." So Berkshire hunts for "businesses where we don't think change is going to be very important" — Gillette, Coca-Cola, See's, "the art of the chew" — and avoids the ones where "the whole industry's going to change in five years," not because they're bad but because the future is unknowable. Microsoft is "a sensational company run by the best of managers," yet "we don't have any idea what that world is going to look like in 10 or 20 years," and Wall Street's pitch that change *is* the opportunity "scares the hell out of us." The corollary (M32, A17): Berkshire's plain businesses are "way easier to predict" than the Dow 30, and the discipline is to "bet on the simple things" rather than "trying to beat people at their own game, where we're not very good at the game." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

### Float: get it growing, but above all get it cheap

Buffett's clearest spoken float lesson runs through the bank-deposit analogy — policyholders pay in advance and losses settle slowly, so "we have, in effect, something that is tantamount to the deposits of a bank," except the true cost isn't known "until all your … losses have all been settled." For 29 years Berkshire's float has cost nothing on average, but "Float, per se, is not a blessing" — plenty of insurers generated it and "lost so much money in underwriting that they'd be better off if they'd never heard of the insurance business." The job is threefold: "get it, get it in increasing quantities, but above all, get it cheap," and you do it only "through having some kind of competitive advantages." The afternoon's thought experiment (A11) makes the growth point unforgettable: Buffett would *not* swap $7B of float for $7B of tax-free cash plus a permanent non-compete, "because I expect the 7 billion to grow." See [float](../concepts/float.md), [cost-of-float](../concepts/cost-of-float.md).

### Capital must carry a price — and the cure for absentee thinking is to *become* an owner

Berkshire charges its wholly-owned managers "something in the area of 15 percent" for capital and pays them the same to release it, not because the rate is precise but because it gets their attention and stops them treating retained earnings as free. Buffett's indictment of conventional pay: "a fixed-price option for 10 years — imagine giving somebody an interest-free loan for 10 years," priced off a market quote so that "the more depressed your market price be, the better your option price be," which "does not make any sense" — a fair option would start at intrinsic value and *step up* with a carrying cost. Munger's "more extreme system" cuts through all of it: Berkshire's executives simply "buy Berkshire Hathaway stock in the market for cash" — "I don't know why it doesn't spread more." Buffett: "People say they want their management to think like shareholders. … It's very easy to think like a shareholder. Become one." See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md), [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md).

### Never reach for an extra eighth of a percent

The meeting's sharpest risk parable (A22) is about *where the cash waits*. Berkshire accepts only "four or five approved names" of commercial paper and otherwise sits in Treasurys, because of Penn Central: a company paying a quarter-point extra that "showed a positive net worth … of a billion and a half" but "had a lot of net worth but no cash. … Turned out cash was more important." The lesson generalizes into an asymmetry rule: "if you'd … apply yourself to getting a quarter of a point extra … and you didn't make any mistake until you got to Penn Central, you would … be behind." Buffett's verdict — "I don't like a business that you can do right for 300 years and then make one mistake and be behind." See [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md), [risk-vs-volatility](../concepts/risk-vs-volatility.md).

### A great business needs no great management — but predictability plus a Tom Murphy is the jackpot

"The really great business is one that doesn't require good management. … And the poor business is one that can only succeed, or even survive, with great management." CEO quality is wildly uneven — "you get a lot of mediocrity, too" — so Buffett bets on the demonstrated record ("we like people that have batted .350 or .360") and is "very suspicious" of "banjo hitters who suddenly proclaim that they can become power hitters" with a new consultant. When a Tom Murphy (built Cap Cities only "when it made sense, not … for his ego") shows up in a decent business, "you want to bet very heavily." The afternoon's banking riff (A32) is the same idea from below: there is "no magic" to running a bank — Bank of Granite and Gene Abegg's Rockford bank earned double-industry returns with *less* leverage by avoiding "something foolish," "a little like investing." See [buffett-management-criteria](../synthesis/buffett-management-criteria.md), [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md).

## Q&A digest — Morning session

**★ M3–M24 · The Class B issuance, engineered to repel speculators.** *Buffett:* created the B to head off promoters' "unit trusts" that would sell never-repeatable past performance to "uninformed purchasers" in thousand-dollar lots; deliberately set "about as low" an underwriting spread "as any I've ever seen," promised to "issue as many shares as people wanted" so it "couldn't be a hot stock," and stated in the prospectus that the stock is *not* undervalued (M7) — "we would not sell a part of your interest in Berkshire at a price which we did not feel was adequate for the present shareholders." The B raises book value but "does not mean it increases the intrinsic value per share" (M22); "the facts are out regarding what we do," so informed buyers can value it themselves (M23). Money is fungible and need not be matched to opportunities — "one of the most important disciplines … is to not … coordinate your actions simply with the availability of cash" (M9). *Munger:* even a "wild" 3% issuance "is a non-event for us … you're also taking in a billion-odd dollars." See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md), [stock-as-acquisition-currency](../concepts/stock-as-acquisition-currency.md).

**M16 · Berkshire can't match its past returns.** *Buffett:* "Money just won't compound at that rate in this world, absent extraordinary inflation"; the harder discipline "is to make sure that we don't do something when we don't find something that makes sense." A standing expectation-setting that recurs all afternoon (A41). See [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md).

**★ M25 · Berkshire is worth more than its breakup value.** *Buffett:* valuing Berkshire as a static sum of divisions sold-and-taxed is the recurring error — as wrong as valuing GE that way; intrinsic value is "the stream of cash over … all of the years in the future, discounted back at an appropriate interest rate." The insurance business bought from Jack Ringwalt for ~$8.7M now carries ~$7B of float; "it would've been a big mistake to think in terms of the book value of that business being representative of its actual value." See [intrinsic-value-vs-book-value](../concepts/intrinsic-value-vs-book-value.md), [float](../concepts/float.md).

**★ M26 · Buybacks at prices that only *look* high.** *Buffett:* repurchasing above intrinsic value harms holders just as issuing below it does — "that's a truism" — but the number that matters is intrinsic value, not "book value or P/E ratios … anytime anybody gives you some simplified formula … forget it." Coke's repurchases at apparent high prices added per-share value; Berkshire's GEICO stake rose from ~33% to ~50% through buybacks "usually … at at least double book value," and "GEICO wasn't an insurance company … comparable to other insurance companies." See [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md), [coca-cola](../entities/coca-cola.md).

**M28 · Banking has no secret formulas.** *Buffett:* Wells Fargo's lead in supermarket branches and online banking is real, but "in the banking business, anything you do, your competitors can copy"; the edge is the temporary advantage of being first and learning fastest, not a durable secret like Coke's 7X formula. See [wells-fargo](../entities/wells-fargo.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**★ M29 · Why GEICO is better off wholly owned.** *Buffett:* freed from "pressure … in respect to reported earnings," GEICO can spend freely to bring on new business, since Berkshire "care[s] not at all about reported quarterly earnings"; the moat is the "low-cost method of distribution," and management is "focused on bringing costs down even further and widening that competitive moat." *Hindsight:* the 100% purchase proved one of Buffett's best — GEICO's growth ran far ahead of this measured forecast. See [geico](../entities/geico.md).

**M30 · Most owned businesses are carried far below worth.** *Buffett:* See's, bought for an effective $25M in 1972 earning $4M pretax, earned over $50M pretax in 1995; Buffalo News, bought for ~$30M making nothing, now earns ~$45M. Book value is "a guide to movement," not a multiplier you can stick a number on. See [sees-candies](../entities/sees-candies.md), [buffalo-evening-news](../entities/buffalo-evening-news.md).

**★ M32 · World Book won't be sold — and Berkshire won't beat technologists at their game.** *Buffett:* electronic competition and collapsing print unit-sales have gutted the encyclopedia business, and Berkshire is "groping a bit" for a workable cost structure, but won't sell it; the meta-lesson is the limit — "it's very unlikely that Charlie and I are going to be smarter than the rest of the world, in terms of the electronic world … we're not trying to beat people at their own game, where we're not very good at the game." *Munger:* "We don't have any way of avoiding declines in some of our businesses some of the time. Blue Chip Stamps once sold stamps at the rate of $120 million [a] year. Now, it's about $200,000." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [scott-fetzer](../entities/scott-fetzer.md).

**★ M34 · "The fairer, the better."** *Buffett:* unlike managements who want the stock as high as possible, "We really feel the fairer, the better … we don't want one party getting wealthy off the other"; the goal is the price tracking intrinsic value so each owner gets "their fair share of the pie." And the conserving question: "whenever somebody tells you something … the first question to ask yourself is, 'And then what?'" Owners in aggregate "cannot come out … better than the businesses come out," minus frictional costs. See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md), [investment-friction](../concepts/investment-friction.md).

**★ M35 · Float: "above all, get it cheap."** *Buffett:* the bank-deposit analogy — money paid in advance plus slow-settling losses — with the catch that the true cost is unknowable "until all your … losses have all been settled." Berkshire's float has cost ~nothing across 29 years and, crucially, has *grown*: "17 million of free money is worth something … Having seven billion … is worth a lot." But "Float, per se, is not a blessing"; the job is to "get it cheap" via "some kind of competitive advantages," because "the ordinary insurance company is not a good business." See [float](../concepts/float.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**M38 · Look-through earnings skipped this year.** *Buffett:* omitted only because the Cap Cities/Disney swap and the GEICO consolidation made the adjustments "a mess"; the $2B-by-2000 goal stands and the concept "is a very important concept" that returns next year. See [look-through-earnings](../concepts/look-through-earnings.md).

**★ M39 · The discount rate: government-bond rate, no risk premium.** *Buffett:* discount owner earnings at "the long-term government rate" (perhaps a touch higher when rates look unusually low), and do *not* bolt on a risk premium — "the purity of the idea is that you're discounting future cash," whose value "doesn't make any difference whether [it] comes from a risky business or a safe business." Risk is handled by *understanding* (you may "decide you can't estimate it at all") and by buying at "a significant discount," not by "playing games with numbers": adding "an extra 6 percent in on the interest rate … may look mathematical. But it's mathematical gibberish." *Munger:* "The discounts were once greater than we now see." See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [margin-of-safety](../concepts/margin-of-safety.md).

**M41 · GEICO's price: "We gulped a few times and paid it."** *Munger:* couldn't have bought many shares in the open market; "the price we paid, with the large number of shares we got, … was a very satisfactory price." *Buffett:* confirms — paid a full price for a terrific company and expects to be glad. See [geico](../entities/geico.md).

**M42 · Newspapers: still fine, but a notch weaker.** *Buffett:* single-paper towns were once "as attractive, economically, as any business you could find"; circulation-per-household trends and pricing power have "weakened a bit," and third-class mail and electronic information "eat away a little bit," but it's "still a very fine business" — you'd "still be a lot better off than owning virtually any other business." (Fold with A10.) See [media-economics](../concepts/media-economics.md).

**★ M43 · Read for "outside information" — competitors' reports, not Wall Street's.** *Buffett:* the report he wants is one a candid partner would give "if I owned half of a company but was away for a year"; to know how a business is doing he reads "the reports for the other eight" competitors, because "I can't be an intelligent owner of a business unless I know what all the other businesses … are doing." "I don't think we've ever gotten an idea … in 40 years from a Wall Street report. But we've gotten a lot of ideas from annual reports." *Munger:* even a simple business "takes a long time to read the annual report … if you really are trying to understand it, it's not a bit easy." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md).

**★ M44 · Berkshire is "not a one-man show" — the succession architecture.** *Buffett:* it's "a two-man show, in terms of capital allocation," over businesses "doing an outstanding job [without] any guidance"; after him the role splits — "someone in charge of investments and capital allocation" (he names Lou Simpson's position) and "someone in charge of operations," the model GEICO already runs with Nicely (underwriting) and Simpson (investments). Identifying managers and keeping them happy "doesn't require 150 IQ … [but] a certain sensitivity to why people want to get up in the morning." *Munger:* the assets "have been lovingly put together, so as not to require continuing intelligence at headquarters"; a successor likely won't match Buffett at new investments — "Well, that's just too damn bad." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [lou-simpson](../people/lou-simpson.md). *(Succession was later resolved very differently — Ajit Jain and Greg Abel, not a single investments/operations pair named here.)*

## Q&A digest — Afternoon session

**A2 · American Express has "slipped" — but the franchise holds; and Gates as manager.** *Buffett:* AmEx "may have taken their customer a little bit for granted," and credit cards are "a very different competitive struggle now"; it "needs to establish special value for its card … or it gets more commodity-like," but "they've got a strong franchise." On Gates: "one of the great managers of all time … an exceptional business talent who loves his business." *(AmEx recovered strongly and remained a core permanent holding — the "slipped" worry was not borne out.)* See [american-express](../entities/american-express.md).

**★ A3 · A great business needs no great management — but management quality is wildly uneven.** *Buffett:* "The really great business is one that doesn't require good management … the poor business is one that can only succeed … with great management." Fortune-500 CEOs "are not selected like … the American Olympic track and field team," so "you get a lot of mediocrity"; bet on the demonstrated record (".350 or .360" hitters), distrust "banjo hitters who suddenly proclaim that they can become power hitters" with a new consultant. Tom Murphy built Cap Cities "only … when it made sense, not … for his ego"; when you find that in a decent business, "bet very heavily." See [buffett-management-criteria](../synthesis/buffett-management-criteria.md), [tom-murphy](../people/tom-murphy.md).

**★ A4 · "Diversification is a protection against ignorance."** *Buffett:* sound for someone who can't analyze businesses, but "crazy to own 50 stocks" for someone who can, since "there aren't that many wonderful businesses … understandable to a single human being"; "three wonderful businesses is more than you need in this life," and "there is less risk in owning three easy-to-identify, wonderful businesses than … 50 well-known, big businesses." Fortunes "were built by someone who identified with a wonderful business." *Munger:* "much of what is taught in modern corporate finance courses is twaddle … a type of dementia I just can't even classify." *Buffett:* the high priests "wouldn't have any edge over the laypeople. And that never sells well." See [concentration-vs-diversification](../concepts/concentration-vs-diversification.md).

**A5 · Bloat is the real disease, not downsizing.** *Buffett:* society gains from more output per unit of labor, though it's "very difficult on the individual"; Berkshire hopes to grow output and keep people, but "a lot of the mistakes … being corrected now are because … businesses got very fat." *Munger:* "name a business that has been ruined because it was over-downsized. I cannot think of a single one"; but ruined "by bloat … I could just rattle off name after name." See [institutional-imperative](../concepts/institutional-imperative.md).

**★ A6 · No premium-volume budgets — wait for the fat pitch.** *Buffett:* a flood of new reinsurance capital is negative because that capital "will get employed" — operators "won't just sit there"; Berkshire promises its insurance staff "we will never have layoffs because of a drop in volume," precisely so no one feels "some internal compulsion to keep writing business." "If we had a budget for premium volume … it would be the dumbest thing we could do, because they would meet the budget." Like investing — "you have to wait until you get the fat pitch." See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md), [institutional-imperative](../concepts/institutional-imperative.md).

**★ A7 · Focus is the asset — GEICO abroad would be a distraction.** *Buffett:* with ~2.5% of a 100M-policyholder market, "there is such an opportunity here that it would be diversionary" to send GEICO overseas; Coke and Gillette, having earlier "lost that focus" and "fooling around with … diversification," recovered "tens of billions of dollars' worth" of value by refocusing. "I love focused management." *Munger:* Berkshire is "indirectly in all of these emerging markets through Coca-Cola and Gillette … one can argue that we, thereby, do it a lot better." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [roberto-goizueta](../people/roberto-goizueta.md).

**★ A9 · USAir is better, but "still … a mistake."** *Buffett:* the preferred "looks considerably better than it did 18 months ago," but the "costs are … relics of a regulated, protected environment" and either USAir fixes them "or those problems will … correct them"; "we would've been a lot better off if I'd just … gone out to a bar that night." *Munger:* "It's, plainly, worth a lot more than it was last year." *(Supersession: the recovery continued — the USAir preferred was ultimately redeemed at a profit, so the "mistake" was rescued by events, not by the airline's economics.)* See [usair](../entities/usair.md), [turnarounds-seldom-turn](../concepts/turnarounds-seldom-turn.md).

**A10 · Newspapers will evolve, not vanish.** *Buffett:* "very, very unlikely" newspapers go the way of Blue Chip Stamps; they may shift the revenue mix but remain "a bargain" to readers and advertisers — "it just doesn't have the lock that it used to have." (Fold with M42.) See [media-economics](../concepts/media-economics.md).

**★ A11 · Why $7B of float beats $7B of cash.** *Buffett:* he would *not* trade the float for $7B tax-free plus a permanent insurance non-compete — "not because I would rather have 7 billion of float than 7 billion of … free money. It's because I expect the 7 billion to grow." Making that trade at $17M in 1967 (or $300M a decade ago) "would've been a terrible mistake." *Munger:* on Buffett admitting he'd once have taken the deal — "he keeps learning. That's one of his tricks." See [float](../concepts/float.md), [cost-of-float](../concepts/cost-of-float.md).

**A12 · Freddie Mac and the "convexity problem."** *Buffett:* lending where the borrower "has the option of calling off the deal tomorrow or retaining it for 30 years … is a very disadvantageous contract to enter into" — the asymmetric "convexity problem" — and clever liability structuring (callable debt) only mitigates it: "you can't address a problem like that totally. There is no way to set up some model that satisfies that entire risk." *Munger:* "It's probably slightly more risky, but I don't think they're taking horrible risks. It's still a very good business." See [model-risk](../concepts/model-risk.md), [lending-discipline](../concepts/lending-discipline.md).

**★ A13 · Don't play mortician waiting for a flu epidemic.** *Buffett:* if you've truly identified a great company — "by definition … one that's going to remain great for 30 years" — "I think it's better just to own them" rather than "sit there and hope that you buy them in the throes of some panic … like a mortician … waiting for a flu epidemic." Bargains from panic (AmEx 1964, GEICO 1976) help "if you happen to come into some added money" then, "but that's a lot to count on." Imagine no stock market: if you like the business, the person, and the price, "take it and don't worry about how it's quoted." See [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md), [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md).

**★ A14 · "Change is likely to work against us."** *Buffett:* Berkshire seeks businesses "where we don't think change is going to be very important" — Gillette will out-spend everyone on shaving systems and owns the distribution and credibility; "we don't really see a lot of technology going into the art of the chew." Microsoft is "a sensational company run by the best of managers," but "we don't have any idea what that world is going to look like in 10 or 20 years," and Wall Street's claim that change is "a great opportunity … scares the hell out of us." *Munger:* "If you have something you think you understand that looks very attractive … it's smart to do what you understand. … We've been unable to find companies that fit our slender talents." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [gillette](../entities/gillette.md).

**A15 · Disclose no more about holdings than required.** *Buffett:* the portfolio line labeled "Others" is deliberate — "we have no interest in people … generat[ing] investment ideas" from the Berkshire report, and none in tipping "where our buying power may be"; "we will keep raising the cutoff level." About half the press reports of Berkshire's trades are wrong, "and again, we leave to you the fun of figuring out which half." See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**A16 · Y2K isn't a headquarters problem.** *Buffett:* "I'll let the people who run the operating businesses work on that. And I'll work on capital allocation." *Munger:* "few companies could handle a big computer snafu better than we could" — the Coke stock, the Gillette stock, the furniture would all still be there. The transferable point: stay in businesses "that are fairly simple and that can't get all messed up." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ A17 · Berkshire's businesses are "way easier to predict."** *Buffett:* contrary to the questioner's worry, candy, furniture, jewelry and shoes are "far easier to predict than most businesses" — easier than the Dow 30 or the first 100 NYSE names — because they're "fundamental things, fairly simple," with a slow rate of change. He expects "one surprise" over five years, "but there won't be a series of them." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [franchise-vs-business](../concepts/franchise-vs-business.md).

**★ A18 · The manager *is* the asset at Disney — "if you own the mouse, you own the mouse."** *Buffett:* Eisner "has been the 'Walt Disney,' … by far the most important factor in Disney's success"; the franchise is "a share of mind" with billions of children, recyclable "kind of like having an oil field … [that] all seeps back in over seven or eight years." *Munger:* the simple arithmetic — "a billion children … $10 per year per child … gets into very large numbers"; "mine want to see Disney … over and over and over again." *(Supersession: the effusive Eisner bet aged poorly — Berkshire exited Disney within a few years and Eisner was forced out in 2005; the franchise endured, the manager did not.)* See [disney](../entities/disney.md).

**★ A19 · The institution vs. the individual — Mayo Clinic vs. the brain surgeon.** *Buffett:* buy a brain surgeon's practice and "the next day … it's really him and not you … producing the earnings"; buy the Mayo Clinic and "the institution has the power," an asset "independent of the attitude of any one person … the next day." Wall Street is a volatile mix of both, and "there aren't many" institutional-character firms in it — which is what makes Salomon hard to own and predict. See [salomon](../entities/salomon.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**★ A20 · A screwy corner of the market is no concern.** *Buffett:* NASDAQ names at "10 times revenues … 30 times revenues" don't matter — "there have always been … thousands of cases of things … ridiculously priced, and phony stock promotions … It always will go on. And it doesn't make any difference to us." Berkshire doesn't predict markets and would happily own Coke or Gillette even "if they said … we'll open it again in 20 years"; shorting even "obvious frauds" is "very tough to make money" doing. See [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md), [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

**A21 · The business matters more than the domicile.** *Buffett:* "we feel that the important thing is the business, not the domicile" — he'd love Coke "if it were domiciled in England" — though a "tiny bit" more comfort comes from understanding home-country tax, politics and governance; would buy a foreign business "with a market cap … of at least $5 billion or greater" that fit. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [look-through-earnings](../concepts/look-through-earnings.md).

**★ A22 · Never reach for an extra eighth of a percent.** *Buffett:* only "four or five approved names" of commercial paper, the rest in Treasurys, because of Penn Central — positive reported net worth but "no cash. … Turned out cash was more important." A century of clipping a quarter-point extra is wiped out by one Penn Central: "I don't like a business that you can do right for 300 years and then make one mistake and be behind." See [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md), [risk-vs-volatility](../concepts/risk-vs-volatility.md).

**A23 · Volatile earnings are intrinsic to Wall Street firms.** *Buffett:* Salomon marks to market, so its earnings swing; had Berkshire run its own marks through income over 30 years, "you would see enormous volatility … a number of quarters of losses … some great upsurges." What counts is "running it so that the volatility never kills you" and earning "a decent return on equity over time." *Munger:* it's "illogical for the credit rating agencies to mark down Salomon as much as they do because the earnings are volatile … But it's their game." See [salomon](../entities/salomon.md).

**A24 · The subsidiaries barely interact.** *Buffett:* cross-selling between Berkshire units is "very, very, very little" — "they're doing fine running their own operations"; managers know what businesses Berkshire owns and "can always go directly," but "they don't need me to communicate." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**A25 · Lloyd's troubles widened Berkshire's reinsurance moat.** *Buffett:* Lloyd's lost luster as the "first stop and … last stop for all kinds of unusual risks," and "the fact that they have lost some of their luster … has helped us"; Berkshire "probably possesses more capital than all of Lloyd's put together" and a reputation "for being willing to quote on very large risks very quickly and to do exactly what it says" — "a preeminent position … difficult for anyone else to replicate." See [super-cat-insurance](../concepts/super-cat-insurance.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**★ A26 · "We assume we'll be around forever."** *Buffett:* unlike companies "thinking about what little pictures they can paint for the next four quarters," Berkshire assumes it will "be here to pay every claim," so "there wouldn't be any sense [playing] games on accounting because it would catch up with us later"; it must run "as if, in the year 2050 … somebody's going to look and say, 'How'd it work out?'" Wall Street "really doesn't seem to learn … business lessons," because selling the future pays better than betting your life on it. See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md), [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md).

**★ A27 · Charge for capital — and the real fix is to *become* an owner.** *Buffett:* the best businesses "take … essentially no capital," but where capital is produced Berkshire charges "something in the area of 15 percent" rather than running a capital-budgeting bureaucracy. A ten-year fixed-price option is "an interest-free loan," made perverse when "the more depressed your market price … the better your option price"; a fair option would start "at not less than … intrinsic value" and step up with a carrying cost. *Munger:* Berkshire's "more extreme system" — executives "buy Berkshire Hathaway stock in the market for cash … most of them have done it. … I don't know why it doesn't spread more." *Buffett:* "It's very easy to think like a shareholder. Become one." See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md), [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md).

**A28 · Disney makes money for *shareholders* — and Eisner's circle.** *Buffett:* most movie companies "make money for everybody associated with it, but not a lot has stuck to the shareholders"; Disney is the exception, and the worry with any manager (Eisner included) is "getting out of our circle of competence … like a duck floating on a pond … you think it's you and not the rain." *Munger:* the film business "reminds me of an oil company … 'If they ever do find any oil, that old man will steal it'" — "only about half of it … has normal commercial morals." See [disney](../entities/disney.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ A29 · Books, the "three ideas," and a margin of safety you can see without a calculator.** *Buffett:* recommends Fisher's first two books and, "first and foremost," Graham's *The Intelligent Investor*, chapters 8 and 20 — investing has "only about three ideas": treat a stock as owning a business, hold the right attitude toward market movements (Ch. 8), and demand a margin of safety (Ch. 20) — "don't try and drive a 9,800-pound truck over a bridge that says … 'Capacity: 10,000 pounds.'" *Munger:* "Warren talks about these discounted cash flows. I've never seen him do one" — "if it isn't pluperfect obvious … he tends to go on to the next idea." *Buffett:* "it ought to just kind of scream at you that you've got this huge margin of safety." See [margin-of-safety](../concepts/margin-of-safety.md), [ben-graham](../people/ben-graham.md), [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md).

**★ A30 · More to come in insurance — sold on peace of mind, not price.** *Buffett:* just bought tiny Kansas Bankers Surety and expanded structured settlements, where Berkshire is "the preferred provider" — annuities for catastrophically injured people who need to know "that that check will be in the mail 50 years from now." "We don't obtain the competitive advantage by price … from the peace of mind." See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md), [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md).

**★ A32 · "No magic" to banking — just don't do something foolish.** *Buffett:* the Bank of Granite earned ~2.58% on assets with a 33% efficiency ratio at ~$400–500M of assets, and Gene Abegg's Rockford bank earned 2% on assets "with way less leverage than anyone else and lower loan losses" — neither needed scale or special laws, "just … doing the right things day after day." Wells Fargo "has done an exceptionally good job," and an in-market merger often "just causes a bank to do what they should've done anyway." "It's a little like investing … you just have to avoid doing things that are ungodly dumb." See [lending-discipline](../concepts/lending-discipline.md), [wells-fargo](../entities/wells-fargo.md).

**A33 · No growth targets — "whatever comes along that makes sense."** *Buffett:* acquisitions doubled revenue only because GEICO, R.C. Willey and Helzberg's landed off a small base; "it's not any goal of ours to double the revenues or increase them 20 percent … If there's a lot that … makes sense, we'll do a lot. And if there's nothing … we'll do nothing." See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md).

**A34 · GEICO's Lou Simpson has a longer leash now.** *Buffett:* freed from standalone capital ratios, Simpson "has the ability to do whatever he wants" with GEICO's ~$5B — even going fully into equities — because Berkshire can backstop policyholders "by quota share reinsurance … [or] just guarantee their obligations." "There are very few people that I will let run money … but … in the case of Lou … that's one in a thousand." *Munger:* "That's a very shrewd question." See [geico](../entities/geico.md), [lou-simpson](../people/lou-simpson.md).

**★ A35 · Permanent holdings stay permanent — even if the market goes crazy.** *Buffett:* "there are things that we think there's no price for," though Gates is right that "at some price, you have to be willing to sell"; Berkshire would "rather just sit and hold the business and pretend the stock market doesn't exist," a mindset that "has worked out way better than I would've predicted." *Munger:* the early-'70s two-tier market hurt mainly people who held things "called tier one when they really weren't … If you're right about the companies, you can hold them at pretty high values." See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md), [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md).

**★ A37 · Not every "float" business is attractive.** *Buffett:* Berkshire likes float but is "not interested in buying the typical insurance business, because … the float will end up costing us too much"; better to "borrow money with an explicit cost … rather than have the implicit costs of an underwriting loss." The standing bias is "toward things that throw off cash." *Munger:* "if we go into the pre-need funeral home business, that'll be the day." See [float](../concepts/float.md), [cost-of-float](../concepts/cost-of-float.md).

**A39 · Corporate returns on equity will likely fall.** *Buffett:* the ~12–13% he wrote of in the 1970s has run nearer 20%, surprising him; some is accounting (post-retirement-benefit reserves shrinking the equity denominator, "big-bath" write-offs) and some real (buybacks at Coke and Wells "had a huge effect"), but "under any system of accounting, the 20 percent returns for American industry are [not] in the cards." *Munger:* "a whole lot of things have combined to goose the results … I don't see how it could go much farther." *(Period-bound call: aggregate corporate ROE stayed elevated and at times rose further over the following decades.)* See [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md), [return-on-equity-yardstick](../concepts/return-on-equity-yardstick.md).

**★ A41 · Past growth is not the yardstick for new investments.** *Buffett:* Berkshire bought competitive retailers (Helzberg's, R.C. Willey) earning well above the industry's 8–9% on equity, but "we have no way of making 23.6 percent in the future. So we do not use our historical … average as a yardstick … we would make no new investments" otherwise. Retail is "a very tough business," bought only for its records, positions and managers. See [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md), [helzbergs](../entities/helzbergs.md).

## Connections to other sources

- The Class B issuance is the most concrete expression yet of the no-split, protect-the-shareholder-base policy enunciated in [berkshire-1994-meeting](berkshire-1994-meeting.md) (M15/M25) and [berkshire-1995-meeting](berkshire-1995-meeting.md) (A50); the 1996 prospectus operationalizes it by *pricing and distributing* an offering to repel the wrong owners, and the [berkshire-1996-letter](berkshire-1996-letter.md) makes the same case in print.
- "Diversification is a protection against ignorance" (A4) is the sharpest spoken form of the concentration argument run through [berkshire-1994-meeting](berkshire-1994-meeting.md) (M61, A11) and earlier — adding the counterintuitive claim that three understood businesses are *safer* than fifty.
- The float teaching (M35, A11, A37) extends [berkshire-1994-meeting](berkshire-1994-meeting.md) (M14) and [berkshire-1995-meeting](berkshire-1995-meeting.md) (M23, A22); the "$7B float vs. $7B tax-free cash" refusal is a new device for showing that float's value is in its *growth*.
- The 15%-capital-charge and "become an owner" material (A27) builds directly on "money costs money" from [berkshire-1995-meeting](berkshire-1995-meeting.md) (M7), here extended into the critique of fixed-price options as interest-free loans.
- The USAir verdict (A9) continues the post-mortem of [berkshire-1994-meeting](berkshire-1994-meeting.md) (M40/A13) and [berkshire-1995-meeting](berkshire-1995-meeting.md) (M11/A48); by 1996 the preferred "looks considerably better," foreshadowing its eventual profitable redemption.
- "Change is likely to work against us" (A14) and "way easier to predict" (A17) sharpen the franchise-durability and circle-of-competence reasoning of [berkshire-1995-meeting](berkshire-1995-meeting.md) (M25, M33), reframing the circle as a preference for a *low rate of change* rather than merely familiarity.
- The Disney/Eisner enthusiasm (A18, A28) is new to the meetings — arriving with the Cap Cities/Disney swap referenced in [berkshire-1996-letter](berkshire-1996-letter.md) — and reads in hindsight as a supersession warning: the franchise endured, but Berkshire exited and Eisner was forced out, a caution that manager-dependent bets carry their own risk.
