# Berkshire Hathaway 1990 Annual Letter

The 1990 letter is the canonical source for four frameworks that recur in every subsequent letter: the **cost-of-float measurement** as the correct scorecard for insurance business quality; the **food-prices analogy** as the definitive statement of why ongoing buyers should welcome — not fear — lower prices; the **super-cat concentration thesis** (a lumpy 15% beats a smooth 12%); and the **junk-bond anatomy** as the sharpest illustration of what goes wrong when capital structures ignore margin of safety. The Wells Fargo accumulation provides the live case study tying all four together: panic-driven prices, management quality immune to the institutional imperative, and disciplined tolerance of near-term uncertainty for long-run advantage. The letter also formalizes look-through earnings for the first time, giving shareholders a tool to see through accounting conventions to economic ownership.

## Key themes

### Float cost, not combined ratio, is the right scorecard for insurance

Buffett introduces in this letter the measurement framework that becomes the recurring Berkshire insurance scorecard: compare annual underwriting losses to average float to derive the effective **cost of funds** the insurance operation generates, then compare that cost to the long-term government bond yield. A low cost of funds signifies a good business; a high cost translates into a poor one — identical logic to a bank paying more on deposits than it earns on loans.

The 1967-1990 table (introduced here for the first time) shows Berkshire developed funds at a cost below the U.S. government's in 19 of those 24 years. In 1990: underwriting loss $26.65M, average float $1.637B, cost of funds 1.63% versus a long-term government yield of 8.24%. Two qualifications Buffett notes: true cost won't be known until all loss reserves settle, and the tax penalty on indirect investment income adds roughly one percentage point. Even adjusted, the spread is substantial.

The conceptual ceiling is GEICO, at 48% Berkshire ownership: operating at an underwriting profit, GEICO's policyholders effectively pay the company interest on the float rather than the other way around.

### Falling prices are an ongoing buyer's ally

The canonical statement of volatility-as-opportunity appears in the Wells Fargo discussion. The reasoning is simple and often confused: if you plan to buy the same thing repeatedly over your lifetime — groceries, newsprint, businesses — then lower prices serve you, not hurt you. The confusion arises because investors conflate the seller's perspective (who dislikes low prices) with their own position as long-duration buyers.

> Knowing they are forever going to be buyers of food, they welcome falling prices and deplore price increases. … We will be buying businesses — or small parts of businesses, called stocks — year in, year out as long as I live. Given these intentions, declining prices for businesses benefit us, and rising prices hurt us.

Wells Fargo stock fell nearly 50% within a few months during 1990 on California real-estate fears. Buffett welcomed the decline: Berkshire accumulated 5 million shares at $289M — less than five times after-tax earnings — precisely because panic produced prices below his conservative worst-case scenario. Even if 10% of the bank's $48B in loans suffered 30% loss severity, the bank would roughly break even. "The most common cause of low prices is pessimism — some times pervasive, some times specific to a company or industry. We want to do business in such an environment, not because we like pessimism but because we like the prices it produces."

### Super-cat: concentrating known risk beats spreading unknown risk

Most insurers avoid super-catastrophe reinsurance because the result profile is binary: either roughly $100M profit (no major catastrophe) or $200M+ loss (a pair of major hurricanes or earthquakes). The combined ratio swings between near-zero and 300%, never in the comfortable 100-120 range most managements prefer to report. Berkshire's structural advantage is a shareholder base that understands the trade-off.

"Charlie and I always have preferred a lumpy 15% return to a smooth 12%." The financial strength to tolerate those swings — unmatched among U.S. insurers — is precisely what makes Berkshire the preferred counterparty for other insurers seeking protection. At the moment of maximum industry stress, weaker reinsurers cannot credibly promise to pay; Berkshire can.

The lesson generalizes: an investor who can tolerate variance that others cannot will earn a structurally higher long-run return, because she faces no peer competition for the most volatile assets.

### Banking leverage makes management quality non-negotiable

At 20:1 leverage — the common bank ratio — mistakes touching only a small fraction of assets can destroy most of the equity. That arithmetic converts management judgment from an important variable into the only variable that matters. Berkshire is not interested in purchasing a poorly-managed bank at a "cheap" price; their only interest is well-managed banks at fair prices.

What distinguishes Wells Fargo's Carl Reichardt and Paul Hazen: the same four characteristics as Tom Murphy and Dan Burke at Capital Cities — each pair stronger than the sum of its parts; they pay able people well but keep head count lean; they attack costs as vigorously at record profit levels as under pressure; they stay within what they understand. The institutional imperative — the lemming-like tendency of bank executives to follow peers regardless of how foolish the behavior — destroyed many California lenders in 1990; Wells Fargo visibly avoided it.

In a business selling a commodity-type product, it's impossible to be a lot smarter than your dumbest competitor. The USAir preferred, admitted here as an "unforced error," is the commodity-business counter-example to the Wells Fargo case.

### Junk bonds: the universe generating the historical data was not the universe being analyzed

Junk bonds were marketed using default histories from fallen angels — bonds originally issued at investment grade, then downgraded when issuers ran into trouble. The flawed inference: because fallen-angel bonds had low net default rates historically, newly-issued junk bonds (never investment grade to begin with) would perform similarly.

The universes were structurally different. A fallen-angel manager has an incentive to regain investment-grade status and works toward it. The junk-bond operator seeks the next deal, not solvency. Some capital structures were guaranteed to fail regardless of operating performance: interest charges on a Tampa TV station exceeded its gross revenues before any other expenses. "The roads of business are riddled with potholes; a plan that requires dodging them all is a plan for disaster."

"Beware of past-performance 'proofs' in finance: If history books were the key to riches, the Forbes 400 would consist of librarians." The constructive alternative, as ever: Ben Graham's three-word principle — Margin of Safety.

### Look-through earnings: accounting for the trees you own but don't cut

Reported earnings understate Berkshire's economic progress because GAAP records only dividends received from investees, not Berkshire's proportionate share of their retained earnings. In 1990, Capital Cities' retained earnings attributable to Berkshire's 17% stake exceeded $82M; only ~$530K (dividends received minus tax) appeared in reported income. Buffett's corrective: add proportionate investee retained earnings (~$250M), subtract the incremental taxes that would be owed (~$30M), and add the $220M remainder to reported operating earnings of $371M — yielding 1990 look-through earnings of roughly $590M.

"The way they are accounted for is of no importance, but their ownership and subsequent utilization is all-important. We care not whether the auditors hear a tree fall in the forest; we do care who owns the tree and what's next done with it." The principle: any accounting that obscures economic ownership requires the analyst to look through it.
