# Berkshire Hathaway 2022 Annual Meeting

The 2022 meeting — held April 30 in Omaha, the first in-person gathering since 2019 — is dominated by one remarkable quarter: between February 18 and roughly March 10, Berkshire deployed ~$40 billion in equities, acquiring ~14% of Occidental Petroleum, scaling up Chevron, and agreeing to buy Alleghany for $11.6 billion, all while the broader market had briefly become what Buffett calls "almost totally a casino, a gambling parlor." The gambling-parlor critique is the meeting's animating theme — Munger calls it an "almost a mania of speculation" and both men trace it through Keynes's General Theory chapter 12 — and the OXY buy serves as a live proof: rational buyers could acquire 14% of a major public company in two weeks precisely because the other participants were pulling handles, not analyzing businesses. The second great theme is productive versus nonproductive assets, crystallised in Buffett's bitcoin argument: he would not accept all the world's bitcoin for $25, because it produces nothing and therefore has no terminal value — a frame that generalises to any pure-greater-fool holding. A third thread is inflation: "it swindles almost everybody," the best protection is developing skills no one can take away, and the massive money creation of 2020–21 made the price surge nearly inevitable. All four principals — Buffett, Munger, Greg Abel, and Ajit Jain — are on stage.

## Key themes

### The market as gambling parlor — and what rational buyers do there

Buffett's central 2022 lecture: capital markets swing between investment-oriented and casino-dominated modes, and the 2020–2022 period went almost entirely casino. His proof is the OXY purchase: BlackRock, Vanguard, State Street, and Dodge & Cox together held ~40% of Occidental and never traded. That left 60% in speculative hands. In two weeks, one person at Berkshire — "30 feet away from me" — bought 14% of the whole company out of that 60%. "That's not investment," Buffett says flatly. "It defies anything that Charlie and I have seen." Munger: "We have computers with algorithms trading against other computers… people that know nothing about stocks being advised by stockbrokers who know even less." Buffett traces the structural driver back to Keynes's 1936 description of markets in chapter 12 of *The General Theory* and to the incentive arithmetic: "They make a lot more money when people are gambling than when they're investing." The episode also illustrates why Berkshire keeps large cash — not for timing, but to be "sane" when others are not. See [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md) and [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

### Speed, serendipity, and no bankers: the Q1 buying burst

Between January 1 and February 18, Berkshire spent $2.2 billion. Then in roughly three weeks it spent another ~$40 billion. The Alleghany deal ($11.6B) originated with a two-paragraph email Joe Brandon sent on February 25 — the day before Buffett mailed his annual letter confessing there was nothing to do. Buffett: "I didn't call up investment bankers and say, 'Will you prepare me a report?'… I knew we'd buy Alleghany at the price we offered." The Occidental position began because Buffett spent a weekend reading an analyst presentation from CEO Vicki Hollub: "What she was saying made nothing but sense." No advisors, no committee, no process — two weeks later Berkshire owned 14% of OXY. The pattern recurs across decades (National Indemnity in fifteen minutes; ISCAR via cold approach): the Berkshire edge is the combination of permanent capital, no bureaucratic lag, and a buyer sellers trust to close. See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

### Productive versus nonproductive assets: why bitcoin fails the ownership test

Buffett's clearest statement of the productive/nonproductive distinction. He offers to write a $25 billion check for 1% of all US farmland or 1% of all US apartment houses — both produce something (food, rent) and will produce more going forward. Then: "If you told me you owned all of the bitcoin in the world, and you offered it to me for $25, I wouldn't take it — because what would I do with it? I have to sell it back to you one way or another." Bitcoin produces nothing; its entire return depends on the next buyer paying more than the last. Munger's formulation is sharper: "In my life I try and avoid things that are stupid and evil and make me look bad in comparison with somebody else. And bitcoin does all three." The frame generalises: any asset whose return requires a greater fool rather than underlying production fails the ownership test — and the class includes gold (held mainly for its monetary narrative), though Buffett does not press that point here. The government-currency corollary is also stated: the US dollar is the only legal-tender money; no government will cede that function to a private protocol. See [investment-categories](../concepts/investment-categories.md).

### Inflation swindles almost everybody — and what survives it

Asked about the 7%+ inflation of early 2022, Buffett quotes the title of his own 1977 Fortune article ("How Inflation Swindles the Equity Investor") and extends it: "Inflation, I should say, swindles the bond investor too. And it swindles the person who keeps their cash under their mattress. It swindles almost everybody." The mechanism: capital-intensive businesses must reinvest at inflated replacement cost just to stand still, so higher earnings in nominal terms may represent lower real earnings. The best protection is not a financial asset class but a personal one — developing a skill so valuable that people will give you their production regardless of what the unit of exchange is worth: "If you're the best doctor in town… they can't take it away from you." On the monetary cause: the government effectively mailed trillions to households, and "it was almost impossible not to have inflation if you're going to mail out the kind of money we mailed out." Buffett credits Fed Chair Jay Powell as a hero for acting at speed in March 2020. See [bonds-and-inflation](../concepts/bonds-and-inflation.md).

### Cash as oxygen: the one commitment Berkshire will never break

Opening with the Q1 10-Q, Buffett flags that cash came down because of the $40B deployment but makes an unconditional pledge: "We will always have a lot of cash on hand." The metaphor: cash is "like oxygen, you know? It's there all the time. But if it disappears for a few minutes, it's all over." During 2008 Berkshire held no commercial paper, no money-market funds — only Treasury bills. The argument is asymmetric: the cost of holding excess cash is an opportunity cost measurable in forgone returns; the cost of running out is the inability to function when everyone else is unable to function. "There have been a few times in history, and there will be more times in history, where if you don't have it, you don't get to play the next day." This is not a liquidity buffer in the conventional risk-management sense; it is an unconditional operating principle. See [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md).

### The culture of lying: how earnings guidance cascades into fraud

Asked how to foster a long-horizon shareholder culture, Buffett pivots to a warning about what destroys corporate culture: publicly forecasting earnings. The cascade is inescapable — once you say "we'll earn $3.59," the accounting department finds a way to $3.59. "It's like taking $5 out of the cash register. The first time you'll say, 'I'm going to put it back.' Do it once and you probably never stop." The corruption compounds through succession: the new CEO inherits a culture of small adjustments and cannot admit it without indicting the predecessor. Analyst-day repetition turns targets into catechisms, and the catechism forecloses honest reassessment. "You just don't go in and say, 'We've really been cheating a little.'" Berkshire has never told any manager to change a figure. "Once you start lying, it's all over." The lesson applies to any organisation that anchors to public targets rather than underlying economics. See [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md), [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

### Share repurchases as compounding ownership — without writing a check

Buffett's most lucid 2022 exposition of why buybacks create value silently. In 1998 Berkshire owned 11.2% of American Express. It bought its last share in 1998 or so, has received dividends since — and now owns 20%. The ownership grew entirely because American Express repurchased shares from others: "We've done it without putting up any money." Apple is the running parallel — every quarter Apple earns more on fewer shares, and Berkshire's look-through earnings per share grow automatically. The farm analogy: "Imagine if you owned 640 acres, and 20 years later it had turned into 1,100 or 1,200 acres. You'd say, how long has this been going on?" The principle is simple — buying out a partner at an attractive price enriches the remaining partners — but requires the discipline to act only when the price is genuinely below intrinsic value. See [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md).

## Q&A digest — Morning session

**★ M6 · "Extreme aversion" to permanent loss.** *Buffett:* Q1 operating earnings ~$7 billion, which is "a real 7 billion." But the one commitment he can always make: "We wake up every morning and we want to be safer, in terms of your eventual investment… We have an extreme aversion to incurring any permanent loss with your funds." He would "die, psychologically" if Berkshire lost a lot of other people's money, and that feeling is a governance mechanism, not theater. See [berkshire-hathaway](../entities/berkshire-hathaway.md).

**M7 · "$40 billion in a hurry in three weeks."** *Buffett:* From January 1 to February 18 Berkshire spent $2.2 billion; in the following ~three weeks, ~$41 billion — executed by a single person in his office who "doesn't have an assistant." The table in the 10-Q shows the burst, then the return to "lethargic mood." The burst was purely opportunity-driven: things got cheap enough, fast enough, that the capital was deployed before the letter dated February 26 — confessing inaction — had even been received. See [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

**★ M8 · Cash as oxygen.** *Buffett:* "We will always have a lot of cash on hand" — not commercial paper, not money-market funds, Treasury bills. Cash is "like oxygen, you know? It's there all the time. But if it disappears for a few minutes, it's all over." Points to March 2020 as a near-repeat of 2008. Berkshire is sized to "be there" when others cannot play. *Munger:* "It's worked so far." See [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md).

**★ M9 · How a casual email led to Alleghany.** *Buffett:* On February 25 — the day before the letter confessing inaction — Joe Brandon (new Alleghany CEO, former Berkshire General Re executive) sent a short email passing along his first annual report. Buffett wrote back suggesting they meet in New York on March 7. "I may have said, 'I've got an idea.' Well, I didn't have that idea the day before." At dinner he named a price; Alleghany accepted; Berkshire wrote a check for ~$11 billion. No bankers, no RFP, no process: "If that email hadn't been sent, we would not have made an offer for Alleghany." See [joe-brandon](../people/joe-brandon.md), [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**★ M10 · The gambling-parlor critique and the OXY buy.** *Buffett:* Long analysis of how the market became "almost totally a casino" in 2020–2022. Four passive institutions (BlackRock, Vanguard, State Street, Dodge & Cox) held ~40% of Occidental and never traded; Berkshire bought 14% of the remaining 60% in two weeks. "That's not investment." The structural driver: brokers and algorithms make far more money on volume than on long-term holding, so the incentives select for gamblers. Traces to Keynes's *General Theory* chapter 12 (1936). *Munger:* "We've never had anything quite like what we have now in terms of volumes and pure gambling activity." Buffett's coda: "It doesn't take a high IQ. It just takes the right attitude." See [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md), [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md).

**M11 · International acquisitions — harder but not excluded.** *Buffett:* During the Q1 burst Berkshire spent ~$5 billion buying three German securities and rounded up its positions in Japan's five major trading houses (toward 5.85% each). Would buy a $10–20B European or Asian business in ten minutes if it fell into their lap the way Alleghany did — but the relationship-driven flow that generates those opportunities is harder to cultivate abroad. See [japanese-trading-houses](../entities/japanese-trading-houses.md).

**★ M12 · Share repurchases compound ownership without new capital.** *Buffett:* American Express example in full: 11.2% stake in 1998, no new shares purchased since, dividends collected — now 20% of the company. "We've done it without putting up any money." Apple parallel: every quarter Berkshire's look-through ownership grows as Apple reduces its share count. Farm analogy: 640 acres quietly becoming 1,100 acres. Munger's gloss: Berkshire's own ~$60B of repurchases "no overhead, no cost, no nothing, just more interest in what we already owned." See [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md), [american-express](../entities/american-express.md), [apple](../entities/apple.md).

**★ M13 · Splitting chairman and CEO is "the most ridiculous criticism" — Munger.** *Munger:* Index funds send letters demanding Buffett split himself in two, written by a "professor somewhere who has never run any business." "It would be like Odysseus coming back from Troy and some guy would say, 'I don't like the way you were holding your spear when you won that battle.'" *Buffett:* The letters violate the equal-information pledge in paragraph three of the annual letter — Berkshire treats its 3 million partners equally and will not hold private meetings for index fund governance teams. *Munger:* "You don't think a director is independent who needs $300,000 to be here? He's 'independent' the way a slave is independent." See [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md), [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**M14 · BNSF vs Union Pacific: improvements will take time.** *Abel:* Acknowledges Union Pacific's operating-ratio advantage (~400 bps) and Progressive's telematics edge over GEICO; refuses to trade BNSF's franchise for UP's. "We have a journey ahead of us and we're going to continue to get better." *Buffett:* Katie Farmer is "the perfect person" running BNSF; a 21,000-mile railroad cannot reorganise its track layout on a short timeline. The franchise is a hundred-year asset. See [bnsf](../entities/bnsf.md).

**M15 · GEICO adopting telematics to catch Progressive.** *Jain:* GEICO is ~2 years into telematics after Progressive spent 10–20 years building it. "The journey has started and the initial results are promising." Hopes GEICO catches up "in the next year or two" on telematics, which will then translate into both growth and margins. *Supersession flag:* By 2023–2024 GEICO posted severe underwriting losses and remained materially behind Progressive on both telematics penetration and combined ratio — the "next year or two" target was not met. See [geico](../entities/geico.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**M16 · State Farm refutes business-school incentive theory.** *Buffett:* State Farm — a mutual, where "nobody's gotten rich" — is the largest auto insurer in the US with ~$140B net worth, dwarfing Progressive's ~$20B. Yet business schools teach that only equity incentives produce competitive success. Progressive had extremely smart leadership (Peter Lewis, then successors) and still trails State Farm in size. The other lesson: Progressive's management was rigorous about underwriting but largely ignored the investment side, owning long-duration bonds when rates rose. "Blind spots." See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**★ M17 · "We haven't ever timed anything."** *Buffett:* Direct refutation of the market-timing attribution. "We haven't the faintest idea what the stock market is going to do when it opens on Monday. We never have had. We have never made a decision where either one of us has either said or been thinking, we should buy or sell based on what the market's going to do." In 2008 he spent ~$15–16 billion in the last few weeks before the bottom — "a really dumb time, as it turned out." In March 2020 he "totally missed" the low. What they do: figure out when they are "getting enough for our money," then buy as much as possible, hoping the price continues to fall so they can buy more. *Munger:* "No." (confirming he has never made a timing call either.) See [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**M18 · "Capitalism is very peculiar in how it dishes out rewards."** *Buffett:* Wall Street self-selects for those who want to maximize income per IQ-point and erg of energy. The Gerry Corrigan / Salomon anecdote: the highest-paid Salomon employee in 1991 was 26 and "can't even throw a football." The system is capricious — Ted Williams earned $20,000/year while today's .230 hitters earn millions because cable TV created revenue that didn't exist. Munger: "Frequently in the wealth advisory business, you go to your investment advisor and he says, 'Why don't you give me $50,000 of your net worth now? That's my contribution to your future.'"

**M19 · "Be a better person in the second half of your life."** *Buffett:* If fortune has showered you with advantages, the second half of your life should be better than the first. "You've had a chance to see how stupid you were." He considers himself still learning from experience — not book knowledge but "actually how the human animal behaves." *Munger:* "I don't even look at what I did when I was young because it would embarrass me."

**M20 · Nuclear risk: no protection possible.** *Buffett:* Every day since August 1945 carries a tiny probability of nuclear use; the world "flips a coin" daily. Berkshire has no product for nuclear risk. *Jain:* Even with nuclear exclusion clauses in policies, regulators and courts would likely require insurers to pay fire losses caused by nuclear weapons. *Munger:* "I know a man who said, 'I know what I'm going to do if there's a nuclear war. I'm going to crawl under the table and kiss my ass goodbye.'" See [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md).

**M21 · Cyberattacks: constant risk, constant defense.** *Abel:* Berkshire Hathaway Energy receives "billions of attacks every day" against its operating systems. No significant event to date. The government–private sector collaboration on cyber is "magnificent" — government agencies submit BHE's operating data daily to help identify penetrations. "It never stops." See [greg-abel](../people/greg-abel.md).

**★ M22 · "Be exceptionally good at something" — the inflation-proof investment.** *Buffett:* The best hedge against inflation is not a security but a skill: "If you're the best doctor in town… whatever abilities you have can't be taken away from you, they can't actually be inflated away from you." Skills are not taxed. Inflating money away does not change who people choose for surgery, for law, for entertainment. *Munger* (on retirement savings): "When your friendly advisor suggests you put all the money into bitcoin — just say no." The skill argument echoes the broader productive-asset thesis: human capital that produces something valuable is the ultimate store of real wealth. See [bonds-and-inflation](../concepts/bonds-and-inflation.md).

**★ M23 · "Berkshire is built for forever. There is no finish point."** *Buffett:* On insurance-risk succession: Berkshire's culture is "99.9% of running the business." The ownership structure (concentrated, long-horizon shareholders), the board composition, and the absence of options-and-career incentives together make a hostile cultural change structurally difficult. "There's no one waiting to retire or have their options vested." If he dies tomorrow: the railroad runs the same way, the insurance operation runs the same, and successive management are "just custodians of a culture that's embedded." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**M24 · "Keep learning" — and the cost of early mistakes.** *Buffett:* Catalogs early blunders: the New England textile mill (ran 25 years in a structurally hopeless competitive position — TVA power rates 60% below New England); a second textile company in New Hampshire; a department store (Hochschild-Kohn) in Baltimore. "Why the hell did we do it in the first place?" *Munger:* "Because we were stupid." And then: "Recognising reality, when it's really awful, and taking appropriate action, just involves the most elementary good sense." Coda: "Keep learning, keep learning, keep learning." See [cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses](../concepts/cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses.md), [mistakes-of-omission](../concepts/mistakes-of-omission.md).

**★ M25 · The "aha moment" that changed everything.** *Buffett:* At 19 or 20, having spent years charting stocks, studying technical analysis, and doing "everything wrong," he read a paragraph in chapter 8 of *The Intelligent Investor* — and it flipped his entire conceptual frame: he had been trying to pick stocks that would go up; the paragraph showed that was "totally foolish." The metaphor: an ambiguous-illusion drawing where the mind suddenly flips from seeing two faces to a vase. "It told me I wasn't looking at the duck." The insight generalises: transformative learning is rare, usually comes from a single concentrated exposure, and feels obvious in retrospect. "How could I have been so stupid?" *Munger:* "Part of the trick is to get so you correct your own mistakes. Frequently way too late." See [ben-graham](../people/ben-graham.md), [margin-of-safety](../concepts/margin-of-safety.md).

**M26 · Robinhood's "unraveling" is "God getting just."** *Munger:* (unprompted) Robinhood went public, lured customers into short-term gambling, charged hidden commissions and kickbacks. "It was disgusting." Now its share price has collapsed from peak. "God is getting just." *Buffett:* The founders are still wealthy from the IPO — "they've gotten a lot of money from it." But Munger's critique of Robinhood (first aired at the 2021 meeting) has been validated. See [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md).

## Q&A digest — Afternoon session

**A2 · Buffett backs off public political positions.** *Buffett:* Previously argued "I don't put my citizenship in a blind trust." Now: one statement on a contested subject can make "a whole lot more people sustainably mad than you can make temporarily happy," and those people may take it out on Berkshire employees and shareholders. "I've decidedly backed off." If political speech is important enough, the right move is to resign the job rather than make other people bear the consequences. See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**A3 · "Berkshire is not a weapon to be used politically."** *Buffett:* Instructs managers not to spend Berkshire's money on preferred candidates, not to pressure suppliers to donate to causes. Berkshire participates in industry trade groups (railroad, insurance, energy) to avoid being a free rider on lobbying others do — but not for personal or political advantage. References Munger's famous 1989 letter resigning from the U.S. Savings and Loan League as the gold standard of institutional honesty under pressure.

**★ A4 · Multi-disciplinary thinking: "a man who carries only a hammer."** *Munger:* The case for mental models from many disciplines in one sentence: "A man who carries only a hammer thinks everything else is a nail, and you may go on wrong decisions if you don't have some command of all the disciplines." The cost: "You do irritate people terribly when you come into their territory and say, 'I'm multi-disciplinary. You're the expert, and I know better than you.' They hate you for it. I can attest to it." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [inversion](../concepts/inversion.md).

**★ A5 · "Inflation swindles almost everybody."** *Buffett:* Quotes his 1977 Fortune article and extends it: bonds are swindled (nominal return evaporates in real terms), cash savers are swindled, equity investors in capital-intensive businesses are swindled (must reinvest at inflated replacement cost). Capital-light businesses that can raise prices proportionally are the rare exception. Best individual protection: a skill for which others will pay regardless of the monetary unit. *Munger:* (silent — "I have nothing to add"). See [bonds-and-inflation](../concepts/bonds-and-inflation.md), [franchise-vs-business](../concepts/franchise-vs-business.md).

**★ A6 · "If you have a culture of lying, the processes just disappear."** *Buffett:* Long analysis of how earnings guidance corrupts corporate culture. Monthly analyst calls turn targets into catechisms. The first small adjustment to hit a number is like the first $5 from the cash register: "Do it once and you probably never stop." Successive CEOs cannot reverse it without indicting their predecessors. "You can have all these processes, but if you have a culture of lying, the processes disappear." Berkshire's rule: "We have never told anybody that the number had to be this or that or to change anything." His bottom line: "once you start lying, it's all over." *Munger:* "I think Berkshire's culture's going to last a long time after we're gone… The rest of corporate America is quite different, and it gets more different, I think, with each passing decade." See [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md), [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

**A7 · Activision as merger arbitrage.** *Buffett:* Discloses Berkshire owns ~9.5% of Activision (stopping just below the 10% SEC-filing threshold). A different Berkshire manager bought ~15M shares months earlier; after Microsoft's January 2022 announcement of a $95/share acquisition, Buffett recognized Activision as a "workout" — a stock whose value now depends on whether a corporate event occurs rather than on the market. He has analyzed it himself, has never spoken to anyone at Microsoft about it, and acknowledges the deal may be blocked by the DOJ or EU. "It can easily be that if the stock went up a few dollars, we'd be out." Cites prior arbitrages: Monsanto (Bayer), Red Hat (IBM), British Columbia Power. *Supersession note:* The Microsoft/Activision deal closed in October 2023 after prolonged UK regulatory opposition; Berkshire's arbitrage position was profitable. See [arbitrage](../concepts/arbitrage.md).

**A8 · Index funds getting "too much influence" over corporate governance.** *Munger:* "I think the thing is out of control and counterproductive. I don't think it's good for the country to have three passive investors, bright young men from Harvard, telling every corporation what proper governance is." Worries about 90% indexing making price discovery fail. *Buffett:* The three large index funds will ultimately do what is politically safe — they will not pursue policies that provoke a congressional backlash threatening their asset-gathering businesses. Self-interest and political survival will eventually bring restraint. See [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md), [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

**A9 · BHE leverage is regulator-mandated, not a choice.** *Buffett:* Berkshire Hathaway Energy carries large debt because utility commissions require it — regulators reason that since debt is cheaper than equity, using it lowers consumer rates. "We would love to have all equity in our utilities, but the regulator wouldn't stand for it." The BHE ownership split (Berkshire ~91%, Walter Scott estate ~8%, Greg Abel ~1%) is a historical accident from the 2000 acquisition; Munger: "I see no behavior from Greg ever that isn't in the best interests of Berkshire." See [greg-abel](../people/greg-abel.md), [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md).

**A10 · Munger willing to take extra risk to invest in China.** *Munger:* Chinese internet stocks sold off sharply after government crackdowns. "The reason I invested in China is I could get so much better companies at so much lower prices, and I was willing to take a little bit of a risk." Acknowledges more difficulties now than two or three years ago — regulatory risk, political risk — and that everyone is more worried. "Other people might reach the opposite conclusion." *Buffett:* "I have nothing to add."

**★ A11 · Float: the "relative absence of bureaucracy" is a co-asset.** *Buffett:* Float discussion prompted by question on Berkshire's insurance moat. Two key stories: (1) National Indemnity bought in 1967 in fifteen minutes from Jack Ringwalt (who periodically wanted to sell when regulators irritated him — Charlie "caught him in heat"); (2) Ajit Jain walked in on a Saturday in 1986 offering to run insurance, having never run an insurance business before — and "transformed Berkshire." On float's potential: if they could invest the full float at 8% after taxes, it would yield ~$11–12 billion annually. *Munger:* "The relative absence of bureaucracy at Berkshire has made the company a lot of extra money for a very, very long time." *Buffett:* "And it's made my life happier." See [float](../concepts/float.md), [national-indemnity](../entities/national-indemnity.md), [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md).

**A12 · "I look at Berkshire as a painting."** *Buffett:* "Berkshire, in a crazy way, I look at it as a painting. It's unlimited in size. It's got an ever-expanding canvas and I get to paint what I want." The object is clearly that partners come out well, but the intrinsic motivation is the act of painting itself. "The closest thing I can come to enjoying myself every minute of the day." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**★ A13 · Economic stimulus was necessary and it caused inflation.** *Buffett:* Federal Reserve currency in circulation rose from ~$800B (15 years ago) to ~$2.2 trillion — roughly $7,000 for every American. Thought experiment: mail every US household $1 million and tell them they can spend it in 30 days. "Prices are going to go up." The government effectively did something like this through pandemic relief. "It was almost impossible not to have inflation if you're going to mail out the kind of money we mailed out. And it's probably a good thing we did it." *Munger:* "It was something that had never been done on that scale before." *Buffett:* "Jay Powell is a hero. He did what he had to do." See [bonds-and-inflation](../concepts/bonds-and-inflation.md).

**A14 · GAAP is a convention, not value — and a hard one to improve.** *Buffett:* Would "resign the job" if asked to redesign GAAP. The rules are designed to protect auditors from lawsuits, not to reflect economic reality; intelligent people play games within them and the SEC can enforce rules but cannot stop technically legal distortions. His one unimplemented suggestion: take 100 randomly selected long-term derivative contracts and compare what each counterparty values it at. "I don't think anyone has ever done anything with that suggestion." *Munger (on Salomon's audit committee):* "When your accountant starts talking about a plug, it's not good." See [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md).

**A15 · Salomon's "floating plug" and the limits of auditing.** *Buffett:* Anecdote shared for the first time. When Salomon merged with Phibro in the early 1980s, the two firms could never fully reconcile their books (one on a trade basis, one on settlement). So a number was simply plugged in to make assets equal liabilities — and it moved every single day. Arthur Andersen, the auditor, signed off for twelve years without finding it. "We've just got this number that floats around every day, and we haven't found it in 12 years, and Arthur Andersen doesn't know where it is." *Munger:* "They called it the floating plug." See [salomon](../entities/salomon.md), [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md).

**★ A16 · The Alleghany "cancel the fee" anecdote — and why investment bankers add little.** *Buffett:* Alleghany's offer price was structured as "$850 per share less the advisory fees they choose to pay their investment bankers," so the total cost to Berkshire is fixed regardless. The point: in 57 years of Berkshire, a fairness opinion was required only twice (Diversified Retailing merger, then Blue Chip Stamps) — and both times Munger devised a competitive-bidding process that got the opinion for $60,000 from E.F. Hutton (1978) and then $110,000 for Blue Chip. "Pick ten prestigious banks, offer them $60,000, and go down the list." Jack Shad of E.F. Hutton took it immediately. Berkshire's point: a fairness opinion at any price certifies roughly the same judgment that the buyers and sellers already reached themselves — the value accrues to lawyers, not to the transaction. See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), [investment-friction](../concepts/investment-friction.md).

**A17 · Munger's technique for making insurance claims worth fighting.** *Buffett:* In the 1960s, a Wheeler & Munger employee stole ~$12,000; the insurer (a large, prestigious firm) denied the fidelity claim on spurious grounds. Munger wrote to the chairman: "I know that you'd be offended by the thought that you might be using this inequality of bargaining position to avoid paying — so I suggest we make the stakes fair. Let's multiply the $12,000 by ten, to $120,000 either way. If you win, I pay you $120,000." A $12,000 check arrived by return mail. Lesson: if the game is unfair, change the stakes so both sides have equal reason to fight.

**★ A18 · "I wouldn't buy all the bitcoin in the world for $25."** *Buffett:* Full productive-vs-nonproductive asset argument. Farmland produces food; apartment houses produce rent; a 1% stake in either is worth buying. "If you told me you owned all of the bitcoin in the world, and you offered it to me for $25, I wouldn't take it — because what would I do with it? I have to sell it back to you one way or another." Bitcoin has no terminal value from production; it depends entirely on the greater-fool chain continuing. Net money in the room never increases — it only changes hands, with "fraud and costs" extracted along the way. *Munger:* "In my life I try and avoid things that are stupid and evil and make me look bad in comparison with somebody else. And bitcoin does all three… it's very likely to go to zero." *Supersession note:* Bitcoin reached approximately $100,000 in late 2024; Munger's "very likely to go to zero" has not been validated. The productive-vs-nonproductive argument remains intact as a framework even if the price trajectory has contradicted Munger's specific prediction. See [investment-categories](../concepts/investment-categories.md), [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md).

**A19 · "Increased tribalism is dangerous."** *Buffett:* Observes that Americans are "behaving somewhat more tribal than they have for a long time" — comparable to the Roosevelt era of the 1930s, when one either had his picture on the wall or "thought he was going to destroy the country." Tribal behavior is human ("Nebraska football is tribal") and fun in the right setting, but dangerous when it overrides observable fact ("Two plus two is five" vs. "Two plus two is three"). Does not propose a solution. *Munger:* California's gerrymander ensures only "insane rightists and insane leftists" survive in the legislature; the six moderates get redrawn out every cycle.

**A20 · Work for whoever you admire the most.** *Buffett:* Best career advice: find what you'd be good at (usually what you love), then work for the person you admire most. He pestered Ben Graham for three years, then worked for himself. "I found somebody else I'd even rather work for than Ben, who happened to be myself." *Munger:* "Figure out what you're bad at and avoid all of it. That's the way Warren and I found our profession — we failed at everything else." See [ben-graham](../people/ben-graham.md).

**A21 · Munger: keep U.S. oil reserves; buy the Arabs' oil first.** *Munger:* Contrarian view — if he were a "benevolent despot," he would import foreign oil cheerfully and conserve domestic reserves for the next 200 years. "Nobody else has my view, so it doesn't bother me. I just think they're all wrong." *Buffett:* Declines to weigh in on energy policy beyond noting that the U.S. producing 11 million barrels/day is clearly preferable to not producing it.

**★ A22 · Share repurchase principles: it's just buying your partner out at the right price.** *Buffett:* If a lemonade-stand partner wants to exit at an attractive price and the business has the funds, you buy them out — simple. The only variables are (a) is it at a price that benefits remaining partners? (b) is there a better use of the capital? Henry Singleton of Teledyne bought back 89% of his company over time, issuing stock when it was overpriced and buying when it was cheap — "the key is having people think you're wrong in doing it." Berkshire has sensible shareholders, which actually limits buyback opportunity. "If we had the same group of shareholders that own two-day puts, we could buy back the whole company in a very short period of time." See [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md), [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md).

**★ A23 · "Independent" directors who get 100% of their income from board fees.** *Buffett:* Received a letter years ago from an "independent director" at five prestigious companies who wrote: "My income is composed 100% of my board fees" — and was asking Buffett for a couple million dollars in personal assistance. Such a person would never raise a governance concern that risked losing a directorship. The only thing that matters for getting on the next board: "that he doesn't cause trouble." Meanwhile, Berkshire itself was voted against by CalPERS for supposedly not being independent — because Dairy Queen (a Berkshire franchise) bought Coca-Cola. *Munger:* "He's 'independent' the way a slave is independent." The whole edifice of process-based "independence" is designed to give directors legal cover, not to produce genuine oversight. See [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md), [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).

## Connections to other sources

- The gambling-parlor critique deepens a thread from [berkshire-2021-meeting](berkshire-2021-meeting.md), where Buffett first used SPAC activity and Robinhood as exhibit A; 2022 adds the OXY purchase as a live, quantified proof — 14% of a major company in two weeks.
- The bitcoin argument is the fullest expansion of the crypto "bad endings" warning in [berkshire-2018-meeting](berkshire-2018-meeting.md) and Munger's Robinhood/trading-turds line in the same; here Buffett provides the theoretical frame (productive vs. nonproductive) and Munger provides the tightest three-part verdict.
- The "we haven't ever timed anything" denial (M17) is the sharpest version of the market-agnosticism position first stated in [berkshire-1994-meeting](berkshire-1994-meeting.md) M47 ("We think about what will happen…it's impossible to figure out when") — here illustrated with the specific 2008 mistake (buying too early) and March 2020 miss (not buying at all).
- The Alleghany "no-bankers" ethic (A16) is the live application of the acquisition philosophy developed across the letters, particularly [berkshire-1994-letter](berkshire-1994-letter.md): sellers who understand Berkshire know there will be no auction, no due-diligence circus, and no post-closing meddling — qualities that produce deal flow that never appears in an investment bank's pitch book.
- "Inflation swindles almost everybody" (A5, A13) is the spoken companion to [berkshire-1977-letter](berkshire-1977-letter.md)'s systematic argument on inflation and equity returns; the 2022 meeting adds the massive-money-creation mechanism and credits Jay Powell for acting faster than any prior Fed chair.
- The culture-of-lying analysis (A6) echoes the institutional-imperative theme woven through the letters from [berkshire-1986-letter](berkshire-1986-letter.md) onward; 2022 is the most direct explanation of how the cascade starts — the first target missed, then fudged, then accepted as practice.
