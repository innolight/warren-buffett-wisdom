# Berkshire Hathaway 2021 Annual Letter

Warren Buffett's letter for fiscal year 2021, released February 26, 2022. A relatively quiet year for transactions ("little action of that sort in 2021"), the letter is dominated by four themes: the scale of Berkshire's infrastructure footprint, the 2019–2021 share repurchase campaign ($51.7B, retiring 9% of shares), Apple as the canonical look-through-earnings illustration, and a retrospective on 55 years of insurance float. Paul Andrews' death prompts an extended tribute that doubles as a case study in Berkshire's "preferred home" acquisition model and traces the TTI relationship directly to the BNSF acquisition.

## Key themes

- **Three paths to value creation** — internal growth, minority public-market stakes, and share repurchases; in 2021 internal opportunities "deliver far better returns than acquisitions" but are "small compared to Berkshire's resources"; minority stakes offer "little that excites us" because low interest rates push productive-asset prices up across all classes; repurchases are the "mildly attractive alternative" for deploying capital in 2020–2021
- **Infrastructure scale** — Berkshire owns more U.S.-based infrastructure (property, plant, and equipment) than any other American corporation: $158B at yearend 2021; "supremacy has never been our goal — it has, however, become a fact"
- **Float at $147B** — grew $9B in 2021; 55-year arc: $19M at the 1967 National Indemnity purchase to $147B; 55-year record of aggregate underwriting profit despite periodic loss years; the Ajit Jain 1986 hiring as the load-bearing lucky event; float is "very sticky" and "immune from precipitous decline"
- **Float per share and repurchases** — a non-obvious compounding connection articulated for the first time: buybacks automatically increase float per share because total float is roughly constant while share count shrinks; over 2019–2021, float-per-"A"-share rose 25%, from $79,387 to $99,497
- **Apple as look-through-earnings case** — GAAP records only $785M in Apple dividends; Berkshire's 5.55% share of Apple's 2021 earnings was $5.6B — more than 7×; "each 0.1% of Apple's 2021 earnings amounted to $100 million"; stake crept from 5.39% to 5.55% costlessly via Apple's ongoing buybacks
- **Earnings quality** — BNSF's record $6B earnings cited as "the old-fashioned sort of earnings that we favor: a figure calculated after interest, taxes, depreciation, amortization and all forms of compensation"; paired immediately with a warning that "deceptive 'adjustments' to earnings have become both more frequent and more fanciful as stocks have risen"; "bull markets breed bloviated bull"
- **"Preferred home" acquisition model** — Paul Andrews' TTI story: after a year considering competitors (would fire associates) and LBO firms (would flip), chose Berkshire as "the only guy left"; Berkshire's permanent-home reputation is a compounding deal-sourcing advantage; TTI in turn led directly to the BNSF acquisition in 2009

## Notable quotes

> Charlie and I are not stock-pickers; we are business-pickers.

> Long-term interest rates that are low push the prices of all productive investments upward, whether these are stocks, apartments, farms, oil wells, whatever. Other factors influence valuations as well, but interest rates will always be important.

> Much of our huge value creation in insurance is attributable to Berkshire's good luck in my 1986 hiring of Ajit Jain. We first met on a Saturday morning, and I quickly asked Ajit what his insurance experience had been. He replied, "None." I said, "Nobody's perfect," and hired him. That was my lucky day: Ajit actually was as perfect a choice as could have been made. Better yet, he continues to be – 35 years later.

> repurchases automatically increase the amount of "float" per share. That figure has increased during the past two years by 25% – going from $79,387 per "A" share to $99,497.

> [On BNSF earnings] we are talking about the old-fashioned sort of earnings that we favor: a figure calculated after interest, taxes, depreciation, amortization and all forms of compensation. … Deceptive "adjustments" to earnings – to use a polite description – have become both more frequent and more fanciful as stocks have risen. Speaking less politely, I would say that bull markets breed bloviated bull.

> After a year of pondering the alternatives, I want to sell to Berkshire because you are the only guy left. — Paul Andrews, as paraphrased by Buffett ("in far more tactful phrasing than this")

> Never bet against America.
