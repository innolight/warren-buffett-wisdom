# Berkshire Hathaway 1988 Annual Letter

The 1988 letter is among the analytically richest in the series. It delivers a systematic, empirically grounded demolition of efficient market theory using 63 years of Graham-Newman/Buffett/Berkshire arbitrage returns; a candid structural explanation of why mediocre CEOs outlast mediocre subordinates; and the first explicit statement of "forever" as a principled holding period rather than a tax preference. Berkshire's Coca-Cola purchase ($592.5M for 14.2M shares) is announced tersely — the full investment thesis follows in 1989 — but the surrounding ideas do the lasting work: concentration without apology, governance failure by structural design, and a theory of shareholder relations that shapes every Berkshire capital-structure decision for decades.

## Key themes

### The market is frequently efficient — not always efficient

Efficient market theory, "almost holy scripture in academic circles during the 1970s," rests on a fatal conflation: observing correctly that the market is *frequently* efficient and concluding incorrectly that it is *always* efficient. "The difference between these propositions is night and day."

The refutation is empirical, not philosophical. Across Graham-Newman (1926–1956), Buffett Partnership (1956–1969), and Berkshire (1969–1988) — 63 continuous years of arbitrage — unleveraged pre-tax returns averaged around 20% annually against a market that returned roughly 10%. The test meets every fairness condition: hundreds of securities, no skew from a handful of events, all trades in publicly announced situations (no obscure research required), no hindsight selection. $1,000 compounding at 10% over 63 years grows to $405,000; at 20% it becomes $97 million. That differential "might, conceivably, arouse one's curiosity." EMT proponents "have never seemed interested in discordant evidence of this type." The conclusion: "From a selfish point of view, Grahamites should probably endow chairs to ensure the perpetual teaching of EMT."

The transferable framework: before evaluating an arbitrage or special-situation investment, apply four questions — (1) how probable is the announced event? (2) how long will capital be tied up? (3) could something better emerge (a competing bid)? (4) what happens if the event fails? The Arcata Corp. case study (KKR LBO at $37 + two-thirds of any redwood-claim recovery) illustrates all four in practice: initial 15% annualized return on a messy deal, followed years later by an additional $29.48/share ($19.3M) when the redwood claim settled for $519M — a bonanza from careful assessment of the uncertainty, not luck.

### "Forever" as a principled ownership stance

The Coca-Cola position — 14.2M shares at $592.5M cost — is announced in a single paragraph. The accompanying principle is stated plainly: "When we own portions of outstanding businesses with outstanding managements, our favorite holding period is forever." This is not a tax-efficiency argument, though taxes favor long holds; it is a statement about the appropriate unit of analysis. A business that compounds well should not be sold to fund another investment merely because it has risen in price. Peter Lynch's metaphor appears: cutting the flowers and watering the weeds.

Concentration follows logically. "There are only a handful of businesses about which we have strong long-term convictions. Therefore, when we find such a business, we want to participate in a meaningful way." Quoting Mae West: "Too much of a good thing can be wonderful." The year-end 1988 portfolio — Coca-Cola, Freddie Mac preferred, and three permanent holdings (Capital Cities/ABC, GEICO, Washington Post) — is deliberately few and large. The deliberateness is the point.

### CEO accountability: why the board fails where the boss does not

The letter contains Buffett's sharpest governance essay to that point. The mechanism is structural, not moral. A secretary hired for 80-words-per-minute who produces 50 is gone quickly — there is an objective standard, easily measured, with direct accountability. A sales hire who misses targets faces the same: "Excuses will not be accepted as a substitute for orders."

A failing CEO is structurally different. Performance standards for the job seldom exist; when they do, they are fuzzy, waivable, or explained away. The board — the CEO's nominal superior — "seldom measures itself and is infrequently held to account for substandard corporate performance." If the board perpetuates a hiring mistake and the company is taken over as a result, the outgoing board members typically receive substantial severance — "The bigger they are, the softer they fall." And board culture discourages candor: "At board meetings, criticism of the CEO's performance is often viewed as the social equivalent of belching." The result: "The supreme irony of business management is that it is far easier for an inadequate CEO to keep his job than it is for an inadequate subordinate." Or, pithily: "At too many companies, the boss shoots the arrow of managerial performance and then hastily paints the bullseye around the spot where it lands."

This essay is the direct precursor to the [institutional-imperative](../concepts/institutional-imperative.md) named one year later — the same Board-inertia dynamic given its canonical label in [berkshire-1989-letter](berkshire-1989-letter.md).

### Owner-orientation: stock should track business value, not drift from it

NYSE listing on November 29, 1988 gives Berkshire a platform to state what it wants from its shareholder base — and what it does not want. Two goals distinguish Berkshire from typical public companies.

First, shares should trade at intrinsic business value, not above or below it: "Both extremes will inevitably produce results for many shareholders that will differ sharply from Berkshire's business results." A shareholder who buys at a large premium to intrinsic value receives a poor result even if the business performs well; one who sells to a buyer at a large premium transfers value from the buyer to themselves — a zero-sum harm to the shareholder base.

Second, low trading volume is a *feature*, not a failure. "We don't understand the CEO who wants lots of stock activity, for that can be achieved only if many of his owners are constantly exiting. At what other organization — school, club, church, etc. — do leaders cheer when members leave?" The goal is a shareholder base of long-term owners who measure Berkshire as Buffett measures it — and who are "uninteresting to those with short-term or unrealistic expectations." This philosophy is the forerunner of the 1996 Class B share design, every candid-disclosure policy, and the no-split stance.

### GAAP as a beginning, not an end

The accounting section opens with a disclaimer — "I would hate to have the job of devising a better set of rules" — then sharpens to a principle: GAAP is a floor, and any CEO who treats it as the ceiling is failing his owners. Three questions that financial statements should answer: (1) approximately how much is this company worth? (2) can it meet its obligations? (3) how well are managers doing given the hand dealt? These questions are rarely answerable from minimum GAAP presentations alone.

Many managements compound the problem by exploiting GAAP's latitude: "So these charlatans interpret the rules 'imaginatively' and record business transactions in ways that technically comply with GAAP but actually display an economic illusion to the world. … It has been far safer to steal large sums with a pen than small sums with a gun." The investor's response is to treat reported GAAP as the starting point for analysis — supplemented with segment data, management narrative, and economic reality — not the ending point.

## Entities discussed

- [berkshire-hathaway](../entities/berkshire-hathaway.md) — net worth +$569M (20%); per-share book value $2,974.52; NYSE listed November 29; AAA credit rating from S&P; Sainted Seven operating businesses earning ~67% on average equity capital with no financial leverage
- [coca-cola](../entities/coca-cola.md) — first major purchase: 14.2M shares at $592.5M cost, held in insurance subsidiaries; announced tersely as a permanent holding; the full investment rationale follows in [berkshire-1989-letter](berkshire-1989-letter.md)
- [geico](../entities/geico.md) — 44%-owned investee; California's Proposition 103 threatens to force auto-insurance price cuts below cost; GEICO's low-cost producer status means substantial economic goodwill is at risk if forced from the California market or if similar measures spread
- [capital-cities](../entities/capital-cities.md) — permanent holding; paired with GEICO and Washington Post as exemplars of outstanding management, cited in direct contrast to the CEO-incompetence essay
- [washington-post](../entities/washington-post.md) — permanent holding; same role as Capital Cities in the exemplar contrast
- [borsheims](../entities/borsheims.md) — 80% interest purchased early 1989; the Friedman family applies the same "sell cheap and tell the truth" discipline as [nebraska-furniture-mart](../entities/nebraska-furniture-mart.md); Buffett purchased without audited financials, inventory review, or receivables verification — trust as the underwriting criterion
- [salomon](../entities/salomon.md) — $700M 9% convertible preferred; Berkshire and Salomon directors walled off from M&A information, which restricted Berkshire's RJR Nabisco arbitrage purchases and cost "significant money"
