# Berkshire Hathaway 2008 Annual Letter

Written during the worst financial crisis since the Great Depression, the 2008 letter is Buffett at his most analytical about systemic risk. Net worth fell 9.6% — the worst single-year decline in Berkshire's 44-year modern history — yet the letter's most durable contributions are not about the crisis itself but about what the crisis revealed: the structural dangers embedded in complex derivatives, the principles of sound consumer lending, and why popular quantitative risk models fail over long time horizons.

## Key themes

**Derivatives validated — and examined from the inside.** The 2005 "canary in the coal mine" warning materialized exactly as described: Bear Stearns' collapse is the textbook counterparty-daisy-chain case in practice. More unusually, Buffett then explains Berkshire's own 251 derivative contracts (equity index puts, high-yield credit indices, individual credit default swaps) and why he believes each was mispriced at inception in Berkshire's favor. The most durable section is the **Black-Scholes critique for long-dated options**: short-term historical price volatility is simply irrelevant to estimating the range of business values over decades, yet Black-Scholes encodes it as the primary input. The 100-year put thought experiment demonstrates that the formula can produce premiums smaller than the mathematical expected loss — a self-evident reductio.

**Clayton Homes as the stress test of the 2003 thesis.** Berkshire's manufactured-housing borrowers — median FICO 644, 35% below the "subprime" threshold — had only 3.6% delinquency during the housing crash, dramatically outperforming conventional mortgage borrowers with better credit scores. The explanation is structural, not demographic: Clayton's borrowers took mortgages with genuine intention to repay, made real down payments from savings, and neither relied on refinancing nor assumed home price appreciation. The contrast reveals that underwriting discipline, not borrower creditworthiness as measured by FICO, is the primary driver of loan performance.

**Lending 101 distilled.** The key insight contradicting popular 2008 discourse: most foreclosures occur because borrowers cannot make monthly payments, not because their house is underwater. A homeowner with meaningful equity from a real down payment does not walk away just because paper value has fallen. Buffett's three non-negotiable conditions for sound mortgage lending: (1) honest down payment of at least 10%, from savings rather than borrowed funds; (2) monthly payments within actual — not hoped-for — income; (3) income carefully verified. "Putting people into homes, though a desirable goal, shouldn't be our country's primary objective. Keeping them in their homes should be the ambition."

**Beware of geeks bearing formulas.** The broader epistemological lesson: back-tested models fail when "universe past" and "universe current" have structurally different characteristics. Mortgage losses came "in large part because of flawed, history-based models" calibrated on modest home price increases and low speculation, then applied to a universe of skyrocketing prices and deteriorating underwriting. The manufactured-housing 1997–2000 collapse was an ignored preview of the 2004–2007 conventional-housing disaster. The diagnostic question before applying any historical model: are the structural forces that generated the historical data unchanged?

**MidAmerican as the reinvestment machine.** No dividend paid since Berkshire's 2000 investment — all earnings plowed back into infrastructure. In 2008 alone, MidAmerican spent $1.8B on wind generation while earning only $1.1B combined from its Iowa and Pacific utility operations. This is the regulated-utility capital deployment template: regulators authorize fair returns in exchange for genuine infrastructure commitment. Iowa electricity prices were held flat from Berkshire's purchase through a promised 2013 — a remarkable multi-decade pricing guarantee achieved through operational efficiency rather than rate-seeking.

**Four-goal clarity under pressure.** Buffett frames 2008 not as a crisis to survive but as an environment that tests and reveals: (1) maintaining a Gibraltar financial position, (2) widening moats around operating businesses, (3) acquiring new earnings streams at favorable prices, (4) nurturing the cadre of outstanding managers. The large fixed-income purchases at crisis-period terms (Goldman Sachs, GE, Wrigley) — with equity participation as a bonus — are the practical expression of pessimism-as-friend.
