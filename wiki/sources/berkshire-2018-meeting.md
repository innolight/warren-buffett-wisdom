# Berkshire Hathaway 2018 Annual Meeting

Held May 5, 2018, at the CenturyLink Center in Omaha, the 2018 meeting opens with a structural shift already in place: Greg Abel (Non-Insurance) and Ajit Jain (Insurance) had been elevated to Vice Chairman in January 2018, and both are formally introduced to the audience as directors — the most concrete succession signal Berkshire had yet delivered. Apple, at ~250 million shares or roughly 5% of the company, has just become Berkshire's largest equity position after a massive Q1 2018 purchase; Buffett uses the meeting to articulate, clearly and repeatedly, why he views it as a consumer-franchise bet rather than a technology bet. The just-announced Haven joint venture with Amazon and JPMorgan Chase to tackle U.S. healthcare costs draws pointed questions, and Buffett delivers a precise update on the "tapeworm" diagnosis he had aired at the 2017 meeting. Bitcoin was near its post-2017 peak hangover — Buffett called it "rat poison squared" in pre-meeting media; Munger at the meeting one-ups him with the most vivid condemnation in the corpus. A new GAAP rule requiring equity securities to be marked to market through the income statement had just taken effect in January 2018, and Buffett opens with a detailed warning about what will make reported earnings "enormously deceptive." The Berkshire opening slideshow is a masterclass in long-term investing: $10,000 in an S&P 500 index fund in March 1942 → $51 million today. Wells Fargo's scandal, now a year old and still festering under Federal Reserve restrictions, receives a deeper treatment than 2017 — with historical parallels to GEICO in the 1970s and American Express in 1963, arguing that companies that survive scandals and clean up often emerge stronger. Trade war anxiety (Trump tariffs announced in early 2018) prompts Buffett's most complete formulation of the educator-in-chief principle and the obligation to protect "roadkill." This page follows the two-layer convention: a distilled thematic top, then a comprehensive ★-marked Q&A digest as the home for detail.

## How to read the Q&A digest

- **★** marks a timeless, load-bearing exchange worth citing in the future; unmarked entries carry investor value but are secondary or period-specific.
- Answers are attributed separately to **Buffett** and **Munger** — a Munger line appears only when he added a distinct lesson (he said "Nothing to add" to most).
- **M#/A#** map to the numbered items in the raw morning/afternoon transcript for traceability.

## Key themes

### The American tailwind is the only bet you need to make — and non-productive assets prove it

Buffett's 2018 opening is a formal master class built around a single slide: $10,000 invested in an S&P 500 index fund on March 11, 1942 (the day he bought his first stock, during the worst months of the Pacific war) would be worth $51 million today. He bought three shares of Cities Service preferred at 38¼ — the high for the day — and sold too soon for a small profit; the stock eventually called at over $200. The lesson he draws: "All you had to do was figure that America was going to do well over time, that we would overcome the current difficulties, and that if America did well, American business would do well." The gold comparison is the analytical punch: 300 ounces of gold bought in March 1942 is still 300 ounces today and worth approximately $400,000 — less than one cent for every dollar made in American business. "It's just remarkable to me that we have operated in this country with the greatest tailwind at our back that you can imagine." The same principle survived fourteen presidents across 76 years — seven Republican, seven Democrat, one assassinated, one resigned — through panics, recessions, and wars. See [american-tailwind](../concepts/american-tailwind.md), [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md). *(M3)*

### Apple is a consumer-franchise bet, and buybacks are value-accretive when stock is cheap

The 2018 meeting is the first time Buffett gives a full public accounting of the Apple thesis after buying roughly 250 million shares (≈5% of Apple). The framework is deliberate: "I didn't go into Apple because it was a tech stock in the least... It's much more the nature of consumer behavior." The iPhone's switching costs are embedded in life management — "the value of an ecosystem and how permanent that ecosystem could be." On Apple's $100B buyback program, Buffett articulates the arithmetic precisely: Berkshire owned ≈5%; as Apple repurchases shares, "with the passage of a little time we may own 6 or 7 percent simply because they repurchase shares. And I love the idea of having our 5 percent... grow to 6 or 7 percent without us laying out a dime." The buyback principle has one firm condition: only if shares are below intrinsic value. Munger's addendum: companies that buy stock "to keep the stock up" are doing something "insane. And immoral. But apart from that, it's fine." See [apple](../entities/apple.md), [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md). *(A6)*

### Scandals don't kill great franchises — they can purify them

Buffett's extended Wells Fargo defence is this meeting's deepest lesson in institutional resilience. His argument traces a consistent pattern: American Express in 1963 (field warehousing fraud; Tino De Angelis in Bayonne, New Jersey — auditors were paid to check the tanks and never went), GEICO in 1976 (reserve understatement to meet Wall Street expectations, near-bankruptcy), Salomon in 1991 (CEO inaction when Paul Mozer's fraud surfaced) — and Wells Fargo now. "We've made a couple of our greatest investments where people have made similar errors." GEICO is now 13% of U.S. households; American Express stronger than ever; the pattern is that cleanup creates institutional strength that had never been tested. The conditions: problems must be acknowledged, incentives must be corrected, and management must act. "The important thing is, we don't want to incent any of that if we can avoid it, and if we find — when we find — it's going on, we have to do something about it." *Supersession flag:* despite defending the WFC position here, Berkshire began reducing it sharply from 2020 onward as the institutional damage proved more systemic than Buffett judged in 2018. See [wells-fargo](../entities/wells-fargo.md), [institutional-imperative](../concepts/institutional-imperative.md), [geico](../entities/geico.md). *(M10)*

### GAAP earnings are "enormously deceptive" — read operating earnings only

A new accounting rule effective January 2018 requires Berkshire to run all changes in the market value of its equity holdings — $170B+ — through the quarterly income statement. Buffett's rebuttal to the accounting expert who endorses the rule is precise: balance-sheet disclosure of market value is appropriate; income-statement flow-through is not, because it will create billions in volatile "earnings" or "losses" from holdings Berkshire intends to own for decades. "If you just were handed this with a TV monitor, you know, at 3:30 in the afternoon or whatever it might be, you would report the net earnings figure, understandably, very quickly. And it really is not representative of what's going on in the business at all." In Q1 2018 — Berkshire's best operating quarter ever — reported net income was sharply negative because of mark-to-market losses on equities. Munger's verdict on the accounting profession: "the questioner doesn't understand his own profession." The operating-earnings line is the only figure that describes what the business actually produced. See [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md), [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md). *(M2, M13)*

### Moats are real, durable, and worth fighting for — but no moat is permanent

Elon Musk called moats "lame" on Tesla's earnings call the week of the meeting; the question came to Buffett and Munger. Munger answered first and most efficiently: "Elon says a conventional moat is quaint. And that's true of a puddle of water... Warren does not intend to build an actual moat." Buffett's substantive reply: the pace of moat invasion has accelerated, but durable low-cost moats are still very real — "I don't think he'd want to take us on in candy" — and the appropriate response to moat pressure is continuous investment in the moat itself, not abandoning the concept. GEICO's cost advantage illustrates: "technology has really not brought down the cost that much." The meta-lesson is about the distinction between transient competitive pressure (normal; survivable; expected) and secular obsolescence (requires reassessment of the investment thesis). See [moat](../concepts/moat.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md). *(M28)*

### Capital-intensive is the forced second-best — and honest about it

An eight-year-old shareholder (Daphne, age 8, two years' holding experience) asked the sharpest capital-allocation question of the meeting: why, given Berkshire's reputation on capital-light wonderful businesses, are its largest recent acquisitions BNSF and BHE — businesses requiring enormous ongoing capital? Buffett's answer was complete and candid: "The fact that we buy a Burlington — BNSF — means that, essentially, we can't get more money deployed in capital-light businesses at prices that make sense to us." The capital-light businesses still exist (Apple "doesn't take much capital") but are priced accordingly; they "haven't shoved anything else off the table" — Berkshire would always prefer See's Candy economics, but won't buy a lousy capital-light business in preference to a good capital-intensive one just for aesthetic reasons. Munger: "the answer is they're good enough." The honest admission that size is the constraint, not a change of philosophy. See [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md), [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md). *(A8)*

### Investing is not complicated — but it must be internalized, not formula-ized

Asked whether the CFA, CFP, and business-school training are substitutes for the fundamentals, Buffett and Munger deliver the 2018 meeting's sharpest statement on the irreducibility of investment judgment. Buffett: "This is not — what we do is not a complicated business. It's got to be a disciplined business, but it does not require a super IQ or anything of the sort... I'd take the person from chapter eight [of The Intelligent Investor] who had it in their bones over the top graduate of any business school." Munger's caricature of why business schools teach CAPM and other obsolete frameworks: "When I had some eye troubles very early in life, I consulted a very famous eye doctor... He was still cutting with a knife after better procedures had been invented. I said, 'Why are you performing absolute obsolete operations?' And he said, 'Charlie, it's such a wonderful operation to teach.'" On the evolution from Graham to Buffett: "We gradually morphed into trying to buy the better companies when they were underpriced, instead of the lousy companies when they were underpriced." The principle: any investment framework that cannot be held as a living judgment — that requires "a formula" to execute — is the wrong framework. See [margin-of-safety](../concepts/margin-of-safety.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [ben-graham](../people/ben-graham.md). *(A23)*

## Q&A digest — Morning session

**M2 · New GAAP mark-to-market rule makes reported net income useless.** *Buffett:* opens by noting Q1 2018 showed Berkshire's best operating earnings in history, but the new GAAP rule marking equity securities to market through the income account produced a reported net loss. $170B of partly-owned companies (intended to hold for decades) will create "a gain or loss of a couple billion dollars" daily in reported income. The only meaningful figure is operating earnings excluding realized gains/losses and derivatives. "It really is not representative of what's going on in the business at all." See [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md).

**★ M3 · Master class: $10,000 → $51 million since 1942.** *Buffett:* walks through the slideshow built around his March 1942 first stock purchase during losing months in the Pacific — Dow broke 100 on the downside the day after he bought. $10,000 in an S&P 500 index fund on that day = $51 million today. Same $10,000 in gold (300 ounces) = ~$400,000. "For every dollar you had made in American business, you'd have less than a penny by buying in this store of value." The investor's only required judgment: America's system would work over a lifetime. "You basically just had to make one investment decision in your life." See [american-tailwind](../concepts/american-tailwind.md), [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md).

**M5 · Precision Castparts: "a very good acquisition with very long tails."** *Buffett:* two years after the acquisition, still bullish: reliability of quality and delivery time is the moat — aircraft makers "do not want to have an aircraft with 75- or 100- or maybe $200 million and be waiting for a part." Berkshire has won multi-year contracts from customers burned by other suppliers' delivery failures. $400M+ per year of non-deductible intangible amortization is non-economic: "Whatever you see, you can add about 400 million that in my view is not an economic expense." Mark Donegan is irreplaceable. *Munger:* "We'd buy another one just like it tomorrow if we had the chance." See [economic-goodwill](../concepts/economic-goodwill.md).

**M6 · US-China trade: win-win is too obvious to sacrifice.** *Buffett:* US exports were ~5% of GDP in 1970, now ~11%; imports ~14.5%. A modest gap is tolerable ("it's really not the worst thing in the world to have somebody send you a lot of goods that you want and hand them little pieces of paper"). Both countries have done remarkably well from trade. "Two intelligent countries will [not] do something extremely foolish." *Munger:* China advanced faster from a lower base, high savings rate; both nations should recognize the stakes.

**★ M7 · "The reputation belongs to Berkshire now."** *Buffett:* responds to the question of whether the deal flow requires his personal presence: the reputation for being a good home for privately-owned businesses built over decades is institutional, not personal. "It isn't that you're such a great guy — it's you're the only one left." Sellers who care about employees, culture, and continuity have nowhere else to go. "We absolutely are the first call. And we will continue to be the first call, whether Charlie or I answer the phone or somebody else does." *Munger:* subsidiaries are already making acquisitions independently of headquarters — "it's happening more there than it is at headquarters." See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**★ M8 · Cyber insurance: "we don't know what we're doing."** *Buffett:* includes cyber in his ~2% annual probability of a $400B super-cat event. "I don't think we, or anybody else, really knows what they're doing when writing cyber. It is just very, very, very early in the game." No actuarial basis for pricing — "offense is ahead of the defense, and will continue that way." Berkshire writes some cyber (BHSI) for competitive reasons but stays far from the largest exposures. "Cyber's uncharted territory, and it's going to get worse, not better." *Munger:* cites algorithmic trading errors as an already-realized analog — "somebody was fine one morning and by the afternoon they were broke because some computer went crazy." The principle: any risk where you cannot estimate probabilities even roughly should be treated as near-uninsurable at scale. See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md), [super-cat-insurance](../concepts/super-cat-insurance.md).

**★ M10 · Wells Fargo: great franchises survive scandals — if they clean up.** *Buffett:* frames Wells Fargo alongside GEICO (1976) and American Express (1963) as cases where incentive-driven scandals nearly destroyed a franchise — and both emerged stronger. "We've made a couple of our greatest investments where people have made similar errors." At American Express, the field-warehousing fraud nearly sank the company; "what emerged was one great company after this kind of, what they thought was a near-death experience." "That is a cardinal sin at Berkshire. We know people are doing something wrong right as we sit here... The important thing is, when we find it's going on, we have to do something about it." Berkshire has ~377,000 employees; perfection is impossible; speed of correction is everything. *Munger:* "I think Wells Fargo is going to be better going forward than it would have been if these leaks had never been discovered." And: "if I had to say which bank is more likely to behave the best in the future, it might be Wells Fargo, of all of them." *Supersession flag:* despite this endorsement, Berkshire began dramatically reducing the WFC position from 2020 onward as the regulatory cloud deepened and the institutional damage proved more pervasive than the 2018 characterization suggested. See [wells-fargo](../entities/wells-fargo.md), [institutional-imperative](../concepts/institutional-imperative.md), [geico](../entities/geico.md), [american-express](../entities/american-express.md).

**M11 · Succession: "I like to think I'll be missed a little bit, but you won't notice it."** *Buffett:* addresses whether Berkshire can still extract premium terms (8–10% coupons) in distressed-capital transactions without his personal imprimatur. Todd Combs and Ted Weschler have already handled similar transactions; counterparties who need Berkshire's capital "will not hang up because I don't answer the phone." *Munger:* the 2008-era crisis transactions were "like the worst in 50 years... a really rare kind of occurrence." The point: the franchise value of Berkshire's balance sheet and decisiveness is institutional; individual celebrity is secondary.

**M12 · Ajit Jain and pricing large unusual risks: "part of the fun of my job."** *Buffett:* for very large, one-off risks (e.g., $5B chemical plant catastrophe cover) where actuarial tables are useless, he and Ajit each price independently in their heads and compare notes. "I enjoy thinking through the pricing of that, and I particularly enjoy comparing it with Ajit." The broader point: some risks require judgment entirely separate from statistical tables, and the only way to develop that judgment is decades of practice. *Munger:* contact with subsidiary managers "largely depends on what they want, not what he wants." See [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**★ M13 · Running unrealized gains/losses through income is "enormously deceptive."** *Buffett:* detailed rebuttal to an accounting expert who argues the GAAP mark-to-market rule is correct: changes in the fair value of long-held equity stakes belong on the balance sheet, not flowing through the income account quarterly. GEICO, bought for ~$50M, is worth enormously more — should that appreciation run through income? "If I looked at our 60 or 70 businesses and every quarter we marked those to market, we would have... a great many... where over time we'd have them at 10 times what we paid, but how quarter-by-quarter we should mark those up and run it through the income account, where 99 percent of investors look at net income as meaningful, in terms of what has been produced from operations... I think would be enormously deceptive." *Munger:* "The questioner doesn't understand his own profession." The balance sheet discloses market value; that is sufficient and appropriate. See [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md), [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md).

**M14 · McLane: margin compression from powerful customers on both sides.** *Buffett:* core food-distribution margins have been cut roughly in half since acquisition from Walmart. "When you get Kraft Heinz, or Philip Morris, or whomever it may be, on one side of the deal, and you get Walmart and 7-Eleven on the other side of the deal, sometimes they don't leave you very much room in between." The liquor-distribution segment (≈$70M pretax in a few states) is partially masking the decline. "There comes a point where the customer says, 'I'll only pay X,' and you have to walk away." Honest assessment: commodity distribution is capitalism at its most austere. *Munger:* "I think you've described it very well." See [commodity-business-economics](../concepts/commodity-business-economics.md).

**★ M15 · Haven healthcare JV: attacking "an industry moat" with Bezos and Dimon.** *Buffett:* confirms the Amazon/JPMorgan/Berkshire joint venture announced in January 2018 to address U.S. healthcare costs. Motivations: the same 5%-of-GDP figure in 1960 has grown to ~18% while per-capita spending went from $170 to over $10,000; other countries plateaued at 10–11%. "We have fewer doctors per capita, fewer hospital beds per capita, fewer nurses per capita, than some of the other countries that are well below us." The initiative is not primarily profit-seeking; the goal is better care at lower cost for the three companies' one-million-plus employees. CEO search underway. Probability of significant success: low, but "if it was easy, it would've already been done." *Munger:* "There is some precedent for success in this public service activity... John B. Rockefeller I, using his own money, made an enormous improvement in American medical care." *Supersession flag:* the joint venture (later named Haven) named Atul Gawande as CEO in July 2018 but dissolved in January 2021 without achieving measurable systemic change. See [american-tailwind](../concepts/american-tailwind.md).

**M16 · Combs and Weschler: "slightly ahead" of S&P since inception.** *Buffett:* both managers, running ≈$12–13B each (≈$25B combined), are "almost identical" in cumulative performance since their respective inception dates and both slightly ahead of the S&P. Will not report individual figures. "It's been better than I've done, so naturally, I can't criticize it." Both perform well despite a higher-AUM constraint — "it's actually harder to run even 12 or $13 billion than a billion." Beyond their portfolios, each has brought ancillary value to Berkshire projects (Todd on the healthcare initiative, Ted on various business analyses). *Munger:* "They both think like shareholders. That is not the normal way headquarters employees think." See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md).

**M17 · GEICO: back on track after 2017 combined-ratio bump.** *Buffett:* GEICO's 2017 combined ratio went above 100 for the first time in ~15 years, driven by catastrophe losses (not a pricing failure). Q1 2018 underwriting margins of ~7% are back to a satisfactory level; April preliminary figures are similar. GEICO has grown from 2%-of-households when Tony Nicely took over in 1993 to 13% today. "GEICO is a jewel... It's saving its customers probably 4 or $5 billion a year against what they would otherwise be paying." "The more it grows, the better I like it." *Munger:* "It was never very bad, and it's better now." See [geico](../entities/geico.md), [float](../concepts/float.md).

**★ M18 · Trade, tariffs, and the roadkill obligation.** *Buffett:* Trump's steel and aluminum tariffs are already raising costs modestly for some Berkshire manufacturers. Macro view: "I don't think either country will dig themselves into something that precipitates and continues any kind of real trade war." But the structural policy problem is real: trade's benefits are invisible (you don't see the price you'd pay for shoes made only in America); the harm is vivid and personal (the Dexter Shoe worker in Maine who was very good at their job, maybe their parents did it before them, and now has no job). "You need to be an educator-in-chief, which Roosevelt was in the Depression." Two obligations: explain the invisible aggregate benefit honestly; and "take care of the people that are roadkill in something that is collectively good for us as a country." *Munger:* "Even Donald Trump can be right on some of this stuff." See [american-tailwind](../concepts/american-tailwind.md), [dexter-shoe](../entities/dexter-shoe.md).

**M20 · Special dividend "very unlikely"; buybacks preferred if capital exceeds uses.** *Buffett:* if cash grows to the point that no attractive use can be found, the mechanism for returning capital would be buybacks rather than a special dividend — "I would not... give you a really... your guess is almost as good as mine as to what margins will be in that distribution business five years from now." B shareholders voted 47-to-1 against a regular dividend in a prior poll; the self-selected shareholder base prefers capital retention. But: "If we really thought we never could use the money effectively in the business, we should get it out, one way or another." *Munger:* "As long as the existing system continues to work as well as it has, why would we change it?" Note: buyback threshold was formally raised from 1.2× to any price below intrinsic value in July 2018, after this meeting. See [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md), [dividend-policy](../concepts/dividend-policy.md).

**M21 · Chinese stocks: Munger has more China exposure than Buffett.** *Buffett:* with small capital he'd look primarily in the $30T U.S. market, but wouldn't rule out emerging markets (he personally bought 15–20 Korean stocks from a directory about 15 years earlier). Size is the real constraint, not geography: building a multi-billion position in international markets is mechanically harder (3% disclosure thresholds in Europe; followers and publicity). *Munger:* "I already have more stocks in China than you do, as a percentage." On the opportunity: "American investors are missing China... it looks too hard, sitting in Omaha, to outsmart the Chinese market. But I think you're absolutely right. It's where they should be looking." And: "generally speaking, the existing system has worked wonderfully for us. I don't think we have the employment that could be cut effectively."

**★ M22 · Berkshire in 2068: "keep the faith."** *Buffett:* on what Berkshire looks like in 50 years — "I don't know... it will be based on certain principles. But where that leads, we will find out." Commitment: "We will be as shareholder-oriented as any large company in the world." *Munger:* directed specifically to younger shareholders: "Those of you who, after we are gone, sell your Berkshire stock and do something else with it, helped by your many friends, I think are going to do worse. I would advise you to keep the faith." The underlying principle: a culture built on partnership-with-owners, perpetuated through selection of managers and directors who genuinely think like owners, is self-sustaining. See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md), [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**M23 · Duracell: "should be earning more money than it is now, and will be."** *Buffett:* earnings well below the P&G-era level because of transition constraints tied to the tax-structured stock-swap acquisition. Restructuring costs in high-severance European jurisdictions are real but temporary. "The brand is strong. Very strong." Saw Duracell reach its earnings potential during the Jim Kilts era at Gillette. "We should be earning that relatively soon." *Munger:* "I like it better than you do." See [gillette](../entities/gillette.md).

**★ M24 · Long-term bonds "almost ridiculous" at current rates.** *Buffett:* "we think long-term bonds are a terrible investment... at current rates or anything close to current rates." The arithmetic: the Fed targets 2% annual inflation; the 30-year Treasury yields "not much more than 3 percent"; after income tax, the real after-tax return may be ½% — "The Federal Reserve Board is telling you we want 2 percent a year inflation... that seems to me a very — I wouldn't go back to penny stocks — but I think I would stick with productive businesses." All Berkshire's waiting cash sits in Treasury bills with average maturity ~4 months; rising short rates mean Berkshire will earn ≥$500M more in 2018 on T-bills than 2017. *Munger:* "It clearly wasn't fair" to savers — "it benefited the people in this room enormously because it drove asset prices up." The early-1980s zero-coupon Treasury at 14% compounded for 30 years was the exceptional counterexample — "every now and then, something really strange happens in markets and the trick is to be prepared and take action when it happens." See [bonds-and-inflation](../concepts/bonds-and-inflation.md), [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md).

**★ M25 · Zero-based budgeting and "a bureaucracy is sort of like a cancer."** *Buffett:* Berkshire will not export 3G's zero-based budgeting approach wholesale because its best subsidiaries have never tolerated wasteful spending in the first place. GEICO grew from 8,000 to 39,000 employees since Berkshire bought control — all productive. "There comes a point where the customer says... 'I'll only pay X.'" At headquarters: "kind of subzero-based budgeting." No consolidated monthly financials; no submission of budgets to HQ; no annual plan. Probably the only Fortune 500 company without these. *Munger:* "it isn't just the cost reduction. I think the decisions get made better if you eliminate the bureaucracy. A bureaucracy is sort of like a cancer. And it functions sort of like a cancer." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).

**M26 · Can GEICO-ize the small commercial insurance market?** *Buffett:* biBERK and similar direct-to-small-business platforms are an early experiment in applying GEICO's direct model to commercial lines. "Over a lot of years — and maybe not so many years — something like small commercial... is going to work over time, if you've got a system that was based on something that had more layers of agency costs." Personal auto took decades to move from general agencies to captive agents (State Farm) to direct (USAA, GEICO, Progressive). "It takes an amazingly long time." Backing experiments with capital and accepting some failures. *Munger:* "It could happen, but it will be slow." See [geico](../entities/geico.md), [direct-distribution-moat](../concepts/direct-distribution-moat.md).

**M27 · A system-level moat does not protect the individual players inside it.** *Buffett:* asked whether the health-care venture's admitted difficulty proves the industry has a bigger moat — and whether that justifies a higher earnings multiple for established players like PBMs. Key distinction: "though the system may have a moat against intruders, it doesn't mean that everybody operating within the system has individual moats." The Haven venture is "attacking an industry moat" defined very broadly — "the moat held by the whole system, since it interacts in so many ways… that's the moat that essentially has to be attacked, and that's a huge moat" — which is far bigger than any single company's moat, yet says nothing about whether a given company inside it is protected. The transferable question for any high-barrier industry (payments, defense, drug distribution): is that an *industry* moat, or a *company* moat? *Munger:* predicts eventual single-payer medicine will not be friendly to today's PBMs (an off-domain political forecast). See [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**★ M28 · Buffett vs Musk on moats: "Warren does not intend to build an actual moat."** *Munger:* delivered the reply with dry precision — "Elon says a conventional moat is quaint. And that's true of a puddle of water. And he says that the best moat would be to have a big competitive position. And that is also right. You know, it's ridiculous. Warren does not intend to build an actual moat." *Buffett:* the pace of moat invasion has accelerated; more moats are "susceptible to invasion" than before. But durable ones remain: "Being the low-cost producer, for example, is a terribly important moat." GEICO's cost advantage has persisted despite technology. "I don't think he'd want to take us on in candy." The lesson: innovation can erode moats; the correct response is continuous investment in the moat's sources (cost structure, consumer habit, switching cost), not fatalism about moat durability. See [moat](../concepts/moat.md), [sees-candies](../entities/sees-candies.md).

**M29 · Berkshire Hathaway Energy: "wouldn't be surprised if we find good uses for capital for a long time."** *Buffett:* the utility company retains its earnings (not paying out the 60–70% dividend a typical utility does), giving it ≈$9B in deployable capital over five years. Tax credit phase-outs for wind and solar are a near-term headwind, but grid infrastructure investment opportunity in the U.S. is "hundreds of hundreds of billions, if not trillions." *Munger:* probability of a special dividend from BHE to the parent is "approximately zero." See [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md).

**M30 · Look-through earnings: "we wouldn't own those stocks otherwise."** *Buffett:* normalized earning power (operating earnings at 21% tax rate) in Q1 2018 annualizes to ~$20–21B but understates the full picture. Berkshire's share of retained earnings across $170B+ of equity holdings (Apple, American Express, Coca-Cola, Wells Fargo, etc.) — earnings that don't flow through the dividend line — amount to "many billions of dollars annually. One way or another, we think those dollars will benefit us as much as if they had been paid out." *Munger:* "I don't think our shareholders are going to see another increase in net worth of $65 billion in a single year... but eventually another will come, and then another. Just be patient." See [look-through-earnings](../concepts/look-through-earnings.md).

## Q&A digest — Afternoon session

**A2 · Microsoft is off-limits due to board-member conflict.** *Buffett:* early in his career, "the answer's stupidity." Since Bill Gates joined the Berkshire board, buying Microsoft would invite accusations of insider information regardless of reality — "both Bill and I would — incorrectly, but — would be a target of suggestions and accusations." A small set of stocks is permanently off-list due to association risk, independent of investment merits. *Munger:* "Part of theology is that a late conversion is better than never — and you've greatly improved yourself." The principle: reputation for integrity is worth forgoing certain investments.

**★ A3 · "Through it all, America really, really moves ahead."** *Buffett:* answers a question about whether the country is more divided today than ever. Since his 1942 first stock purchase: 14 presidents (seven Republican, seven Democrat; one assassinated, one resigned); Cuban Missile Crisis; nuclear weapons; the 2008 financial panic; multiple recessions; war in the streets in the late '60s. "$10,000 became 51 million. Through it all, you know — in fits and starts — but America really, really moves ahead." Per-capita GDP in one lifetime: six-for-one. "I would love to be a baby being born in the United States today." *Munger:* "there's a tendency to think that our present politicians are much worse than any we had in the past. But we tend to forget how awful our politicians were in the past." See [american-tailwind](../concepts/american-tailwind.md).

**A4 · General Re growth under Ajit: "we will be in reinsurance 50 years from now."** *Buffett:* Tad Montross retired; under Ajit's oversight, Gen Re has grown and become somewhat more growth-oriented, while keeping underwriting discipline. Life reinsurance has grown particularly well internationally. The large-deal capacity (AIG's $10.2B retroactive deal was the largest in history) is a structural advantage: "We are almost certainly the only insurance operation in the world that AIG would have trusted to honor a $20B commitment decades into the future." *Munger:* "the part that any idiot financier can easily get into has gotten way tougher. And why wouldn't it?" See [general-re](../entities/general-re.md), [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**★ A5 · No formula for intrinsic value — only judgment mixed with all the factors.** *Munger:* asked to demonstrate the arithmetic of intrinsic value calculation, replies: "Well, I can't give you a formulaic approach because I don't use one. I just mix all the factors and if the gap between value and price is not attractive, I go on to something else." Uses Costco as an example: "Costco was selling at about 12 or 13 times earnings, I thought that was a ridiculously low value, just because the competitive strength of the business was so great and it was so likely to keep doing better and better. Well, I can't reduce that to a formula for you." Business schools perpetuate obsolete formulas for the same reason an eye surgeon still cut with a knife after better procedures were available: "It's such a wonderful operation to teach." *Buffett:* "whenever you hear a theory described as elegant, watch out." See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ A6 · Apple buybacks: "I love the idea of growing from 5% to 7% without laying out a dime."** *Buffett:* Berkshire owns ≈250M Apple shares (≈5%). As Apple repurchases shares, Berkshire's percentage ownership rises automatically. "With the passage of a little time we may own 6 or 7 percent simply because they repurchase shares. And I — I love the idea of having our 5 percent... grow to 6 or 7 percent without us laying out a dime. It's worked for us in many other situations." Apple's alternatives for the $100B: large acquisitions at Apple's scale are essentially unavailable at rational prices — "I don't see anything that would make a lot of sense for them in terms of what they'd have to pay and what they would get." Would prefer Apple stock go down, because Apple will buy more shares more cheaply. *Munger:* "generally speaking in America, when companies go out hell-bent to buy other companies... they're worth less after the transaction is made than they were before." And: "some people just buy it to keep the stock up. And that, of course, is insane. And immoral. But apart from that, it's fine." See [apple](../entities/apple.md), [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md).

**★ A7 · American Express vs Visa/MasterCard: honest admission of a missed opportunity.** *Buffett:* Combs and Weschler each bought Visa and MasterCard at significant percentages of their own portfolios; Buffett admits he should have too and didn't. "Looking back, I should have." On American Express: the decision to not renew the Costco co-brand contract at a lower price was controversial (Buffett and Munger disagree on its wisdom), but AmEx has continued to expand — 17%+ ownership stake now achieved via buybacks alone, international growth of 15%+ in local currencies in several major markets, small-business penetration improving. *Munger:* one cloud: WeChat's payment dominance in China as an example of how payments could be disrupted. "I don't have the faintest idea how important that cloud is, and I don't think Warren does either." The principle: holding a successful franchise through turbulence is defensible; not buying the faster growers alongside it was the opportunity cost. See [american-express](../entities/american-express.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**★ A8 · Capital-intensive "good enough" — the 8-year-old's question.** *Buffett:* Daphne, age 8, two-year Berkshire shareholder, asked why Berkshire's largest recent investments require capital when its reputation was built on capital-light businesses. "The fact that we buy BNSF means that, essentially, we can't get more money deployed in capital-light businesses at prices that make sense to us... Wouldn't it be wonderful if we could run the railroad without trains, and track, and tunnels, and bridges?" The capital-light businesses haven't been abandoned — they just can't absorb the capital Berkshire generates. Apple "doesn't take much capital." Still, the second-best choice is a good choice. "You definitely have a job in our capital allocation department." *Munger:* "the answer is they're good enough." See [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md), [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md), [bnsf](../entities/bnsf.md).

**★ A9 · Newspapers: "I've been surprised that the rate of decline has not moderated."** *Buffett:* revisiting the 28-newspaper purchase from 2012. Of the 26 still owned, circulation fell sharply — "30 percent to almost 50 percent" in many cases. "No one except The Wall Street Journal, The New York Times, and now probably The Washington Post has come up with a digital product that... will replace the revenue that is being lost as print newspapers lose both circulation and advertising." The economic significance to Berkshire is "almost negligible"; the societal significance is "enormous." "Little local newspaper monopolies tended to be owned by people who behaved well and tended to control the politicians. And we're going to miss these newspapers if they disappear. We're going to miss them terribly." *Munger:* "I think, to the extent we miscalculated, we may have done it because we both love newspapers." *Supersession flag:* Berkshire subsequently sold or closed essentially all of its community newspapers by 2023, confirming that no viable economic model emerged. See [media-economics](../concepts/media-economics.md), [buffalo-evening-news](../entities/buffalo-evening-news.md).

**A10 · TTI: electronic components distribution as a moat no one wants to attack.** *Buffett:* TTI (electronic components distribution, Dallas-Fort Worth, founded by Paul Andrews from a General Dynamics division 45–50 years ago) has quadrupled under Berkshire. Average item value < one nickel; ≈1M SKUs; global operation. Book-to-build ratio has accelerated sharply in the past year. TTI's competitive moat: carrying more inventory than competitors creates a service advantage especially in tight supply markets. *Munger:* "it's a wonderful business because it's so difficult to do that competitors don't want to try it." Classic Munger moat theory: anything this operationally complex creates a natural competitive deterrent. "My theory, Warren, is if it can't stand a little mismanagement, it's no business."

**A11 · Phillips 66: stay under 10% in marketable securities.** *Buffett:* sold Phillips 66 shares (~93–94; now ~115) to stay just below 10% ownership. Overwhelmingly the policy will be to remain below 10% in all marketable securities because of regulatory reporting complexities and potential trading restrictions. Already executed small sells in American Airlines and United Continental for the same reason. Exception: the longtime large positions in American Express and Wells Fargo (held through grandfathered arrangements) will not be reduced solely to hit the threshold. CEO Greg Garland: "very, very, very experienced and sensible manager." See [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md).

**★ A12 · Cryptocurrencies "will come to bad endings."** *Buffett:* non-productive assets have no intrinsic value — you are entirely dependent on finding a buyer who believes in a future buyer. "It's been tried with tulips and it's been tried. It's been tried with various things over time. And it does come to a bad ending." The check or wire transfer is a wonderful mechanism for moving money, but that utility doesn't make the payment mechanism itself intrinsically valuable. "Cryptocurrencies will come to bad endings. And along with that, it draws in a lot of charlatans." *Munger:* "I like cryptocurrencies a lot less than you do. And so to me it's just dementia. And I think the people who are professional traders that go into trading cryptocurrencies, it's just disgusting. It's like somebody else is trading turds and you decide, 'I can't be left out.'" The principle: any asset whose only return driver is a future buyer's willingness to pay more is not an investment. See [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md).

**A13 · Corporate tax cut: shareholders benefit, but regulated utilities must pass through.** *Buffett:* 35%→21% federal rate change: regulated utilities must return 100% of the rate reduction to customers (commissions set allowed returns on equity; tax savings are not shareholder value in regulated businesses). For unregulated businesses: some of the benefit gets competed away quickly, some sticks, depending on industry economics. The tax change was designed to benefit shareholders and corporate profits — "Congress passed" it with that intent. But: "we will find out whether it will or not... it's very, very, very difficult in economics to measure the impact of single variables." See [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md).

**A15 · Subsidiary 401(k) plans: headquarters failed employees on low-cost options.** *Buffett:* a ProPublica/Washington Post investigation found many Berkshire subsidiaries offer only high-fee actively-managed 401(k) choices, inconsistent with Buffett's repeated public advocacy for index funds. Admitted failure: "We delegated the whole subject to the managers of the subsidiaries. And so no attention at all is being given to the employee choices at headquarters." Managers run their own HR; Berkshire won't mandate. *Munger:* "a lot of the employees in the subsidiaries would do better if they indexed... I hope we'll do a little better at encouraging better choices." The tension: genuine subsidiarity vs. the principal responsibility for employee financial wellbeing. See [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md), [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).

**A16 · Berkshire's culture will outlast current management: "it works."** *Buffett:* the culture is reinforced by self-selection of shareholders, directors (most hold significant personal stakes; no special deals), and managers (they chose to join). It is not 100% — "a few people all of the time that really don't buy into it entirely" — but it is unusually coherent for a public company. *Munger:* at the manager's luncheon before each annual meeting, "I feel more strongly at the end of the luncheon that the culture and values of Berkshire Hathaway will go on and on for a long time after the present management is gone." And: "One of the reasons it will last is it's not that damned easy to duplicate. Think of how little direct copying of the Berkshire system there's been." *Buffett:* "It works." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**A17 · Float and cash machine: "400 million comes into Berkshire every week."** *Buffett:* corrects a shareholder who thinks cash is tied to float as a coverage ratio. The two are not correlated by design; cash is piling up only because attractive uses aren't available, not because float requires backing. In Q1 2018: operating earnings ≈$5.25B; float grew ≈$2B net; combined inflow ≈$7B — but cash actually declined because Berkshire invested more in equities than the $7B inflow. "We do have this position where, even absent a change in float, about 400 million comes into Berkshire every week." The constraint is finding attractive recipients for that flow. See [float](../concepts/float.md), [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md).

**★ A18 · Asset-light economy: corporate profits permanently elevated because capital is no longer required.** *Buffett:* revisits his 1999 Fortune article arguing corporate profits couldn't sustain above 6% of GDP. They've been 8–10% since 2008. "You now have the four largest companies, by market value, in the United States — a $30 trillion market — you have four companies that essentially don't need any net tangible assets." AT&T, Carnegie's steel mills, Exxon — the entire prior century of American capitalism required enormous reinvestment. "American industry has gotten incredibly more profitable, in aggregate, in the last 20 or 30 years... this has become somewhat, you could call it an asset-light economy." IBM now has negative tangible net worth. "I didn't see that coming in 1999 when I wrote whatever I wrote there." The investment implication: the sustainable ROE of the S&P 500 is genuinely higher than the historical 4–6% of-GDP profit band implied — not because of financial engineering alone, but because dominant businesses no longer need capital to be dominant. *Munger:* "Warren may have predicted a little wrong when he wrote that very scholarly article. He didn't invest wrong. And so it just shows that it's hard to make these economic predictions." See [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md), [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md).

**A19 · AIG retroactive reinsurance: "we still feel OK about it."** *Buffett:* the $10.2B 2017 deal (Berkshire pays 80% of AIG's losses above $25B on pre-2016 domestic business, to a cap of $20B additional exposure) is tracking within projections. AIG has paid $15B+ on this book; payments naturally taper as years pass. "Everybody is wrong one way or the other when you estimate losses that may not get settled for 20 or 30 years. But so far, on the group as a whole of these deals we've done, we've been OK." Berkshire's structural edge: probably the only insurer in the world that AIG would trust to honor a long-tail $20B commitment. See [float](../concepts/float.md), [national-indemnity](../entities/national-indemnity.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**★ A20 · Consumer products: "we really want products where people feel like kissing you."** *Buffett:* asked how to distinguish between lasting consumer franchises (American Express vs Diners Club; Coke vs RC Cola). Observation: American Express entered the charge-card market after Diners Club but charged higher, not lower, prices — the centurion logo conferred status. "if you were a salesperson out with somebody, and you could pull out that American Express card with that centurion, you looked like JP Morgan. And if you pulled out the Diners Club, it had a whole bunch of flashy symbols, you looked like a guy that was kiting his checks." On See's: "if you live in California and you were a teenage boy, and you went to your girlfriend's house and you gave the box of candy to her or to her mother or father and she kissed you — you lose price sensitivity at that point. So we really want products where people feel like kissing you rather than slapping you." The scuttlebutt method (Phil Fisher, *Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits*, c. 1958): go out and observe consumer behavior directly; Combs and Weschler do this systematically. See [sees-candies](../entities/sees-candies.md), [american-express](../entities/american-express.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**A21 · Hostile bids: not "evil" but not Berkshire's style.** *Buffett:* not all 500 Fortune 500 companies are run optimally; there is nothing fundamentally wrong with hostile takeovers. "We just won't do it... we're counting on [management] to run the company, and we're not bringing in a whole bunch of people that know how to change businesses." Have withheld Coke votes on compensation; voted against a few proposals in 50 years. "The stockholders still own the company." *Munger:* "I don't envy these people that are in these unfriendly uproars all the time. Imagine doing that after you're already rich. It's insane." See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

**A22 · International acquisitions: "not on the radar screen" the way U.S. is.** *Buffett:* disappointed in international deal flow. ISCAR (Israel, 2006) is the model — Eitan Wertheimer wrote a letter, a remarkable business resulted. But European and Asian sellers do not reflexively think of Berkshire the way U.S. sellers do. "They know about us, they know we've got a lot of money and they know we like to buy things. But we really, we're on the radar screen big-time in the United States, and we're not as — we don't — the immediate desire to be sure that they've thought about the Berkshire option does not occur the same way outside." Also: European 3%-disclosure thresholds make building large positions mechanically difficult. *Munger:* leveraged-buyout buyers can "finance practically anything in about a week" — a fundamental structural disadvantage for Berkshire in competitive international auctions. See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**★ A23 · Investing doesn't require advanced learning — just disciplined internalization.** *Buffett:* Chapter 8 of *The Intelligent Investor* (Mr. Market) and Chapter 20 (margin of safety) contain what matters. "This is not — what we do is not a complicated business. It's got to be a disciplined business, but it does not require a super IQ or anything of the sort... I'd take the person from chapter eight who had it in their bones, basically, over the top five graduates of number one, two, or three of the business schools." Business schools perpetuate wrong frameworks for the same reason an eye doctor did obsolete cataract surgery: "It's such a wonderful operation to teach." *Munger:* on Graham's personal evolution: "Ben Graham recognized that the exact way he sought undervalued companies wouldn't necessarily work for all times under all conditions." Morphed into "trying to buy the better companies when they were underpriced, instead of the lousy companies when they were underpriced." And: "If you're going to live a long time, you have to keep learning. What you formerly knew is never enough. So if you don't learn to constantly revise your earlier conclusions and get better, you're like a one-legged man in an ass-kicking contest." See [margin-of-safety](../concepts/margin-of-safety.md), [ben-graham](../people/ben-graham.md), [cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses](../concepts/cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses.md).

**★ A24 · Women entering the workforce: "makes me bullish on the human race."** *Buffett:* the historical exclusion of women from most professional opportunities was "enormously to my advantage when I was a kid" because the teaching profession attracted higher talent than it otherwise would. Now: "we have more selection by merit, rather than by gender or race or inheritance." Of the handful of CEO appointments Buffett made personally in his last five years before the management changes: "About half of them have been women, which is about what should turn out to be the case in terms of ability." Mrs. B (Rose Blumkin) ran Nebraska Furniture Mart to age 103 and lived to 104. *Munger:* "the world has really changed. And it was totally different. And it was ridiculous." The broader economic lesson: systems that more broadly deploy the full talent pool compound more efficiently than those that don't. See [american-tailwind](../concepts/american-tailwind.md), [rose-blumkin](../people/rose-blumkin.md).

**A25 · Insurance regulatory limits don't constrain Berkshire buybacks or large acquisitions.** *Buffett:* the $16B in "ordinary dividends" that insurance subsidiaries can declare in 2018 is not a ceiling on what Berkshire can actually do. Extraordinary dividends can be requested from insurance regulators. Alternative structures (partnerships, equity commitments) give additional flexibility. "We can make anything that came along — we could work out how to get it done." The constraint on large deals is price and availability, not capital. *Munger:* "You should not assume that we're constrained by the laws of nature to the amount that we can take out under the statutes now." See [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md).

**A26 · Machine intelligence: "more hype than probable achievement."** *Munger:* machine intelligence has produced genuine achievements (beating the best Go players) but "I think there's more hype in that field than there is probable achievement. I don't think the world is going to be changed that much by machine intelligence. Some, but not hugely." Does not apply to capital allocation: "Generally I'm — I think false precision just does nothing but get you in trouble. You've got to master the general ideas, and you've got to work to improve your judgment slowly." *Buffett:* agrees machine intelligence doesn't bring "much to the table in terms of capital allocation or investing." *Munger:* "You're missing a lot of very remunerative, fee-earning twaddle." See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md).

**★ A27 · "American investors are missing China."** *Munger:* asked why U.S. investors underweight China despite its scale and growth: "you're absolutely right. That we are — American investors are missing China. And they're missing it because it's a long way away, it looks different, they're not used to it, it's complicated, the headlines confuse them. In other words, it just looks too hard, sitting in Omaha, to outsmart the Chinese market." *Buffett:* PetroChina bought ≈14% of the public float (1.4% of the full company) about 15 years ago and did very well. The mechanical difficulty: a 3% disclosure threshold in much of Europe (and similar complications elsewhere) makes building multi-billion positions without telegraphing intentions nearly impossible. Problem is size, not geography. *Munger:* "The Chinese stock market is cheaper than the American market." See [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ A28 · Bezos: "something close to a miracle — and if I think something is a miracle, I tend not to bet on it."** *Buffett:* watched Amazon from the beginning; Bill Gates himself steered him from AltaVista to Google in the early days. "I saw that Google was skipping past AltaVista, and then I wondered if anybody could skip past Google." The circle-of-competence failure was not ignorance of what the companies did — Berkshire was paying Google "$10 or $11 a click" since the early days. It was uncertainty about competitive durability: "in the game where four or five people were slugging it out without making as much money as they could if one company dominated." On Bezos: "What Jeff Bezos has done is something close to a miracle. And the problem is, if I think something will be a miracle, I tend not to bet on it." Underestimated Bezos's ability to execute AWS and retail transformation simultaneously. "I underestimated him." *Munger:* "Some of the age-related stupidity at headquarters has been ameliorated by Ted and Todd joining us. We are looking at the world with the aid of some younger eyes now." See [mistakes-of-omission](../concepts/mistakes-of-omission.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**A30 · Incentive plans: individualized, private, no precise formula.** *Buffett:* asked for specific Berkshire compensation examples. Acknowledges the genuine difficulty: different capital intensities, different basic profitabilities, different growth profiles make a universal formula impossible. Has kept virtually 100% of the managers Berkshire wanted to keep — more from culture fit and sense of mission than from financial formulas alone. "I wish I could give you some precise formulas, but there aren't any." *Munger:* preserving the privacy of individual compensation arrangements is a competitive advantage; "he makes all those decisions personally. He's got every formula in the book. And he keeps them all private. That's our system." See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md), [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

## Connections to other sources

- The $10K→$51M S&P masterclass (M3) is the 2018 formal elaboration of the "American tailwind" framing that Buffett has built across annual letters since the 1990s, here given its sharpest empirical anchoring: a single starting date (March 1942), a single ending date (today), and a gold comparison as the control case. The same tailwind principle underlies the American tailwind discussion at [berkshire-2017-meeting](berkshire-2017-meeting.md) A30 (trade roadkill) and [berkshire-2016-meeting](berkshire-2016-meeting.md) M3 (index fund bet).
- The Apple thesis (A6, M28) completes an arc that began at [berkshire-2016-meeting](berkshire-2016-meeting.md) (Apple stake first disclosed in the 13F after the meeting) and developed at [berkshire-2017-meeting](berkshire-2017-meeting.md) A12 (consumer-franchise not technology). In 2018, Buffett adds the mechanical buyback arithmetic — "5% to 7% without laying out a dime" — that becomes the signature Apple explanation.
- The Wells Fargo defence (M10) extends the 2017 meeting's three-mistake anatomy ([berkshire-2017-meeting](berkshire-2017-meeting.md) M6) by adding the historical precedents: GEICO 1976 and American Express 1963. The 2017 version focused on the CEO-inaction failure; the 2018 version argues that scandal + cleanup creates institutional strength — the counter-argument Buffett will eventually abandon as WFC's problems proved more systemic.
- The Bezos/Amazon admission (A28) revisits the 2017 meeting's "because I was too dumb to realize what was going to happen" formulation ([berkshire-2017-meeting](berkshire-2017-meeting.md) A15) but adds the specific cognitive failure: he identified Amazon as a "miracle" and his own rule is not to bet on miracles. That is a different and more precise diagnosis than mere circle-of-competence failure.
- The asset-light economy observation (A18) extends [berkshire-2017-meeting](berkshire-2017-meeting.md) M23 ("the five largest companies require no capital at all") from a forward-looking structural observation to a retrospective revision of Buffett's 1999 Fortune article — an unusually explicit public admission that a macro prediction was wrong even as the investment results were fine.
- The newspaper decline (A9) updates [berkshire-2012-meeting](berkshire-2012-meeting.md)'s purchase rationale and [berkshire-2013-letter](berkshire-2013-letter.md)'s initial assessment; the 2018 verdict is harsher — "surprised the rate of decline has not moderated" — and the Munger comment ("we may have done it because we both love newspapers") is the most candid acknowledgment of an emotional-rather-than-analytical error in the corpus.
- The Haven healthcare JV (M15) picks up the "tapeworm" analogy first coined at [berkshire-2017-meeting](berkshire-2017-meeting.md) A23 and represents Berkshire's operational attempt to address the problem it had diagnosed. The 2021 dissolution of Haven will complete the circle: systemic moats (M27) proved more durable than even the triumvirate of Buffett, Bezos, and Dimon could overcome.
