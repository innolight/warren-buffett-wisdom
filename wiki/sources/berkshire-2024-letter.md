# Berkshire Hathaway 2024 Annual Letter

Warren Buffett's letter for fiscal year 2024, dated February 22, 2025 — written at 94, with Greg Abel's succession to CEO explicitly imminent ("it won't be long before Greg Abel replaces me … and will be writing the annual letters"). A late-period, doctrine-restating letter organized around a few enduring themes rather than new frameworks: the obligation a CEO owes owners (the "report" ethic and candor about mistakes), the economics of property-casualty insurance and float, the record corporate-tax payment as a parable about reinvestment and compounding, the firm's permanent preference for productive businesses over cash, and the expanding Japanese position. The human centerpiece is the story of **Pete Liegl** of Forest River — an illustration of innate business talent and owner-aligned compensation. Operating earnings reached a record $47.4 billion even though 53% of Berkshire's 189 operating businesses reported lower earnings.

## Key themes

- **The "report" ethic and candor about mistakes.** Buffett opens by distinguishing a mandated filing from a *report* — "what we would wish you to use if our positions were reversed." He follows Tom Murphy's rule, "praise by name, criticize by category," and notes he used the words "mistake" or "error" 16 times across his 2019–23 letters while "many other huge companies have never used either word over that span." Capital-allocation errors come in two flavors — misjudging a business's future economics and misjudging a manager's ability or fidelity (the fidelity disappointments "can approach [the pain] of a failed marriage"). The cardinal sin is **Charlie Munger's "thumb-sucking"** — delaying the correction of a known mistake. "Problems … cannot be wished away." The succession note carries the same ethic forward: Greg Abel "understands that if you start fooling your shareholders, you will soon believe your own baloney and be fooling yourself as well." See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).
- **Winners blossom; mistakes fade.** "A single winning decision can make a breathtaking difference over time" — GEICO as a business decision, Ajit Jain as a managerial decision, Munger as a partner. "Mistakes fade away; winners can forever blossom." The asymmetry justifies tolerating a decent-but-imperfect batting average in both business and personnel selection. See [cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses](../concepts/cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses.md).
- **Talent is largely innate; credentials are not the signal.** "I never look at where a candidate has gone to school. Never!" Pete Liegl (Forest River), Ben Rosner (never past 6th grade), and Bill Gates (left Harvard) are the exhibits. "A very large portion of business talent is innate with nature swamping nurture." Buffett believes in lifelong learning but warns against mistaking the parchment for the ability.
- **Owner-aligned compensation — the Pete Liegl deal.** When Berkshire bought Forest River in 2005, Liegl asked for a $100,000 salary ("I wouldn't want to make more than my boss") plus "an annual bonus of 10% of any earnings above what the company is now delivering" — paid only on *incremental* profit, with an agreed adjustment for capital deployed in acquisitions. The deal, sealed over dinner, ran 19 years to Liegl's death in 2024. The structure is the letter's living example of paying for value actually created over a base, and of the broader Berkshire principle: "if you lose money, so do we." See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).
- **Property-casualty insurance and float.** P/C is "Berkshire's core business," following a financial model "rare — very rare — among giant businesses": payment is received up front and the true cost is learned much later (sometimes 30+ years; asbestos claims 50+ years out). This "money-up-front, loss-payments-later" model funds float but can also let a company report "large but fictitious profits … for many years — even decades," especially in long-tail lines and "if the CEO is an optimist or a crook." Over the past two decades the insurance business generated **$32 billion of after-tax underwriting profit** (~3.3¢ per dollar of sales), and float grew from **$46 billion to $171 billion**, "with intelligent underwriting (and some luck) [having] a reasonable prospect of being costless." P/C also rides a secular tailwind: "P/C insurance growth is dependent on increased economic risk. No risk – no need for insurance" — and modern economies manufacture insurable risk continuously (135 years ago there were no autos; now 300 million U.S. vehicles cause "huge damage daily"). See [float](../concepts/float.md) and [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).
- **Underwriting as disciplined pessimism.** "Properly pricing P/C insurance is part art, part science and is definitely not a business for optimists." Mike Goldberg's standard: underwriters should "daily come to work nervous, but not paralyzed." Berkshire must "shrink when prices are inadequate" and "never write inadequately-priced policies in order to stay in the game. That policy is corporate suicide." Structural advantages: capacity no private insurer can match, willingness to "financially and psychologically handle extreme losses without blinking," and freedom from dependence on reinsurers — "a material and enduring cost advantage." The shift from one-year to six-month auto policies is cited as discipline trading float for "more intelligent underwriting." See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).
- **The record tax payment as a compounding parable.** Sixty years after Buffett took control, Berkshire made four 2024 payments to the IRS totaling **$26.8 billion** — "about 5% of what all of corporate America paid" and more than the U.S. had "ever received from any company." Cumulative payments now exceed **$101 billion**. The crucial enabling fact: across 1965–2024 Berkshire paid **exactly one cash dividend** (January 3, 1967 — $101,755, or 10¢ per A share; "Now it seems like a bad dream"). Sixty years of "continuous reinvestment … combined with the magic of long-term compounding" built the taxable income behind the record. See [american-tailwind](../concepts/american-tailwind.md).
- **Equities over cash, permanently.** "Despite what some commentators currently view as an extraordinary cash position … the great majority of your money remains in equities. That preference won't change." Marketable equities fell from $354B to $272B during 2024 while the value of controlled businesses rose and "remains far greater than the value of the marketable portfolio." Berkshire "will never prefer ownership of cash-equivalent assets over the ownership of good businesses." The rationale is purchasing-power preservation: "Paper money can see its value evaporate if fiscal folly prevails … Fixed-coupon bonds provide no protection against runaway currency," whereas good businesses and people with desired talents "find a way to cope with monetary instability." See [investment-categories](../concepts/investment-categories.md).
- **Ambidextrous ownership.** Berkshire deploys capital in two forms — control (≥80%, usually 100%, of 189 subsidiaries) and minority stakes in "a dozen or so very large and highly profitable businesses with household names such as Apple, American Express, Coca-Cola and Moody's" ($272B at yearend). It is "impartial in [its] choice of equity vehicles." The trade-off: marketable positions are easier to exit when Buffett errs (though Berkshire's size now "diminishes this valuable option"), while controlled businesses can have their capital flows and management directed but "almost never" get sold. "Really outstanding businesses are very seldom offered in their entirety, but small fractions of these gems can be purchased Monday through Friday on Wall Street." See [passive-ownership-advantage](../concepts/passive-ownership-advantage.md).
- **The Japanese position expands.** The five trading houses (ITOCHU, Marubeni, Mitsubishi, Mitsui, Sumitomo), held since July 2019, "operate in a manner somewhat similar to Berkshire itself." Aggregate cost $13.8B against $23.5B market value at yearend; the five agreed to "moderately relax" Berkshire's self-imposed 10% ownership ceiling, so holdings "will likely … increase somewhat." The yen-balanced strategy is reaffirmed: fixed-rate yen-denominated borrowing produces approximate currency-neutrality, with $2.3B of cumulative after-tax gains on the yen debt ($850M in 2024). 2025 expected dividend income ~$812M against ~$135M of yen interest cost. The position is "for many decades." See [japanese-trading-houses](../entities/japanese-trading-houses.md).
- **Capitalism, America, and Uncle Sam.** "America is Exhibit A" — over 235 years, from "a base of only four million people," American savings "wisely deploy[ed]" produced "output beyond the dreams of any colonist." Berkshire participated "in a very minor way … by foregoing dividends." Buffett closes with thanks to "Uncle Sam," a plea to "take care of the many who … get the short straws," and a warning that a stable currency "requires both wisdom and vigilance." Candidly: "America would have been every bit the success it has been if Berkshire had never existed," but "Berkshire would not have achieved its results in any locale except America."

## Entities discussed

- [berkshire-hathaway](../entities/berkshire-hathaway.md) — 189 operating subsidiaries plus ~$272B marketable portfolio; record $47.4B operating earnings; record $26.8B / cumulative $101B+ tax payments; the 60-year reinvestment-and-compounding arc from a dying textile mill
- [geico](../entities/geico.md) — the long-held "gem that needed major repolishing"; Todd Combs's five-year overhaul (efficiency, modernized underwriting) drove a "spectacular" 2024 insurance result; cited as the canonical "single winning decision"
- [national-indemnity](../entities/national-indemnity.md) — Berkshire's P/C insurance flagship (not named in the 2024 letter, which discusses P/C broadly as "Berkshire's core business"); the firm-wide P/C figures cited belong here as the flagship: $32B after-tax underwriting profit over two decades (~3.3¢ per dollar of sales), float $46B → $171B
- [bnsf](../entities/bnsf.md) — railroad operation; aggregate earnings improved but "much left to accomplish"
- [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md) — utility operation (Berkshire Hathaway Energy); ownership raised from ~92% to 100% late in 2024 for ~$3.9B ($2.9B cash, balance in B shares); "much left to accomplish"
- [japanese-trading-houses](../entities/japanese-trading-houses.md) — the five sōgō shōsha; cost $13.8B / value $23.5B; relaxed 10% ceiling; yen-balanced funding; 2025 dividends ~$812M vs. ~$135M yen interest
- [apple](../entities/apple.md), [american-express](../entities/american-express.md), [coca-cola](../entities/coca-cola.md) — named (with Moody's) as the household-name minority holdings earning "very high returns on … net tangible equity"

## Notable quotes

> Our goal is to communicate with you in a manner that we would wish you to use if our positions were reversed.

> The cardinal sin is delaying the correction of mistakes or what Charlie Munger called "thumb-sucking." Problems, he would tell me, cannot be wished away.

> If you start fooling your shareholders, you will soon believe your own baloney and be fooling yourself as well.

> Mistakes fade away; winners can forever blossom.

> A very large portion of business talent is innate with nature swamping nurture.

> Well, I looked at Berkshire's proxy statement and I wouldn't want to make more than my boss, so pay me $100,000 per year. … But we will earn X (he named a number) this year, and I would like an annual bonus of 10% of any earnings above what the company is now delivering. [Pete Liegl]

> In "long-tail" lines, a P/C insurer may report large but fictitious profits to its owners and regulators for many years – even decades. The accounting can be particularly dangerous if the CEO is an optimist or a crook.

> We must never write inadequately-priced policies in order to stay in the game. That policy is corporate suicide.

> We want our underwriters to daily come to work nervous, but not paralyzed. [Mike Goldberg]

> Properly pricing P/C insurance is part art, part science and is definitely not a business for optimists.

> If Berkshire had sent the Treasury a $1 million check every 20 minutes throughout all of 2024 … we still would have owed the federal government a significant sum at yearend.

> Berkshire will never prefer ownership of cash-equivalent assets over the ownership of good businesses, whether controlled or only partially owned.

> Paper money can see its value evaporate if fiscal folly prevails. … Fixed-coupon bonds provide no protection against runaway currency.

> EBITDA, a flawed favorite of Wall Street, is not for us.
