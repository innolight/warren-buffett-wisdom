# Berkshire Hathaway 1985 Annual Letter

Dated March 4, 1986. The letter chronicles a 48.2% gain in net worth ($613.6M) — "the visit of Halley's Comet coincided with this percentage gain: neither will be seen again in my lifetime." Per-share book value compounds from $19.46 (1964) to $1,643.71 — 23.2% over 21 years. Four marquee events frame the year: the Cap Cities/ABC commitment, the Scott & Fetzer acquisition, the Fireman's Fund quota-share, and the General Foods sale to Philip Morris. Underneath them, the letter delivers two extended set-pieces of timeless investment writing: the textile-shutdown obituary (with the Burlington Industries case study and the auction proceeds) and the incentive-compensation essay (the savings-account analogy, the double-standard critique, and the three structural fixes).

## Key themes

- **The textile shutdown as a final commodity-business lesson.** Burlington Industries' 21-year capital-expenditure record ($3B+ on a starting $60 stock) used as the canonical illustration that brilliant capital allocation in a structurally bad business still produces a bad outcome. The auction's terminal proof: $13M of equipment, $30–50M replacement value, $866K book value, $163K gross proceeds — net less than zero.
- **The incentive-compensation essay.** A full critique of fixed-price ten-year stock options, the savings-account analogy, the double standard, and Berkshire's alternative: uncapped unit-specific bonuses, no seniority adjustment, no coupling to Berkshire's stock price.
- **Insurance commodity dynamics deepened.** "Capacity is an attitudinal concept, not a physical fact" — capital begets comfort begets capacity, and capacity returns instantly when capital is raised. The reinsurance lay-off game collapses: issuing companies that wrote large gross lines while keeping small net lines profited at reinsurers' expense; with reinsurers retreating, gross capacity has converged on net, and Berkshire's strong balance sheet is now an asset rather than an idle differentiator.
- **The Washington Post "triple-dip."** Business value soared, repurchases compounded per-share value, narrowing discount lifted price faster than per-share value. The clean illustration of three reinforcing tailwinds operating simultaneously.
- **The three-business economic-goodwill math.** NFM + See's + News went from $8M to $72M aggregate pre-tax earnings (1970 → 1985) on only $40M of additional invested capital. The average American business would have required $300M of additional capital ($5 of capital per $1 of additional earnings).
- **Capital allocation through patient passive blocks.** The Cap Cities $172.50 commitment with Murphy voting Berkshire's shares — same model as GEICO and WPC — promotes managerial stability over the financial premium a free-floating block would command. Buffett's ambivalence about the price ("a full price… it's no field for bargains") is accepted as the cost of getting the people right.

## Notable quotes

> A horse that can count to ten is a remarkable horse — not a remarkable mathematician. Likewise, a textile company that allocates capital brilliantly within its industry is a remarkable textile company — but not a remarkable business.

> When a management with a reputation for brilliance tackles a business with a reputation for poor fundamental economics, it is the reputation of the business that remains intact.

> Should you find yourself in a chronically-leaking boat, energy devoted to changing vessels is likely to be more productive than energy devoted to patching leaks.

> A good managerial record (measured by economic returns) is far more a function of what business boat you get into than it is of how effectively you row.

> The economic goodwill attributable to two paper routes in Buffalo — or a single See's candy store — considerably exceeds the proceeds we received from this massive collection of tangible assets that not too many years ago, under different competitive conditions, was able to employ over 1,000 people.

> Many corporate compensation plans reward managers handsomely for earnings increases produced solely, or in large part, by retained earnings — i.e., earnings withheld from owners.

> The .350 hitter expects, and also deserves, a big payoff for his performance — even if he plays for a cellar-dwelling team. And the .150 hitter should get no reward — even if he plays for a pennant winner.

> In any business, insurance or otherwise, "except for" should be excised from the lexicon. If you are going to play the game, you must count the runs scored against you in all nine innings.

> Managers who have learned much from personal experience in the past usually are destined to learn much from personal experience in the future. [On learning from others' mistakes, not just one's own.]

> Capacity is an attitudinal concept, not a physical fact. [On insurance underwriting capacity.]

## The textile shutdown — figures of record

- Aggregate sales 1955–1964 (Berkshire/Hathaway merged years): $530M; aggregate loss $10M.
- Equipment original cost: ~$13M (including $2M of 1980–84 reinvestment).
- 1985 book value of the equipment (after accelerated depreciation): $866,000.
- Replacement-new value: $30–50M.
- Gross sale proceeds at the 1986 auction: $163,122.
- Net proceeds after pre- and post-sale costs: **less than zero**.
- Looms purchased for $5,000 each in 1981 sold for scrap at $26 each.
- Industry context: 250 textile mills closed since 1980. "Their owners were not privy to any information that was unknown to me; they simply processed it more objectively."

## Burlington Industries — the 21-year case study

| Metric | 1964 | 1985 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sales | $1.2B | $2.8B |
| Stock price | $60 | $34 (split-adjusted) |
| Cumulative capex 1964–85 | — | ~$3B ($200/share on a $60 stock) |
| Real per-share purchasing power | 1.00× | ~0.33× (CPI tripled) |

> A very large part of the expenditures, I am sure, was devoted to cost improvement and expansion. Given Burlington's basic commitment to stay in textiles, I would also surmise that the company's capital decisions were quite rational… Nevertheless, Burlington has lost sales volume in real dollars and has far lower returns on sales and equity now than 20 years ago.

The textile-industry capital-investment dynamic itself: "each company's capital investment decision appeared cost-effective and rational; viewed collectively, the decisions neutralized each other and were irrational (just as happens when each person watching a parade decides he can see a little better if he stands on tiptoes)."

## Incentive compensation — the savings-account analogy

A trustee manages a $100,000 account at 8% interest with the discretion to set the payout ratio. At a 25% payout ratio over ten years:

- Account balance: $179,084 (up 79%).
- Annual earnings: $8,000 → $13,515 (up 69%).
- Annual "dividends": $2,000 → $3,378 (up 69%).
- Every chart marches skyward.

Now grant the trustee a ten-year fixed-price option on a portion of the account based on year-one fair value. The trustee captures substantial profit purely from retaining earnings — no skill, no risk, no carrying cost. "If he were both Machiavellian and a bit of a mathematician, your manager might also have cut the pay-out ratio once he was firmly entrenched."

The double standard: "Ten months, in fact, would be regarded as extreme" for an option granted to outsiders. Ten *years* to managers themselves is unremarkable.

Three structural fixes Buffett offers:

1. **Scope.** Options only to managers with overall responsibility. "The .350 hitter… even if he plays for a cellar-dwelling team."
2. **Structure.** Build a retained-earnings or carrying-cost factor into the strike — otherwise the option holder benefits purely from compound interest.
3. **Pricing.** Strike at true business value, not depressed market prices. Managers freely cite "unrealistic market prices" when fielding outside bids — but then quietly use those same prices when granting options to themselves.

Buffett's caveat: some managers he admires use fixed-price options well within strong owner-oriented cultures. "If it ain't broke, don't fix it" preferable to "purity at any price."

### Berkshire's alternative

- Bonuses tied to performance against targets in each manager's own bailiwick. See's does well → See's incentives pay; News does poorly → News incentives don't.
- No coupling to Berkshire's stock price.
- No caps on bonuses; top managers can receive 5× base salary or more; one bonus could top $2M in 1986.
- No seniority/age adjustment. A 20-year-old hitting .300 is as valuable as a 40-year-old hitting .300.
- A small-unit manager can earn more than a large-unit manager.
- Managers may use bonus or borrowed money to buy Berkshire stock in the market — they walk in the shoes of owners by *purchase*, not by *grant*.

See [[incentive-compensation]].

## The three businesses — economic-goodwill arithmetic

| | Aggregate pre-tax earnings | Additional capital required (vs. 1970) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| NFM + See's + News, ~1970 | $8M | — |
| NFM + See's + News, 1985 | $72M | ~$40M |
| Average American business (notional) | $72M | ~$300M (at $5 capital per $1 earnings) |

Per-business 1985 pre-tax earnings: See's $28.99M, Buffalo News $29.92M, NFM $12.69M.

Operating notes:
- **NFM.** Sets a new sales record despite the depressed farm economy. Rose Blumkin at 92, "wheeling and dealing seven days a week."
- **See's.** Per-store volumes "far beyond those achieved by any competitor we know of." Industry-wide same-store poundage slipping; price increases now "only modestly" to defend the customer franchise.
- **Buffalo News.** ROP linage declined; preprints (lower-margin, more vulnerable) drove the headline gain. Penetration "continues to be exceptional."

## Insurance — the 1985 dynamics

Industry combined ratio 118.0 (1985 estimate), vs. 117.9 in 1984. The expense surge in 1985 driven by (a) belated reserve catch-ups ("I've sinned, I've sinned"), (b) "social" or "judicial" inflation — deep-pocket awards lacking precedent, (c) 17% growth in insured losses against slowing CPI inflation. Catastrophes were not the culprit (~2% of premium volume, normal).

Berkshire's premium volume "more than tripled" — a step-function up off a long stagnation. Drivers:

- **Flight to quality** by large buyers who learned in 1985 that "a policy is only an IOU."
- **Net underwriting capacity** that brokers cannot find elsewhere. Berkshire willing to risk $10M of its own money on a single uncorrelated event; "Very few insurers are willing to risk half that much on single events."
- The collapse of the **lay-off-at-a-profit game**: one major issuer cited as a case where 1984 retained business produced a $200M underwriting loss (combined ratio under 110) while the laid-off $3.5B produced a $1.5B reinsurer loss (combined ratio over 140). With reinsurers retreating, gross capacity has converged on net capacity, and net capacity is generally small.

The combined ratio for Berkshire's own insurance group improved from 134 (1984) to 111 (1985) — but loss-reserve development on 1984 numbers remained adverse, "as it did in the several years preceding." Buffett's wry self-rebuke: "Naturally, I made this promise [to update annually on loss-development] thinking my future record would be much improved. So far this has not been the case."

The "Except-For Insurance Company" anecdote — General Reinsurance's old chairman's joke about managers who place all their unwanted business in a hypothetical separate company — closes the section with: "the real mistake is not the act, but the actor."

## Fireman's Fund quota-share

A 7% participation in all FFIC in-force business as of September 1, 1985, four-year term. Following Jack Byrne (see [[jack-byrne]]) — "never one to let go of a meal ticket." Berkshire holds the float between premium receipt and loss payment; Buffett is available for general investment consultation only, not specific decisions and not underwriting. FFIC unearned premium reserve $1.324B → 7% transfer of $92.7M to Berkshire, less $29.4M of pre-incurred underwriting expense. All FFIC business written through National Indemnity; two-sevenths of Berkshire's share passed to Wes-FIC, a new Wesco-organized subsidiary.

Subsequent to year-end, a second quota-share contract with 1986 volume "over $50M."

## GEICO

A leadership transition. Jack Byrne leaves GEICO at mid-year to head Fireman's Fund. **Bill Snyder** succeeds as chairman; **Lou Simpson** becomes vice chairman. Buffett's recurring praise of Byrne now emphasizes the succession itself: "he found managers to succeed him who have talents as valuable as his own." Premium growth and investment results good; underwriting weaker than GEICO's standards but "far better than that of virtually all its major competitors."

## Washington Post — the triple-dip

> We had a triple dip: the company's business value soared upward, per-share business value increased considerably faster because of stock repurchases and, with a narrowing of the discount, the stock price outpaced the gain in per-share business value.

Position recap: $10.6M cost (1973), $8M market by yearend 1974 (a 25% loss), $221M total value at yearend 1985 (including the 1985 proportional-redemption proceeds). Counterfactual: any of half-a-dozen "investment favorite" media companies of mid-1973 would have produced ~$40–60M today; the additional ~$160M is attributed in large part to Kay Graham's repurchase decisions and operating skill.

> Most institutional investors in the early 1970s… regarded business value as of only minor relevance when they were deciding the prices at which they would buy or sell. This now seems hard to believe. However, these institutions were then under the spell of academics at prestigious business schools who were preaching a newly-fashioned theory: the stock market was totally efficient, and therefore calculations of business value — and even thought, itself — were of no importance in investment activities. (We are enormously indebted to those academics: what could be more advantageous in an intellectual contest — whether it be bridge, chess, or stock selection — than to have opponents who have been taught that thinking is a waste of energy?)

## Capital Cities/ABC

3,000,000 shares at $172.50 = $517.5M, the commitment made early in March 1985, closing right after 1985 year-end. The financing helps Cap Cities fund the $3.5B ABC acquisition. Buffett's recurring praise of Tom Murphy and Dan Burke ("the best of any publicly-owned company in the country"; "the sort of fellows that you would want your daughter to marry"). The structural feature worth memorializing:

- **Murphy (or Burke as CEO) votes Berkshire's stock for an extended period.**
- **Berkshire is restricted in the sale of its shares** to prevent the block landing with an unfriendly large holder.
- Same structural model as the [[geico]] and [[washington-post]] arrangements.

> We feel the long-term economic prospects for these businesses — and, thus, for ourselves as owners — are enhanced by the arrangements. With them in place, the first-class managers with whom we have aligned ourselves can focus their efforts entirely upon running the businesses and maximizing long-term values for owners. Certainly this is much better than having those managers distracted by "revolving-door capitalists" hoping to put the company "in play".

A self-deprecating note: "some of you probably wonder why we are now buying Cap Cities at $172.50 per share given that your Chairman, in a characteristic burst of brilliance, sold Berkshire's holdings in the same company at $43 per share in 1978-80… I spent much of 1985 working on a snappy answer that would reconcile these acts. A little more time, please."

## Scott & Fetzer

Acquired right after year-end for ~$320M (plus ~$90M of existing debt). Sales ~$700M from 17 businesses, led by **World Book** (~40% of sales, "by far the leader in its industry, selling more than twice as many encyclopedia sets annually as its nearest competitor"). Other names: Kirby, Campbell Hausfeld, Wayne. **Ralph Schey**, CEO of nine years, who narrowed Scott Fetzer from 31 businesses to a focused portfolio while preserving the right ones (World Book acquired 1978). The deal history Buffett enjoys: an ESOP-led sale process failed mid-1985; Buffett wrote a single letter to Schey on October 10, dinner October 22, contract signed within the week. See [[scott-fetzer]].

## The six-point acquisition ad — 1985 version

The threshold for after-tax earnings raised — now "at least $10 million." All other criteria preserved. See [[acquisition-criteria]].

## General Foods — sold to Philip Morris

$338M pre-tax realized gain (of $488M total securities-sale gain in 1985). Position originally built 1980 "at a price far below what we felt was their per-share business value." Buffett credits four factors: (1) bargain purchase, (2) good underlying economics, (3) able shareholder-oriented management (Jim Ferguson, Phil Smith), (4) a buyer willing to pay full business value. "In selecting common stocks, we devote our attention to attractive purchases, not to the possibility of attractive sales."
