# Berkshire Hathaway 1985 Annual Letter

The 1985 letter, dated March 4, 1986, contains two of the most referenced pieces of writing in any Berkshire letter: the textile-shutdown obituary and the incentive-compensation essay. The first closes Berkshire's longest-running mistake and offers Burlington Industries as external proof that the commodity-business trap destroys even well-managed, well-capitalized incumbents. The second dissects how fixed-price ten-year stock options systematically extract owner wealth through retained earnings rather than performance. Around those centrepieces, the letter delivers three further durable ideas: insurance capacity as an "attitudinal concept, not a physical fact"; the NFM–See's–News economic-goodwill arithmetic that operationalizes the 1983 Appendix; and the Washington Post triple-dip as a live illustration of how margin-of-safety purchases compound through multiple reinforcing mechanisms. Net worth rose $613.6M (48.2%), but Buffett explicitly flags that this rate will not recur.

## Key themes

**The leaky-boat principle: business selection beats management quality.** Burlington Industries spent $3B on capital expenditures between 1964 and 1985 — more than $200 per share on a starting $60 stock — with rational, well-managed operations. The result: stock price essentially flat in nominal terms, down two-thirds in real purchasing power. The lesson from Berkshire's own textile auction presses the point from the other direction: machinery with $30–50M replacement value and $866K book value fetched $163K gross (net: less than zero). Management excellence inside a structurally disadvantaged business produces a structurally disadvantaged outcome. The Burlington case gives the principle external validity — not an idiosyncratic Berkshire failure, but an industry-wide dynamic: "each company's capital investment decision appeared cost-effective and rational; viewed collectively, the decisions neutralized each other and were irrational (just as happens when each person watching a parade decides he can see a little better if he stands on tiptoes)." The correct response to a chronically leaking boat is not better bailing.

**Incentive compensation as retained-earnings extraction.** Fixed-price ten-year stock options, granted by companies that reinvest most of their earnings, let managers profit from compound interest without producing any incremental value. A trustee retaining 75% of 8% interest grows account balance 79%, annual earnings 69%, and dividends 69% over ten years — all charts march skyward — while adding no skill. A fixed-price option on that account rewards the trustee for arithmetic, not management. The double standard: outsiders are never granted ten-year fixed-price options on any business; ten months would be "regarded as extreme." Three structural repairs: (1) confine stock options to managers with company-wide responsibility; (2) build a retained-earnings or carrying-cost factor into the strike; (3) price options at true business value, not depressed market prices that managers simultaneously dismiss as unrealistic when fielding outside bids. Berkshire's alternative: uncapped bailiwick-specific bonuses, no coupling to stock price, no seniority adjustment, manager compensation sized to manager performance regardless of unit size.

**Insurance capacity is attitudinal, not physical.** Unlike steel or aluminum, insurance capacity is created the moment capital arrives — more capital, more comfort, more willingness to write. There is no factory to build. This makes insurance cycles sharper and faster than physical-capacity industries: a capital infusion can flood a tight market almost overnight. The 1985 consequence: reinsurers burned by years of premium splits that left them bearing losses while issuing companies kept disproportionate premiums retreated wholesale from long-tail casualty lines. Gross capacity for most issuers converged on their much smaller net capacity. Berkshire, having never played the lay-off-at-a-profit game, held net underwriting capacity when few others did, and premium volume more than tripled. Strong financial position in slack times is not idle capital — it is optionality that pays at the cycle turn. The lesson transfers wherever a commodity market has a capital-only capacity constraint.

**Economic goodwill and the capital-light compounding test.** Nebraska Furniture Mart, See's Candies, and Buffalo News together grew pre-tax earnings from $8M (1970) to $72M (1985) while requiring only $40M of additional invested capital. The average American business, at $5 of additional capital per $1 of additional pre-tax earnings, would have needed over $300M to replicate that result. The test generalizes: when evaluating any earnings growth story, first ask how much capital produced it. Earnings compounding without proportionate capital consumption are the exception. Most earnings growth is retained capital working at ordinary returns — a savings account in disguise, not evidence of business excellence.

**The triple-dip: three reinforcing tailwinds from one undervalued entry.** The Washington Post position (cost $10.6M in mid-1973; value $221M at year-end 1985) illustrates how an intrinsic-value purchase can generate compounding through three simultaneous mechanisms: operating value growth, per-share value growth via buybacks, and price appreciation as the market discount closes. Comparable media companies of 1973 would have produced roughly $40–60M — the extra ~$160M flowed from Kay Graham's repurchase discipline and operating skill. The precondition was an entry price created by an EMT-suppressed market: institutions taught that value calculations were pointless created the gap. This letter contains the fullest articulation of why the academic efficient-market consensus created opportunity rather than danger for those who disagreed.

## Entities discussed

- [berkshire-hathaway](../entities/berkshire-hathaway.md) — net worth gain $613.6M (48.2%); per-share book value compounded 23.2% annually since 1964; insurance premium volume more than tripled
- [capital-cities](../entities/capital-cities.md) — 3M shares at $172.50 ($517.5M) to help finance Cap Cities' $3.5B ABC acquisition; Tom Murphy votes Berkshire's shares for an extended period; Berkshire restricted from selling the block to hostile parties — same model as GEICO and Washington Post
- [scott-fetzer](../entities/scott-fetzer.md) — acquired for ~$320M; ~$700M in sales from 17 businesses; World Book (~40% of sales) the dominant industry leader; CEO Ralph Schey found via a one-page cold letter from Buffett
- [geico](../entities/geico.md) — Byrne departs mid-year for Fireman's Fund; Bill Snyder (chairman) and Lou Simpson (vice chairman) succeed; Buffett's noted observation: Byrne "found managers to succeed him who have talents as valuable as his own"
- [washington-post](../entities/washington-post.md) — triple-dip case study; $10.6M cost (1973) → $221M value (1985); ~$160M of outperformance vs. comparable media companies attributed to Kay Graham's repurchase discipline and operating skill
- [buffalo-evening-news](../entities/buffalo-evening-news.md) — $29.9M pre-tax 1985; household penetration exceptional; ROP linage declining while lower-margin preprints drove headline growth
- [sees-candies](../entities/sees-candies.md) — $29.0M pre-tax 1985; industry poundage declining same-store; price increases now "only modestly" to protect consumer franchise
- [nebraska-furniture-mart](../entities/nebraska-furniture-mart.md) — $12.7M pre-tax 1985; new sales record despite depressed farm economy

## Notable quotes

> A horse that can count to ten is a remarkable horse — not a remarkable mathematician. Likewise, a textile company that allocates capital brilliantly within its industry is a remarkable textile company — but not a remarkable business.

> When a management with a reputation for brilliance tackles a business with a reputation for poor fundamental economics, it is the reputation of the business that remains intact.

> Should you find yourself in a chronically-leaking boat, energy devoted to changing vessels is likely to be more productive than energy devoted to patching leaks.

> The economic goodwill attributable to two paper routes in Buffalo — or a single See's candy store — considerably exceeds the proceeds we received from this massive collection of tangible assets that not too many years ago, under different competitive conditions, was able to employ over 1,000 people.

> Many corporate compensation plans reward managers handsomely for earnings increases produced solely, or in large part, by retained earnings — i.e., earnings withheld from owners.

> The .350 hitter expects, and also deserves, a big payoff for his performance — even if he plays for a cellar-dwelling team. And the .150 hitter should get no reward — even if he plays for a pennant winner.

> Capacity is an attitudinal concept, not a physical fact.

> In any business, insurance or otherwise, "except for" should be excised from the lexicon. If you are going to play the game, you must count the runs scored against you in all nine innings.

> Most institutional investors in the early 1970s… regarded business value as of only minor relevance when they were deciding the prices at which they would buy or sell… these institutions were then under the spell of academics at prestigious business schools who were preaching a newly-fashioned theory: the stock market was totally efficient, and therefore calculations of business value — and even thought, itself — were of no importance in investment activities. (We are enormously indebted to those academics: what could be more advantageous in an intellectual contest — whether it be bridge, chess, or stock selection — than to have opponents who have been taught that thinking is a waste of energy?)
