# Berkshire Hathaway 2005 Annual Letter

Letter dated February 28, 2006. Net worth gain of $5.6 billion; book value per share +6.4% for both Class A and B. Forty-one years of Buffett management: book value from $19 to $59,377, compounded at 21.5% annually.

## Key themes

- **Hurricane losses absorbed without cost to float.** Katrina ($2.5B), Rita and Wilma ($0.9B) — Berkshire's largest-ever catastrophe year — yet GEICO's outstanding results made float costless for the year as a whole. The letter's handling is calm: loss acknowledged precisely, consequences absorbed, and a sharpened pricing discipline articulated for future mega-cat coverage.
- **GEICO's productivity flywheel.** Tony Nicely's two-year run: 32% productivity improvement, employment −4%, policy count +26%. Market share 5.6%→6.1%. Advertising $502M (up from $31M in 1996). Record conversion rates.
- **Five acquisitions, all for cash.** MedPro (medical malpractice insurer), Forest River (RV manufacturer), Business Wire, Applied Underwriters, and PacifiCorp (via MidAmerican). "None of the deals involve the issuance of Berkshire shares. That's a crucial, but often ignored, point."
- **MidAmerican: PUHCA repeal and PacifiCorp.** The 1935 regulatory constraint on utility holding companies dissolved; Berkshire's preferred converts to voting common, bringing the stake to 83.4%. PacifiCorp (1.6M customers in six western states) cleared regulatory approvals; Berkshire to invest $3.4B.
- **Clayton: industry at 40-year lows; Clayton alone earns.** Loan portfolio grew from $5.4B at acquisition to $17B by 2005. Clayton the only manufactured-housing participant earning significant money.
- **Gen Re derivatives near extinction.** 741 contracts remaining (from 23,218 at peak). $104M loss in 2005; $404M aggregate. "When a problem exists, whether in personnel or in business operations, the time to act is now."
- **Gillette → P&G.** Merger closed Q4 2005; $5B GAAP capital gain described as "meaningless from an economic standpoint." Jim Kilts praised for the turnaround. Berkshire now holds 100M P&G shares.
- **CEO compensation essay.** "Ratchet, Ratchet and Bingo" — the Fred Futile / Stagnant Inc. worked example showing how fixed-price options produce CEO enrichment even from zero business improvement. "Nothing succeeds like failure" in the executive suite.
- **"How to Minimize Investment Returns."** The Gotrocks allegory — the most rigorous statement in the letters of why frictional costs systematically reduce aggregate investor returns below business earnings. Newton's Fourth Law: "For investors as a whole, returns decrease as motion increases."
- **Currency bets: $955M 2005 loss, +$2.0B since inception.** Buffett's dollar/trade-deficit position cost $955M pre-tax in 2005 — included directly in reported earnings because long-term currency positions are marked-to-market daily. By contrast, long-term equity positions (e.g., Coca-Cola's swing from $1B to $13.4B and back to $8.1B) are never reflected in earnings unless sold. The asymmetry is GAAP's, not Berkshire's. Buffett shifted the non-dollar exposure toward foreign-company equities rather than direct currency contracts, avoiding the negative carry that dollar-denominated currency shorts had begun generating.
- **Debt philosophy restated.** Three narrow exceptions; "a long string of impressive numbers multiplied by a single zero always equals zero."
- **Management succession.** Board has unanimously named a CEO successor (kept private). Buffett explicitly describes the post-Buffett architecture: CEO and investment manager will be separate roles — the CEO will hire a talented individual for the investment function (as GEICO has done for 26 years). This is a structural governance design disclosure, not merely succession reassurance.

## Notable quotes

> For investors as a whole, returns decrease as motion increases.

> Fault me for dithering. (Charlie calls it thumb-sucking.) When a problem exists, whether in personnel or in business operations, the time to act is now.

> It's hard to overemphasize the importance of who is CEO of a company.

> Over the years, a number of very smart people have learned the hard way that a long string of impressive numbers multiplied by a single zero always equals zero.

> It's a strange world in which two parties can carry out a paper transaction that each can promptly report as profitable.

> We are a canary in this business coal mine and should sing a song of warning as we expire.

> Every day, in countless ways, the competitive position of each of our businesses grows either weaker or stronger. If we are delighting customers, eliminating unnecessary costs and improving our products and services, we gain strength. But if we treat customers with indifference or tolerate bloat, our businesses will wither. On a daily basis, the effects of our actions are imperceptible; cumulatively, though, their consequences are enormous.

> Today, in the executive suite, the all-too-prevalent rule is that nothing succeeds like failure.
