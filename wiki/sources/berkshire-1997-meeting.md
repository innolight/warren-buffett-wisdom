# Berkshire Hathaway 1997 Annual Meeting

The 1997 meeting is held with a bull market roaring and the Dow near 7,000, and its signature lesson is a discipline against the very enthusiasm in the room: **a wonderful business bought at a foolish price is still a way to lose money.** Having just labelled Coke and Gillette "The Inevitables" in the annual report, Buffett spends the day walking the qualifier back — "you can pay too much, at least in the short run, for businesses like that" — and Munger guarantees that "real inflation-adjusted returns … will be lower in the long-term future than they've been in the last 15 years." Around that warning, the pair restate the load-bearing Berkshire ideas with unusual freshness: risk is *business* risk and volatility is the investor's friend; opportunity cost — "would I rather own this than more Coca-Cola?" — is the master filter; the durable moat is *share of mind*, not share of market; the big money is lost in mistakes of *omission*; and 22% returns on equity cannot persist in a 7% world. Per the wiki's two-layer convention, a short distilled top of the durable themes precedes a **comprehensive Q&A digest** that captures every value-bearing exchange.

## Key themes

### A wonderful business can still be a terrible purchase — price discipline on the Inevitables

The meeting's organizing tension: Buffett had named Coke and Gillette "The Inevitables" and now insists the label is not a buy recommendation. "No matter how wonderful a business it is … there always is a risk that you will pay a price where it will take a few years for the business to catch up with the stock. That the stock can get ahead of the business" (M6). The qualifier is a confession of his own evolution — "I originally was incredibly price conscious. We used to have prayer meetings before we would raise our bid an eighth … But that was a mistake," yet certainty about the *business* now matters more than shaving the last 5–10% off the *price*. The two cut against each other and the synthesis is judgment, not formula. By M26 he reports the lesson playing out in real time: Berkshire's own stock "after years of overperforming the business somewhat, underperformed the business" in 1996 as intrinsic value caught up — exactly what a stock that got ahead of itself must eventually do. See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [margin-of-safety](../concepts/margin-of-safety.md).

### Risk is business risk, not volatility — and volatility is the investor's friend

Buffett's cleanest meeting-floor restatement: "we think first in terms of business risk" — the chance the *enterprise* is permanently impaired by a bad capital structure, long lead times, heavy capital needs, or commodity economics (the textile mill that "wasn't the low-cost producer"). Price volatility is not risk; it is opportunity. "It doesn't make any difference to us whether the volatility … averages a half a percent a day or … 5 percent a day. In fact, we'd make a lot more money if volatility was higher, because it would create more mistakes." Ben Graham's Mr. Market is "an obliging partner who comes around every day" — and "it's a bigger advantage if this partner of yours is a heavy-drinking manic depressive … the crazier he is, the more money you're going to make" (M12). The capstone rule: "the stock market is there to serve you, and not to instruct you." Munger's coda damns the academy: finance professors "got volatility mixed up with a lot of foolish mathematics" and his advice on risk-adjusted return is to "totally ignore this development." See [risk-vs-volatility](../concepts/risk-vs-volatility.md), [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md), [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md).

### Opportunity cost is the master filter — measure every idea against the best one you already own

The day's most repeated mental model. Munger states it plainly (A2): "If you have one opportunity that you already have available in large quantity, and you like it better than 98 percent of the other things you see … you can just screen out the other 98 percent because you already know something better." Buffett's operating version: "the first thing that goes through our head is would we rather own this business than more Coca-Cola? Would we rather own it than more Gillette?" The same filter governs whole-company managers — if every CEO asked "is this acquisition better than buying in our own stock, or buying Coca-Cola, there'd be a lot fewer deals done." The risk-free government-bond rate is a *yardstick* in this comparison, "merely to equate one item to another," not a thing they want to own (A12). Munger reduces the whole edifice to life advice: "life is a whole series of opportunity costs. You got to marry the best person who is convenient to find who will have you. Investment is much the same" (A14). See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md), [concentration-vs-diversification](../concepts/concentration-vs-diversification.md).

### Stay inside the circle — you only need a few, and you only need to understand a few

Asked whether copying his method with Intel and Microsoft would double his returns, Buffett declines without disdain: "there's a whole group of companies … that Charlie and I just don't know how to value. And that doesn't bother us." Understanding means "you've got a pretty good idea where it's going to be in ten years," and he can get that conviction for "relatively few" businesses — "but I only need a few" (M17). The candor is total: Bob Noyce and Bill Gates "are great teachers but I'm a lousy student"; Munger adds that "having flunked when we were young … at understanding some complex businesses, we're not looking to master what we earlier failed at … in our latter years." The same humility names a costly blind spot — the pharmaceutical industry he "could've bought the whole industry and done very well" but didn't (A24). The discipline is to define the circle honestly and act only inside it. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

### The durable moat is share of mind, not share of market

Buffett's fullest spoken account of the consumer-brand moat (A17). "It's share of mind. It's not share of market. It's share of mind that counts." Coca-Cola occupies a favorable position in the minds of perhaps three-quarters of humanity, "associated with pleasant experiences," reinforced a fraction more each year — a position you could not buy "by a billion dollar advertising budget or a $3 billion advertising budget or hiring 20,000 super salesmen." Disney means something "to billions of people," so the harried parent grabs the Disney video without previewing it. The See's lesson generalizes the principle to *why ponds beat swimming*: "what pond you jumped in was probably more important than how well you could swim." And the moat is a *learned* recognition — See's "was the first time we really stepped up for brand quality" (Munger), the experience that made Coca-Cola buyable in 1988. See [moat](../concepts/moat.md), [economic-goodwill](../concepts/economic-goodwill.md), [sees-candies](../entities/sees-candies.md).

### The big money is lost in mistakes of omission — and you must take a big bite when one is obvious

"Most of our mistakes have been mistakes of omission rather than commission" (A11). The largest single category is "being reluctant to pay up a little for a business I knew was really outstanding … the cost of that has been many, many billions." Crucially, the mistakes that *don't* bother him are the ones outside his circle — "I don't worry at all about … when I met Bill Gates, I didn't buy Microsoft … That's not my game." Munger sharpens it into a call to courage: no-brainer opportunities "are very few and they may be separated by periods of years," so "people have to learn to have the courage … to step up in a major way when those rare opportunities come by." Buffett: "you got to be willing to take a really big bite. And it's crazy if you dabble around at the edges." See [mistakes-of-omission](../concepts/mistakes-of-omission.md).

### Macro humility — 22% returns on equity cannot compound in a 7% world

The distinctive 1997 counterweight to the bubble. Corporate America was earning ~22% on equity against a long-run norm of 12–13%, and Buffett admits "I never thought it would happen … if you'd listened to me, you'd have been dead wrong." Munger drives the arithmetic home: "15 percent per annum compounded will … grow way faster than the economy can grow, way faster than aggregate profits can grow" (A16), and "Corporate profits can't be 200 percent of GNP. Indeed, they can't be 50 percent of GNP. So these high rates of compounding just go automatically into absurdity." The valuation follows: today's prices are justified *only if* both record ROEs and low rates persist — neither guaranteed — and "it wouldn't surprise us in the least if stocks averaged 4 percent a year … for the next 10 years" (A36). The thinking tool underneath is to always ask "And then what?" of any economic assertion (A20). See [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md), [return-on-equity-yardstick](../concepts/return-on-equity-yardstick.md).

## Q&A digest — Morning session

**★ M5 · McDonald's is not an "Inevitable."** *Buffett:* the "Inevitables" label fits Coke's soft drinks and Gillette's blades, not food — "you never would get in the food business … quite the inevitability." People vary where they eat; someone who shaves with a Sensor "is very unlikely to go elsewhere." A better *stock* it may still be, "depending on the price." (See A33 for the switching-cost mechanics.) See [moat](../concepts/moat.md), [coca-cola](../entities/coca-cola.md).

**★ M6 · You can pay too much for a wonderful business.** *Buffett:* the annual-report caution was deliberate — "no matter how wonderful a business … there always is a risk that you will pay a price where it will take a few years for the business to catch up with the stock." Certainty about the business now outweighs shaving 5–10% off price; "I originally was incredibly price conscious … But that was a mistake." *Munger:* guarantees real returns "will be lower in the long-term future"; this has been "an unprecedented period," and mean reversion is coming. See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [margin-of-safety](../concepts/margin-of-safety.md).

**M6b · The good premise is more dangerous than the bad one.** *Buffett:* quoting Graham — "you can get in more trouble … with a good premise than with a bad premise, because the bad premise will shout out to you immediately." Falling rates and a strong economy genuinely lifted values, but "the prices themselves take over" and people forget the math (Edgar Lawrence Smith's 1924 book as the 1920s precedent). See [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md).

**★ M9 · Realistic expectations — 15% is the ceiling, and size is the anchor.** *Buffett:* the 24% past return rode "a huge tailwind" of falling rates and reappraised businesses that "we won't have … in the future"; he'd "sign up now" for 15% over the next decade and warns money managers promising "really outstanding returns" should be avoided — "the numbers just get too big." Needs "the very large idea," not thousands. *Munger:* only "somebody from Singapore would call [15%] modest. It's not modest, it's arrogant." See [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md), [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md).

**★ M12 · Volatility is a huge plus; risk is business risk, not beta.** *Buffett:* think of stocks as pieces of businesses; risk arises from capital structure, long lead times, heavy capital, or commodity economics (the textile mill that "wasn't the low-cost producer"). "The stock market is there to serve you, and not to instruct you." Higher volatility "would create more mistakes," so "the crazier [Mr. Market] is, the more money you're going to make." The Washington Post at $80M was *less* risky than at $170M, yet beta called it more risky — "I still haven't figured it out." *Munger:* "totally ignore" risk-adjusted-return theory; professors "got volatility mixed up with a lot of foolish mathematics." See [risk-vs-volatility](../concepts/risk-vs-volatility.md), [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md), [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md).

**★ M14 · Owner earnings ≈ reported earnings when there's little compulsory reinvestment.** *Buffett:* for Berkshire's businesses, reported earnings plus amortization of intangibles "give a good representation … of owner earnings," because they deliberately avoid businesses with heavy "compulsory reinvestment … just in order to stand still." GEICO is the exception in the other direction: float buildup adds intrinsic value "above and beyond the reported earnings." Look-through earnings are "very rough" — "we don't believe in carrying things out to four decimal places." *Munger:* "after some unpleasant early experience, we have tried to avoid places where there was a lot of compulsory reinvestment." See [owner-earnings](../concepts/owner-earnings.md), [look-through-earnings](../concepts/look-through-earnings.md).

**M15 · TV ratings turn into money, but on a lag.** *Buffett:* ratings translate into ad prices over time, "but there's a difference in the time cycle"; schedule fixes don't work week-to-week "because people have habits." ESPN was the unforeseen "powerhouse" of the Cap Cities/ABC deal — "enormously better for us than we ever anticipated." *Munger:* the network business "is intrinsically a pretty tough business." See [disney](../entities/disney.md), [capital-cities](../entities/capital-cities.md).

**M16 · No stock split; the Class B *is* your split.** *Buffett:* a split is "not a pro-shareholder move … If we did, we'd do it"; the unsplit price screens for an investment-oriented audience. The B shares (forced by the threatened unit trust) "worked out very well," with "no bad effects whatsoever." Anyone wanting a split "can split 30-for-1 this afternoon." See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md), [berkshire-hathaway](../entities/berkshire-hathaway.md).

**★ M17 · Intel and Microsoft — we don't know how to value them, and that's fine.** *Buffett:* doubling returns works "if Intel and Microsoft do twice as well as Coke and Gillette," but "there's a whole group of companies … that Charlie and I just don't know how to value." Understanding = "a pretty good idea where it's going to be in ten years"; he needs only a few. Even IBM "couldn't see the future" of Intel and sold. *Munger:* physics eventually caps transistor density, so Intel "has to leverage its current leadership into new activities … too tough for us" to predict. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**M18 · Salomon — confidence in the people, not the industry.** *Buffett:* on the convertible preferred's annual redemption choice, "we see no reason ever to swing at the ball while it's still in the pitcher's glove"; odds "overwhelming" they'll convert. Terrific confidence in management "who helped us through some incredibly dark days," but no Coke-like conviction "about the profitability of the investment banking … business as a whole." See [salomon](../entities/salomon.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**M20 · No master plan — opportunism plus an insurance edge.** *Buffett:* "Berkshire, we have no master plan"; insurance float won't sustain 20% growth, but GEICO's voluntary-auto unit growth hit 10% (best in two decades) and ~16% early in 1997. The insurance edge is structural: "capital strength, and a willingness to take on risk, and a speed of action, and a certainty of payment, that in aggregate no one matches." Most other businesses throw off cash "which we can use to buy other things." See [geico](../entities/geico.md), [float](../concepts/float.md).

**★ M21 · Money managers, in aggregate, have "gotten a lot for nothing."** *Buffett:* professionals "simply cannot overperform, in aggregate. There are too many of them managing too big a portion of the pool"; fees and turnover guarantee they lag a low-cost index. It is "the only field" where "the amateur, as long as he recognizes he's an amateur, will do better than the professional." He'd wager any $10B-plus manager underperforms a no-load index fund over 5–10 years. *Munger:* "exactly one-fifth have to be in the bottom 20 percent"; it is "a very peculiar profession where you have to be in a state of psychological denial to shave in the morning if you do the work" — Lou Simpson the rare exception. See [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md), [lou-simpson](../people/lou-simpson.md).

**M22 · USAir — an able CEO nullifying Buffett's mistake.** *Buffett:* "I made a mistake in getting into it, but Mr. Wolf is … capable of nullifying my mistake"; dividends are caught up, ~$260–270M received, and the conversion privilege "we would have sold … for nothing a few years ago" now has value. *Hindsight/supersession note:* this is the rare USAir entry that ends *well* — the preferred was ultimately redeemed near par, a better outcome than the dead-loss writedown feared in [berkshire-1994-meeting](berkshire-1994-meeting.md) (M40) and [berkshire-1995-meeting](berkshire-1995-meeting.md) (A48); the *forward rule* against fixing non-viable cost structures still stands. See [usair](../entities/usair.md), [turnarounds-seldom-turn](../concepts/turnarounds-seldom-turn.md).

**M23 · 99% of Buffett's money is in Berkshire — but bought at a different price.** *Buffett:* "we like the idea of having it all in there, but we don't recommend that people do that," because "the price at which you enter is very important" and Berkshire's low-cost quality is now "fairly widely recognized." *Munger:* eventually the price could "get to such a high price that it's no longer sensible at all to buy," or "we'll just fail to meet expectations due to the vicissitudes of life." See [concentration-vs-diversification](../concepts/concentration-vs-diversification.md).

**M24 · Tobacco — willing to own the stock, declined to own the business.** *Buffett:* has owned tobacco stocks and RJR bonds, and Berkshire's distribution chain (the Buffalo News carries the ads; Costco sells cigarettes) already touches the product; declined to *manufacture* chewing tobacco despite knowing "it was going to do well." Can't fully justify the line — "I just know that one bothers me and the other doesn't." *Munger:* "each company, each individual, has to draw its own ethical and moral lines, and … I like the messy complexity of having to do that." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**M26 · Berkshire is now "more appropriately valued."** *Buffett:* a year earlier at $36,000 he'd called the stock "not undervalued," so wouldn't buy or recommend it during the unit-trust offering; since then intrinsic value rose while price stayed flat, so the stock "underperformed the business. Which … it's bound to do." Would still "rather own … Berkshire myself than … most other securities." See [intrinsic-value-vs-book-value](../concepts/intrinsic-value-vs-book-value.md).

**M27 · World Book vs. Encarta — "we copied him."** *Buffett:* Gates's Encarta (originally Funk & Wagnalls) was "a masterpiece of moving into an area and pushing hard"; Berkshire launched World Book on CD-ROM with IBM in response — frank admission that the incumbent had to imitate the disruptor. *Munger:* World Book is "clearly the class of the field," but he "can hardly imagine a world where the wise people don't do a lot of reading." See [scott-fetzer](../entities/scott-fetzer.md).

**M28 · The "ovarian lottery" — design the system before you know your ticket.** *Buffett:* a Rawlsian construct for judging fairness — set society's rules 24 hours before birth "not knowing whether you're going to be born … black or white … male or female … in the United States or Afghanistan." You'd want a system that produces abundance *and* treats the lottery's losers "in a way that you would want to be treated if you were in their position." A general-purpose thinking tool for stripping self-interest out of a judgment. *Munger:* sharply rejects the philosopher (Rawls) while accepting the construct (M30). See [inversion](../concepts/inversion.md).

**★ M32 · The five-minute filter — and why due-diligence theater proves nothing.** *Buffett:* "we really can tell you in five minutes whether we're interesting in something," because 40 years of study means "you get to know all the players." On FlightSafety he never visited a center, headquarters, or read a lease — "to date, that's never cost us a penny. What costs us money is when we misassess the fundamental economic characteristics of the business," which lawyers and studies "are going to support whatever … the guy that pays them … wants anyway." *Munger:* "people underrate the importance of a few simple big ideas … these filters of ours have worked pretty well … because they're so simple." See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**M33 · A "normal" day — reading, a telephone, no meetings.** *Buffett:* "I read a lot … We have no meetings. We have no committees. We have no slide presentations." The boring-looking job is one "I'm in love with"; by now he can "filter out so much." A portrait of the unglamorous, low-activity temperament the method requires. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ M34 · Stock options — a royalty on retained earnings, and "weak, corrupt, contemptible" accounting.** *Buffett:* options should be tied to "the area in which you have responsibility" (pay the typist for typing, not last year's EPS); a fixed-price option on a non-dividend payer "would mean that I was getting a royalty on money that you left with me," so options "should have a step-up in price." The real sin is "having a mediocre manager … paid huge sums." *Munger:* mandated option accounting is "weak, corrupt, and contemptible"; overuse makes the whole thing "sort of a chain letter," and the Tom Murphy / Commodore Vanderbilt restraint "is the right attitude." See [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md), [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md).

## Q&A digest — Afternoon session

**★ A2 · Intrinsic value = the unprinted coupons, discounted.** *Buffett:* a business is "like … a bond that had a whole bunch of coupons on it … due in a hundred years," except "they aren't printed on the instrument" and the investor must estimate them. "The only reason for putting cash into any kind of an investment now is because you expect to take cash out … not by selling it to somebody else … but by what the asset itself produces." That distinction *is* the line between an investor and a speculator. "You have to be able to walk away from anything that doesn't work" and "anything you don't understand." *Munger:* the old Graham Geiger-counter game (buying at 20–50% of liquidating value) "gets much harder" now — "you've got to get into Warren's kind of thinking." See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [intrinsic-value-vs-book-value](../concepts/intrinsic-value-vs-book-value.md).

**A3 · The market "looks high, it is high, but it's not as high as it looks."** *Buffett:* quoting Ben Graham's 1955 Senate testimony — present prices can be justified *if* record ROEs and low rates persist, but those are the load-bearing "ifs." After a big run, "people get captivated simply by the notion of rising prices without going back to the underlying rationale … very dangerous conditions in terms of possible bubbles." See [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md), [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md).

**★ A4 · When Berkshire is cheap, other things are usually cheaper.** *Buffett:* a 50%-down market wouldn't bother them — "we would have very intelligent things … to do with whatever capital we came into." A Berkshire buyback "would imply that our own stock was cheaper relative to value than anything else," which is unlikely: in 1973–74 Berkshire "wasn't as cheap as the Washington Post"; in 1988–89 it "wasn't as cheap as Coca-Cola." *Munger:* "We don't have any rule against it. Opportunity cost is the game around here." See [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md), [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

**A5 · International earnings without a foreign domicile.** *Buffett:* Coke earns "80 percent or more" abroad, Gillette "two-thirds or more," so Berkshire already captures global growth; domestic domicile is "a slight advantage" only on dividend taxation. Some products travel (Gillette, Disney, Coke, McDonald's); "See's Candy doesn't travel as well" — candy preferences are intensely regional. *Munger:* "if you can sell Coca-Cola … do you really want to get into steel in Malaysia?" See [coca-cola](../entities/coca-cola.md), [gillette](../entities/gillette.md), [look-through-earnings](../concepts/look-through-earnings.md).

**A6 · Businesses with natural limits are welcome — because the cash comes to Omaha.** *Buffett:* Kansas Bankers Surety and Western Surety are "good business[es]" precisely because they can't be force-grown — their specialized distribution and small premiums form a moat large rivals "can't get into," but "you can't pump two or three times the volume through." *Munger:* "there's a huge class of businesses … which … throw out large amounts of cash … but which can't rationally be expanded. … The beauty of the Berkshire Hathaway system is that such businesses are very welcome here because the cash comes into headquarters and is allocated there." See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md), [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**A7 · Exchangeable notes — taking capital out of a non-core holding cheaply.** *Buffett:* the $500M low-coupon notes exchangeable into Salomon stock let Berkshire "take the capital out of that block of stock at a low-interest cost," keeping limited upside. Berkshire's three convertible preferreds (Champion, US Airways, Salomon) sit in industries — paper, airlines, investment banking — "where we don't feel … the same kind of long-term economic advantage … as something like a Coke or a Gillette," so "those are not natural places for us to be common shareholders." See [salomon](../entities/salomon.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**A8 · A lower capital-gains rate probably won't flush out Berkshire sellers.** *Buffett:* ~80% of A shares are held below $100 cost, so a rate cut "might make less difference than you think." *Munger:* yet a one-month drop to a zero rate would produce "very dramatic effects" — tax law can move markets (the 1986 liquidation flood), but no such change is coming. See [tax-deferral](../concepts/tax-deferral.md).

**★ A9 · Super-cat — we know our exposure; the deadly risk is the *unwitting* one.** *Buffett:* Berkshire "pay[s] off infrequently, but … big" (~$120M on Andrew, ~$600–700M if it recurred today); the industry's catastrophe models "couldn't have been further off" with Andrew and Northridge — "we don't rely on those." The worst exposures sit with big direct writers along Long Island or the New Madrid Fault who "don't think of themselves as being in the super-cat business." Surprises in insurance "are never symmetrical. They're all bad." *Munger:* a $1B loss is "2 1/2 percent … of the liquid assets … irritating, but … not going to destroy the enterprise" — whereas "an unwitting super-cat exposure … could destroy your company" (Twentieth Century at Northridge). See [super-cat-insurance](../concepts/super-cat-insurance.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**★ A10 · The margin of safety scales with your uncertainty.** *Buffett:* "if you understood a business perfectly … you would need very little … margin of safety. So the more volatile the … possibility … the larger the margin." The truck-and-bridge image: a 9,800-lb load on a 10,000-lb bridge feels fine six inches above a ditch, but over the Grand Canyon "you may feel you want a little larger margin." The first defense, though, "is to understand the business," where "surprises are few." See [margin-of-safety](../concepts/margin-of-safety.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ A11 · Learn vicariously; the great mistakes are omissions, and you must bite hard.** *Buffett:* "the best thing to do is learn from other guys' mistakes" (Patton's "make sure the other guy gets the honor"); his own worst category is "being reluctant to pay up … for a business I knew was really outstanding … the cost … has been many, many billions. … most of our mistakes have been mistakes of omission rather than commission." *Munger:* no-brainers "are very few and … may be separated by periods of years," so "have the courage … to step up in a major way." Buffett: "you got to be willing to take a really big bite … it's crazy if you dabble around at the edges." See [mistakes-of-omission](../concepts/mistakes-of-omission.md).

**★ A12 · The risk-free rate is a yardstick, not a hurdle you want to clear.** *Buffett:* government-bond yield is used "merely to equate one item to another … It's not because we want to buy government bonds"; he'd discount at the risk-free rate and then demand "a substantial discount from that present value" rather than bump the rate for risk. If forced to hold a single security ten years versus a ten-year Treasury, "in at least 80 percent of the cases, we'd take the ten-year Government" — for lack of understanding or insufficient edge. *Munger:* "It's child's play as a mental process. Now, it's hard to make the business appraisals. But the mental process is a cinch." See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [bonds-as-a-business](../concepts/bonds-as-a-business.md).

**A13 · Insurance and investing are equal, distinct businesses.** *Buffett:* each Berkshire insurer (GEICO, super-cat, workers' comp, Kansas Bankers) is "a distinct asset" with its own float and economics; money is invested "when it comes in." Unlike rivals where "investments usually play second fiddle," Berkshire treats both sides "as being of equal importance" and never lets the investment side "impinge on … the insurance side." See [float](../concepts/float.md), [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md).

**★ A14 · Opportunity cost is the whole game.** *Munger:* compare each stock against the best alternative — "half the stocks in America … you'd rather have the government bond," so they drop out of the filter; among those you prefer, buy "the best opportunity, that you can understand as the best opportunity." "Life is a whole series of opportunity costs. You got to marry the best person who is convenient to find who will have you. Investment is much the same." See [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md), [concentration-vs-diversification](../concepts/concentration-vs-diversification.md).

**★ A15 · Retain a dollar only if it becomes worth more than a dollar.** *Buffett:* "the only reason for us to keep your money is if it becomes worth more by us keeping it." See's and the Buffalo News would pay "very large dividends" as standalones because Berkshire "wouldn't have a way of using … the money it generates" inside them; the surplus is redeployed instead. Berkshire will probably "go too long" before admitting it can't pass the test "because there'll be a certain denial." *Munger:* this simple standard "is not … taught in the corporate finance departments of our major universities … there are things gravely wrong with American education." See [dividend-policy](../concepts/dividend-policy.md), [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md).

**★ A16 · 22% returns on equity cannot persist in a 7% world.** *Buffett:* S&P ROE near 22% against a 12–13% norm "does not seem … sustainable" once "competitive forces … operate over time"; a perpetual 22% bond reinvesting at 22% "becomes a mathematical fallacy … when the compound rate becomes higher than the discount rate, you get into infinity" (the Petersburg Paradox / growth-stock fallacy). *Munger:* buybacks and looser antitrust lifted ROEs, but "you wouldn't think that can go on forever … Corporate profits can't be 200 percent of GNP. Indeed, they can't be 50 percent." And beware the advisors who "have an immense vested interest in believing that things that can't be true are true." See [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md), [return-on-equity-yardstick](../concepts/return-on-equity-yardstick.md).

**★ A17 · Share of mind, not share of market.** *Buffett:* a consumer brand's value is its position in "the mind of how many people throughout the world." Coke owns a favorable place in ~75% of minds, un-buyable "by a billion dollar advertising budget"; Disney's name lets a parent grab a video unseen. "It's share of mind. It's not share of market. It's share of mind that counts." See's taught the lesson — and "what pond you jumped in was probably more important than how well you could swim." *Munger:* See's was the first time "we really stepped up for brand quality … a very hard jump"; had they "demanded an extra $100,000 … we wouldn't have bought it" — a lesson in learning to recognize quality. See [moat](../concepts/moat.md), [economic-goodwill](../concepts/economic-goodwill.md), [sees-candies](../entities/sees-candies.md).

**A18 · Do what you enjoy now.** *Buffett:* "I think you will do well in what you enjoy"; refusing "jam tomorrow and not jam today." If they "were in this only for the money … we'd have quit a long time ago." A note on intrinsic motivation as a driver of long-run results. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ A20 · We don't care who's buying or selling — and always ask "And then what?"** *Buffett:* capital flows, Fed moves, and "M2 … whatever happened to M2?" are noise — "we don't care if the market closes for the next five years. We care how much Coca-Cola has sold." The test: "Would I be happy buying this stock if the market closed for five years?" On macro claims (a trade deficit, the Japanese "selling all their bonds"), trace the second-order consequence — "you always want to say, 'And then what?'" — because the seller must take *some* U.S. asset in exchange. See [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md), [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

**A21 · Prefer low labor costs — and low costs of every kind.** *Buffett:* "we like a business with low costs of every kind … because the rest is profit," so on balance they avoid labor-intensive businesses, "but there's some very good businesses that are labor intensive." See [commodity-business-economics](../concepts/commodity-business-economics.md).

**★ A22 · Managers make their own operating decisions — just "send money to Omaha."** *Buffett:* American Express lobbied to get into the Furniture Mart, but "we let every manager make his decisions"; second-guessing would cost them "the pride that comes from running" their businesses, and most managers "do not need to work for a living." Berkshire has "no companywide policy on virtually anything … except send money to Omaha." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**A23 · Intellectual capital, Berkshire-style, is the managers themselves.** *Buffett:* Goizueta, Eisner, Zeien, Murphy "have added billions of dollars of value"; "you should pay for it" without "an elaborate exercise" to put it on a balance sheet. A Goizueta or Eisner extracting latent value from an existing asset (Coke's system, Disney's library) "is our form of intellectual capital … what we can understand better" than software or drug patents. *Munger:* the buzzword is fashionable because Microsoft-scale aggregation effects are real, but "by and large, we've avoided the field." See [economic-goodwill](../concepts/economic-goodwill.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**★ A24 · Not buying pharma was a real mistake of omission.** *Buffett:* the drug industry was "obviously … terrific to invest in," but "distinguishing among the companies" was too hard — yet "you could've just bought the whole industry" cheaply when the Clinton health plan cast a cloud, "and we didn't … It was a mistake." A clean example of a knowable opportunity skipped. *Munger:* pharma is "one of the glories of American civilization … But … we haven't been part of it." See [mistakes-of-omission](../concepts/mistakes-of-omission.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**A25 · A good general education sharpens investing.** *Munger:* favors broad learning and memorable formulations ("everything should be made as simple as possible, but no more simple" — Einstein), with Ben Franklin "the bible around Berkshire." *Buffett:* "The reason I'm so financially conservative is I don't want to find out how badly I might behave if I were stretched." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ A26 · Why risk what you need for what you don't need?** *Buffett:* stick to owning businesses, not LEAPS or index options — once "you start focusing on short-term price behavior … you take your eye off the main ball." Borrowed money "usually leads to trouble. And it's not necessary." Berkshire on leverage "would've done very much better," but "it wouldn't have made any difference to us … why risk losing what you need … and have, for what you don't need and don't have?" *Munger:* Buffett "urged the civilization not to allow the new [options] exchanges" — to no effect. See [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md).

**★ A28 · State Farm — "what in hell is going on here?"** *Buffett:* a Bloomington mutual started "with no capital to speak of, no agency force … no stock options" became the dominant auto/home insurer with "the third largest net worth of any company in the United States" — overturning the belief that capitalist incentives are essential to building a business. Nobody studies it (nor USAA, nor how GEICO went unbought at $1.2M in 1948) "because it doesn't fit the pattern." *Munger:* "the idea of picking some extreme example and asking my favorite question, which is what in hell is going on here — that is the way to wisdom in this world." See [geico](../entities/geico.md), [inversion](../concepts/inversion.md).

**★ A29 · Love it when a wonderful business buys back its stock.** *Buffett:* repurchases by a truly great business appeal "almost regardless of price," because "there aren't that many super businesses." Coke consistently repurchases; the catch is that "most companies that repurchase their shares … are so-so businesses" buying for the wrong motives. Berkshire's Coke stake rose from 6.2% (~36M servings/day) to 8% of 900M-plus servings partly via Coke's buybacks. See [share-repurchases](../concepts/share-repurchases.md), [coca-cola](../entities/coca-cola.md).

**A30 · Coca-Cola's bottling history — "one of the dumbest contracts" and a 25-year fix.** *Buffett:* Asa Candler bought Coke for ~$2,000 ("the smartest purchase in the history of the world") then sold perpetual bottling rights with a *fixed* syrup price — disastrous once sugar prices spiked. Rationalizing that system took Goizueta and Keough "20 or 25 years" of patient capital — the long-horizon form of intellectual capital that "you aren't going to get results on … in a day, or a week, or a month, or a year." *Munger:* the company's history is "way more studied than it is … lesson after lesson." See [coca-cola](../entities/coca-cola.md).

**A31 · Wesco is in Berkshire by "historical accident."** *Buffett:* 80%-owned Wesco has "relatively close" prospects to Berkshire but a weaker long-run record; cheaper *per share* ($200 vs. $38,000) "is a mistake to think … it's got way more potential." Big share-exchange deals route through Berkshire, not Wesco. *Munger:* "A small school but there are those who love her." See [wesco-financial](../entities/wesco-financial.md).

**★ A32 · How Buffett surpassed Graham — Graham + Fisher, dark glasses vs. searchlight.** *Buffett:* the three core ideas (stocks as businesses, the right attitude to the market, margin of safety) "all come straight from Graham"; Fisher (and Munger) "opened my eyes … to … the wonderful business." He outran Graham partly by enjoying the game more — "I just find it interesting." *Munger:* "Buffett became way better than Graham … standing on the shoulder of giants"; Graham had "blind spots, partly of … an ethical professorial nature," limiting himself to what "any intelligent layman could learn," whereas Graham "was trying to play … 'Pin the Donkey,' wearing very dark glasses … Warren … would use the biggest search light he could find." See [ben-graham](../people/ben-graham.md), [franchise-vs-business](../concepts/franchise-vs-business.md).

**A33 · Gillette customers are more loyal than McDonald's customers — switching costs.** *Buffett:* the Inevitability gap is mechanical — a great shave costs "20-odd dollars a year," so "if you're getting a great result, you're not going to fool around," while fast-food choice "is simply based on which one you see. … convenience is a huge factor." You "can spend a lot more money on hamburgers … than razor blades" (Munger), so the price-saving incentive to switch barely exists in blades. See [moat](../concepts/moat.md), [gillette](../entities/gillette.md).

**A35 · Grow both columns — operating earnings and investments.** *Buffett:* the two-column scorecard (investments per share; operating earnings ex-investments) is "a very important table." Operating businesses get "first claim" on capital for their own needs, but those needs "use … only a small fraction" of Berkshire's cash; the rest is fungible and centrally deployed — "we don't leave it around awaiting the day." *Munger:* "the odds are very good that the marketable securities will keep going up, even as the businesses expand." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md).

**★ A36 · Stocks returning 4% a year "wouldn't surprise us."** *Buffett:* the *businesses* (Coke, Gillette, Wells Fargo, See's) aren't in danger; the question is valuation. "I don't think you'll get the investment result from owning the S&P over the next 10 years that you've gotten over the past 10," and 4% a year "would not be a surprising outcome … And it wouldn't bother us." *Munger:* "That's not predicting a crash … just saying that the guaranteed result … is almost certain to be less than … the last [decade]." See [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md).

**A37 · Don't talk a stock down to buy it cheaper.** *Buffett:* he could buy more if he forecast doom for Coke or Disney, but won't — selling wonderful businesses is "usually a bad mistake," and holders who sell at X then refuse to repay 105% of X get whipsawed. The teaching ethos (inherited from Graham, who taught openly over Jerry Newman's objection) is worth "a tiny, little bit of money from time to time." Buffett: "I didn't behave that way till I got rich." See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**★ A38 · The filters — understandable, durable edge, and a non-jerk on the other side.** *Buffett:* the first filter is "whether it's a business we're going to understand," then "whether a company can have a sustainable edge"; "98 percent of the conversations we can end … in the middle of somebody's first sentence." For whole businesses, the seller matters — an auction or a renegotiator means "we're going to have to buy the business two or three times." *Munger:* avoid the "awful people" whose warning "flags [are] flying" — "the amount of misery you bring into your life by trusting some awful person" outweighs any deal. Buffett: it's "like they got a sign on their chest that just says, 'Jerk. Jerk. Jerk.'" See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**A39 · Soft drinks aren't winner-take-all.** *Buffett:* Dr Pepper "has a future"; Coke's share rises in tenths of a percent, and Sprite is "the fastest growing big beverage." Unlike a two-newspaper town, "you can make money with a soft drink company that doesn't dominate … You'll do a lot better with one that does." See [coca-cola](../entities/coca-cola.md), [media-economics](../concepts/media-economics.md).

**A40 · Accumulated business knowledge compounds.** *Buffett:* evaluating big businesses "is all cumulative … you don't have to start over again every day"; the 1988 Coke decision turned on "a couple small incremental bits of information" added to "a mass … accumulated over decades." "It's why we like businesses that don't change too much, because the past is useful to us." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ A41 · Push when things get cheap — never enough to lose a minute of sleep.** *Buffett:* simultaneous super-cat losses plus a market crash would have them "thinking … ways to buy securities if they got cheap enough"; they'd borrow "some money," but "never … a ton … relative to capital." *Munger:* the prize is *flexibility* — "if something were large enough and cheap enough, we could stop writing super-cats … We have enormous options," where most businesses "waltz down the channel" with none. Buffett: push "never to a degree that … causes us to lose a minute of sleep about fulfilling every obligation." See [leverage-discipline](../concepts/leverage-discipline.md), [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md).

## Connections to other sources

- The whole meeting is the spoken footnote to the **"Inevitables"** essay in [berkshire-1996-letter](berkshire-1996-letter.md) (written March 1997): the letter coins the label and flags full valuation; the meeting (M6, M26, A29, A37) spends the day insisting the label "is not an unqualified buy recommendation regardless of price."
- The **risk-as-business-risk and anti-beta** argument (M12) restates the [berkshire-1994-meeting](berkshire-1994-meeting.md) (M50) horizon-based risk definition, now anchored explicitly in Mr. Market and the Washington Post beta paradox.
- **Opportunity cost as the measuring stick** (A2, A12, A14, A38) is the same idea Munger gave fullest form in [berkshire-1995-meeting](berkshire-1995-meeting.md) (A43, "your best opportunity is your measuring stick"), here generalized into the five-minute filter and the marry-the-best-available analogy.
- The **USAir** thread reverses tone: a dead loss feared at [berkshire-1994-meeting](berkshire-1994-meeting.md) (M40) and called "a mistake" at [berkshire-1995-meeting](berkshire-1995-meeting.md) (A48), it is now (M22) being "nullified" by Stephen Wolf and headed toward near-par redemption — a reminder that the forward *rule* (don't buy non-viable cost structures) outlives any single outcome.
- The **share-of-mind moat** (A17) and the **See's-as-teacher** story extend the moat-and-castle metaphor of [berkshire-1995-meeting](berkshire-1995-meeting.md) (M25) from castle economics to brand psychology, and feed the [moat](../concepts/moat.md) and [economic-goodwill](../concepts/economic-goodwill.md) pages.
- The **mean-reversion / 22%-ROE** humility (A16, A36) is the meeting-floor companion to the equity-return cautions developed across the mid-1990s letters and a forward marker for the more pointed bubble warnings to come at the 1999–2000 meetings.
- **"Money managers got a lot for nothing"** (M21) and the standing index-fund wager prefigure the public bet Buffett would formalize a decade later, and extend the [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md) reasoning seeded in [berkshire-1994-meeting](berkshire-1994-meeting.md) (A10).
