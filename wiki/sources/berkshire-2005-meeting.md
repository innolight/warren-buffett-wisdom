# Berkshire Hathaway 2005 Annual Meeting

The 2005 meeting opens under a legal shadow that shapes its whole tone: Buffett explains at the outset that he cannot discuss specifics of Berkshire's disclosures to investigators examining finite reinsurance industry practices, because investigators "do not want one witness talking with other witnesses." Beyond that constraint the meeting covers more ground per session than most years — a sustained diagnosis of why the private-equity and hedge-fund era is structurally hostile to patient capital; the sharpest formulation yet that pricing power is the right diagnostic for competitive strength; a prescient systemic-risk warning about Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac (validated in full by 2008); a vivid account of "everything correlates" when catastrophes hit; and the clearest on-record statement of why Berkshire's management model depends on "wonderful lack of oversight" rather than oversight itself. Per the wiki's two-layer convention, a short distilled thematic top precedes a **comprehensive Q&A digest** that captures every value-bearing exchange.

## Key themes

### 1. Pricing power is the sharpest diagnostic of competitive strength

"You can almost measure the strength of a business over time by the agony they go through in determining whether a price increase can be sustained." Thirty years ago the local newspaper raised rates "almost like a big yawn" because no advertiser dared leave. Today newspaper publishers agonize over every 20-cent monthly circulation increase — "when they drop it they don't usually take it up again." Beer still raises prices annually but is experiencing pushback for the first time in some markets. And See's Candy in 1972, at $1.95 a pound: "if we raised the price 10 cents a pound, would sales fall off a cliff? … the answer … was that no, there was some untapped pricing power." The business with untapped pricing power rewards its owners; the one that holds prayer sessions before raising a penny is in a structural trap. See [moat](../concepts/moat.md), [franchise-vs-business](../concepts/franchise-vs-business.md).

### 2. "Everything correlates" — manage for tail events, not averages

Buffett's framework for catastrophe risk generalizes far beyond insurance: when you run a concentrated operation, risks that seem uncorrelated in normal times become correlated in stress. A mega-earthquake in California would not only hit National Indemnity and Gen Re simultaneously — it would hit See's Candy, Wells Fargo, and any GSE-backed mortgage book. "We find when there's trouble, everything correlates." The corollary is institutional: Berkshire can afford to write large, unusual risks (a $500M airport policy; an NCAA Final Four cancellation policy at $75M) because its balance sheet can absorb correlation, and it deliberately excludes nuclear/chemical/biological exposures that would make check-clearing the next day uncertain. "Everything will — everything that can happen, will happen." See [super-cat-insurance](../concepts/super-cat-insurance.md), [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md).

### 3. The patient-capital advantage in a fee-driven era

In 2005 Berkshire was badly positioned to buy businesses: private equity was paying prices "that nobody who was going to live with the downside" would pay, and hedge funds had joined the auction. The root: "the investment manager will rationalize any price paid because he likes the extra fees for managing the extra assets." Berkshire's disadvantage is also its identity — "we run it as if it's 100 percent our money, which it is close to 100 percent of our net worth." But the cycle always turns. Proof: in 1969 Buffett liquidated his partnership ("so much money sloshing around"); within four years came the greatest opportunities of his lifetime. In fall 1998, a 30-basis-point spread between the 30-year and 29.5-year Treasury. In late 2002, high-yield bonds at 25–70% yield, sold out at 6% within 14 months — $7B deployed. "We are going to be buying things … as long as I live, just like I'm going to be buying groceries." See [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md), [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md).

### 4. "Wonderful lack of oversight" — why Berkshire's model compounds

Munger's correction of the meeting's most interesting assumption: a shareholder suggested Berkshire's success came from "skillful oversight" of subsidiaries. Munger disagreed: "It's come from our lack of oversight of the subsidiary companies. And I think our successors will be able to provide the same wonderful lack of oversight." The logic is tight: if you will not oversee, you must be extremely careful what you bring in and how you treat the people there. Berkshire has no group VPs, no policy directives, no HR or PR departments in Omaha telling subsidiaries how to run. The successor will "come from within Berkshire. They will have seen how it worked." The bigger risk is that "the culture would get tampered with … people would try to oversteer." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).

### 5. Earnings expectations: the "blood brother of evil"

The most forceful anti-earnings-management statement in any meeting. Munger: "The history of much of which we don't like in modern corporate capitalism comes from an unreasonable expectation, communicated from headquarters, that earnings have to go up with no volatility and great regularity. That kind of an expectation from headquarters is not just the kissing cousin of evil. It's the blood brother of evil." Buffett: Berkshire keeps no budgets, issues no "body language" about desired earnings, makes no Wall Street promises — because even without compensation incentives, a manager who knows his CEO has promised a number will move reserves from "45 billion" to "44¾ billion" to avoid embarrassment. "Setting up a system that either exerts financial or psychological pressure on the people around you to do things that they probably really don't even want to do … it's a terrible mistake." See [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md), [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md).

### 6. Productive assets vs. gold: the test is earning power under any currency

Asked twice about gold as an inflation hedge, Buffett pivots to a unified framework: the right test for any store of value is whether it produces real economic output regardless of the currency environment. "See's candy — if the dollar goes down 50 percent, we will be selling See's candy for double the present price. We'll be getting the same real price for See's candy. People will work the same number of minutes or hours per week in order to buy a pound or two-pound box of the candy." Gold has almost no utility outside of being a medium of flight from paper. A farm, an apartment house, a great business — all produce something real. Gold goes from the ground in South Africa to Fort Knox and "doesn't do much along the way, for anybody." Munger's addendum: gold was invaluable to a Jewish family in Vienna in 1935 — when the hazard is expropriation rather than currency depreciation, the utility calculus flips entirely. See [owner-earnings](../concepts/owner-earnings.md), [bonds-and-inflation](../concepts/bonds-and-inflation.md).

### 7. The investigation constraint: what Buffett could not say about AIG in 2005

Three exchanges weave together the Gen Re / AIG finite reinsurance investigation. Buffett opens the meeting explaining he cannot discuss specifics of what Berkshire has disclosed to authorities — investigators "do not want one witness talking with other witnesses." When asked directly about AIG, he declines to comment, awaiting the 10-K. Munger offers the only substantive statement: "I think whatever comes along, people are going to find that a lot that was very right was done over the years by AIG. There's a lot of ability in that place." On the broader concept, Buffett draws a clean line: a retroactive contract with genuine risk transfer (the ACE/INA example: ~$1.25B premium for up to $2.5B of old liabilities, with real risk in amount, timing, and reinvestment rate) is proper insurance. Contracts that transfer no risk but enable accounting manipulation are what investigators seek. *Supersession flag: Gen Re settled related charges in 2010 for $92M; the deal was found to have been structured to fraudulently boost AIG's reported reserves. Hank Greenberg resigned from AIG in 2005 amid related investigations. By 2008, AIG's near-collapse came from $440B in credit default swaps — a different but connected failure mode Munger had been warning about since 2003.* See [general-re](../entities/general-re.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

## Q&A digest — Morning session

**★ M8 · Manager selection: passion first, integrity always.** *Buffett:* "The most important factor … is a passion for their business." When Berkshire pays $100M or $1B for a business, the seller has no financial reason to keep working — they have to want to. Beyond passion: intelligence, energy, integrity. "We tell them, if they don't have the last, the first two will kill you. Because if you hire somebody without integrity, you really want them to be dumb and lazy." The key diagnostic: do they love the money, or do they love the business? *Munger:* "The interesting thing is how well it's worked over a great many decades and how few people copy it." See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md), [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**M9 · Anheuser-Busch: durable position, flat near-term.** *Buffett:* bought 100 shares ~25 years ago to receive reports directly; the position now reflects a very strong consumer franchise at "a little patch of unpleasantness" — flat beer volumes, pricing pressure, market share contested by a rejuvenated Miller. The deeper history: post-WWII Storz had 50% of Omaha's beer market; Anheuser was number four nationally. National brands consolidated to domination — "a trend toward concentration into a few giants that is, I think, permanent." No private-label threat in beer (a small plus over other consumer categories). *Munger:* "At our scale of operation now, if we're ever going to buy into a terribly well-regarded company, we almost need a little patch of unpleasantness." See [moat](../concepts/moat.md), [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

**★ M10 · The hedge fund era: patient capital at a structural disadvantage — and a structural advantage.** *Buffett:* "There's no doubt about it that there is far more money looking at deals now than five years ago." Private equity sells to private equity; fee economics motivate higher bids that no one is "going to own the downside" on. Berkshire can't compete in auctions. But the cycle has turned at least three times in Buffett's career and will again. "We are going to be buying things … as long as I live, just like I'm going to be buying groceries." *Munger:* "I don't think any of the businesses that have sold to us over the years … would have wanted to sell to a hedge fund. So there exists a class of assets out there that doesn't want to deal with hedge funds or private equity funds. Thank God." See [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md), [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**M11 · How to start in investing: read everything, start young, use the framework.** *Buffett:* got interested at ~7; took every book from the Omaha Public Library on markets; first stock at 11. Graham's *The Intelligent Investor* at 19 "just changed my whole framework." Advice: "Read everything in sight. And start very young." Key insight: "It requires qualities of temperament way more than it requires qualities of intellect. … If you've got more than 125 IQ, you can throw away the rest of the points." *Munger:* "money management is sort of a low calling, compared to being a surgeon or a lot of other things" — but studying investment "helps you learn more about how the world really works." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**M12 · CEOs who don't understand investing make bad acquisition decisions.** *Buffett:* an absurdity observed throughout his career: a CEO will say evaluating individual stocks is "much too tough — what do I know about investing?" then let an investment banker walk in and approve a $3B acquisition in a two-hour presentation. The acquisition is just concentrated stock in one company — but the CEO treats it as requiring no investment judgment. "It's a little like they say about making sausage and making laws, it's better unobserved." *Munger:* the present era has "a higher percentage of the attention of our intelligent classes into buying little pieces of paper … I can recall no past era which had a similar concentration of this type of activity." See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md), [institutional-imperative](../concepts/institutional-imperative.md).

**★ M13 · PetroChina: reading the annual report, $400M to $1.2B.** *Buffett:* bought PetroChina after reading the annual report — "the first stock — Chinese stock — and really the last" at that point. At purchase: market cap $35B, earnings $12B (~3x earnings). Dividend policy explicitly stated in the report at 45% of earnings → ~15% cash yield on cost. "We did not go over and — we never had any contact with the management before we bought the stock. We'd never attended an investor presentation or anything of the sort. It's right there in black and white." Compared Yukos (better known in the U.S.) to PetroChina; preferred China's economic climate and the significant discount to international peers. Would not have bought it at a domestic U.S. multiple ("there's some disadvantages, always, to being in a culture that you don't perfectly understand"). *Munger:* "It would be nice if this sort of thing happened all the time, but that hasn't been true in recent years." *Supersession flag: Berkshire sold PetroChina in 2007 at roughly 5x cost, generating over $3B profit.* See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md).

**★ M14 · The pricing-power agony test.** *Buffett:* "You can almost measure the strength of a business over time by the agony they go through in determining whether a price increase can be sustained." Thirty years ago a local newspaper "went merrily along, increasing prices" when newsprint went up — and when it went down. Today publishers agonize. Shaw Industries (carpet) is "hit time after time" with raw material increases it can't immediately pass through. See's Candy in 1972: the question of whether a 10-cent-per-pound increase would cause sales to "fall off a cliff" was the key diagnostic — and the answer was no. "It's not a great business when you have to have a prayer session before you raise your prices a penny." *Munger:* "It's hard to know just which companies can pass through the increases in costs that come from higher commodity prices. And it's also important to know." See [franchise-vs-business](../concepts/franchise-vs-business.md), [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

**M15 · Dividend policy: tax rate is not the test.** *Buffett:* with dividends now taxed at 15%, the question of a Berkshire dividend arises. "If there were no tax on dividends, to this point at least, we would have followed an identical dividend policy." The test is whether retaining a dollar produces more than a dollar of present value. When cash piles up and can't be intelligently deployed, "the burden of proof will certainly shift" to explain retention. Directors will discuss it Monday. See [dividend-policy](../concepts/dividend-policy.md).

**M16 · Trade deficit: selling pieces of the farm.** *Buffett:* the $618B trade deficit is "like an incredibly rich family that owns millions of acres of land" and consumes 6% more than it produces, selling tiny pieces of the farm every day without noticing. "The rest of the world is happy to take a little piece of our farm … because it's such a terrific asset." But it can't go on indefinitely — "the world will own a good bit of us and our children will be paying, one way or another." Berkshire held ~$21B in foreign exchange contracts. *Munger:* "I think the end will be bad." Adds Adam Smith's dictum that a great civilization "has a lot of ruin in it." See [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md).

**M17 · Housing bubble: local but severe.** *Buffett:* prices in premium markets have grown at "far, far greater rates than construction costs or inflationary factors." Cites his Laguna Beach house sold for $3.5M (land implicitly $60M/acre), a Berkshire director's neighbor selling a house for $27M. Historical analog: Nebraska farmland went from $2,000/acre in 1980 to $600 (an FDIC purchase) a few years later. The mechanism differs from other bubbles because people live in houses — "it will not behave, necessarily, the same as other markets." Easy GSE-guaranteed lending broke the normal lending discipline: the lender "does not need to care about what kind of financial transaction the purchase is. All they have to do is look at the guarantee." *Munger:* "If you get to Laguna, California … you have a real asset price bubble." See [lending-discipline](../concepts/lending-discipline.md).

**★ M18 · Catastrophe insurance: "everything correlates" when trouble comes.** *Buffett:* the management of mega-cat risk requires integrating across all of Berkshire's operations — insurance and investment. A major California earthquake hits National Indemnity, Gen Re, See's Candy, and Wells Fargo simultaneously. "Part of my job is to have at least a general idea of the sort of risk that the various enterprises might be running operationally, and then integrate that into my own notion of the risk." Berkshire writes risks nobody else will: a $500M airport policy excess of $2.5B; NCAA Final Four cancellation at $75M; Grammy Awards coverage. Can do so because the balance sheet can absorb correlation: "we're willing to lose a lot of money in one day, but we're not willing to do anything that causes us any discomfort, in terms of writing checks the following morning." Nuclear/chemical/biological exposures were excluded after recognizing they were existential: "we would regard ourselves as vulnerable to extinction, as a company, if we did not have nuclear, chemical, and biological risks excluded." "Everything will — everything that can happen, will happen." *Munger:* "We naturally have minds that think about tidal waves in California, where they've never had one in modern California civilization." See [super-cat-insurance](../concepts/super-cat-insurance.md), [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md).

**M21 · Zero-down mortgages and a Ponzi-like mechanism.** *Buffett:* "Terms have become easier and easier as prices have increased" — exactly backwards from prudent lending. The GSE guarantee broke the link: the mortgage buyer "does not need to care about what kind of financial transaction the purchase is. All they have to do is look at the guarantee." Thought experiment: Omaha with constant population, everyone sells their house to their neighbor each year at an increasing price, funding the markup by borrowing from Tokyo — "an accidental aggregation of behavior that somewhat leads to the same effect." *Munger:* "You have varied Ponzi effects in various parts of any modern economy … very little studied in economics." See [lending-discipline](../concepts/lending-discipline.md).

**★ M22 · Gold vs. productive assets: the earning-power test.** *Buffett:* gold is "way down on my list as a store of value." The right question: what produces real value regardless of the currency in use? "See's candy — if the dollar goes down 50 percent, we will be selling See's candy for double the present price. We'll be getting the same real price for See's candy." His father preached gold from the dinner table; from $35/oz in 1940 to ~$400 today — "the compound rate … is not something that causes me to salivate." The Dow went from 66 to ~11,000–12,000 in the same period and paid dividends throughout. Gold goes from a South African mine to Fort Knox and "doesn't do much along the way, for anybody." *Munger:* gold was invaluable to "a well-to-do Jewish family in Vienna in 1935" — the hazard of expropriation is categorically different from currency depreciation. See [owner-earnings](../concepts/owner-earnings.md).

**★ M23 · Earnings expectations: the blood brother of evil.** *Buffett:* no budget submissions to Omaha, no "body language" signaling desired earnings, no Wall Street promises — because even well-intentioned managers who know their CEO has promised the Street a number will move reserves from "45 billion" to "44¾ billion" to avoid embarrassment. "Setting up a system that either exerts financial or psychological pressure on the people around you to do things that they probably really don't even want to do … it's a terrible mistake." *Munger:* "The history of much of which we don't like in modern corporate capitalism comes from an unreasonable expectation, communicated from headquarters, that earnings have to go up with no volatility and great regularity. That kind of an expectation from headquarters is not just the kissing cousin of evil. It's the blood brother of evil. And we just don't need that blood brother in our headquarters." See [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md), [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md).

**M24 · Track record beats potential in manager assessment.** *Buffett:* can't rank 50 MBA students by business potential from interviews. Berkshire's approach: buy businesses from people who have already demonstrated results for 10–15–20 years. "It is far easier to tell the great baseball batters after you've seen a couple seasons of their batting than it is to go to a college … and pick out the superstars." One study finding: the best correlation with business success was "the age at which they started their own business first." *Munger:* "Part of it is intelligence and part of it is temperament. … I think sensible people with the right temperament and the right intelligence, if they live long enough in our system, will get rich. But temperament is, I think to some extent, inherited, too." See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**M26 · AIG: Munger's defense and the investigation constraint.** *Buffett:* declines to comment on AIG's situation, awaiting the 10-K. "I really don't know anything about what will be in that statement." *Munger:* "I think whatever comes along, people are going to find that a lot that was very right was done over the years by AIG. There's a lot of ability in that place." Buffett adds that Hank Greenberg "was the number one man in insurance … he developed an extraordinary company." *Supersession flag: AIG eventually restated ~$3.9B in earnings (2005); the finite reinsurance matter settled (Gen Re, 2010, $92M). By 2008, AIG's near-collapse from $440B in credit default swaps required a $182B government bailout — validating at macro scale the derivatives-concentration warnings Munger had made at every meeting since 2002.* See [general-re](../entities/general-re.md), [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md).

**★ M27 · Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac: systemic risk in plain sight.** *Buffett:* the GSEs expanded beyond their original guarantee mandate into a "carry trade using the government's credit" — borrowing cheaply via the implied guarantee and earning the spread on mortgage portfolios. "There's no way to lend money for 30 years to somebody who can pay you off 30 seconds later … and actually match assets and liabilities in a way that's risk-free." When earnings targets in "the mid-teens" became unachievable through legitimate means, "they got involved in some accounting shenanigans, which really sort of boggle the mind." One had a $5B mismark in one direction; the other had a $9B mismark in the other direction simultaneously. Prediction: some curtailment, tougher capital ratios — "I don't think the government is going to do something worse" (i.e., a forced rapid portfolio sell-off). *Munger:* "A lot of the troubles that came, came from a large use of derivative books, and from Fannie and Freddie both believing a lot of silver-tongue salesmanship from derivative traders." *Supersession flag: In September 2008, both Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac were placed into U.S. government conservatorship after their combined losses threatened systemic collapse. Buffett's prediction of a soft curtailment proved wrong; the correction was a forced government seizure, not a taper.* See [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md), [lending-discipline](../concepts/lending-discipline.md).

**M28 · UK investing: 3% reporting threshold is a deterrent, not a prohibition.** *Buffett:* UK disclosure rules require reporting when ownership hits 3%, disrupting subsequent purchases. "We've thought twice before going over three percent." No bias against UK businesses (MidAmerican's Yorkshire and Northern Electric made ~$300M after-tax). "If we get a chance to buy a significant piece of something we think is cheap … we would feel very comfortable with lots of U.K. businesses." Also notes a slight preference for non-dollar earnings given the trade-deficit view. *Munger:* finds it "kind of amusing that we ended up preferring the currencies of Europe when it's so much more socialized." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**M29 · Financial intermediary liability: "knowing participation" is the line.** *Buffett:* on whether a party to a transaction has an obligation to ensure the other party accounts for it correctly: "If you knowingly are doing something that causes a company that are participating in it, you know, you may have very serious obligations." But Berkshire reinsures hundreds of companies — "they have legal departments. They have auditors." The analysis turns on knowing vs. unwitting participation. *Munger:* "It's very hard to predict what things are going to get legally shifted around so a supplier gets liability for its customer's behavior." See [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

**M30 · The best investment is in your own abilities.** *Buffett:* when asked about hedging personal savings against a weakening dollar: "The best investment you can have, for most people, is in your own abilities." Would pay a student $100,000 for 10% of their future earnings — valuing the whole person at $1M in capital terms. "Anything you do to develop your own abilities is probably going to be more productive for you than starting to think about individually making commitments into foreign exchange." Berkshire itself keeps 80%+ of capital in the U.S. *Munger:* "If a thing's not worth doing at all, it's not worth doing well." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

## Q&A digest — Afternoon session

**★ A2 · Inflation-resistant businesses: no big capital reinvestment needed.** *Buffett:* "The best business to have during inflation is one that retains its earning power in real dollars without commensurate investment to, in effect, fund the inflation-produced nominal growth." The brain surgeon or leading lawyer bought their expertise in old dollars and doesn't have to keep reinvesting. See's Candy has succeeded because "it does not have huge capital investments that have to be made in current dollars." The worst: airlines, where each new plane costs far more in nominal terms and returns remain inadequate — "they have to stay in the game." Public utilities sit between: they keep reinvesting in current dollars and depend on regulators to allow commensurate returns. *Munger:* the same imports that are deflating U.S. consumer prices are partly masking underlying dollar weakness. See [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md), [owner-earnings](../concepts/owner-earnings.md).

**A3 · Don't bet against America.** *Buffett:* despite the macro concerns about trade and debt: "In 1790 we had less than four million people in this country … 215 years later, those 3.9 million people … have 30 percent or so of the world's GDP. So it does not make sense to bet against America." Real GDP per capita is seven times what it was a hundred years ago. Never decided not to buy a business liked "because of a macro view." *Munger:* "I agree with you that the economics of the country are probably going to increase for a considerable period ahead. But I suspect that, in very important ways, we are at or near the apex of a great civilization." See [american-tailwind](../concepts/american-tailwind.md).

**A4 · GM and Ford: the long cost of deferred accounting.** *Buffett:* both Wagoner and Ford are dealing with contracts signed when GM had 50%+ of the U.S. auto market — health and pension commitments made without accrual accounting, so felt cost-free at the time. "It's a terrible mistake for managers not to think in terms of reality rather than the accounting numbers." Before the 1990s you didn't have to accrue for health care obligations. "People said, 'Well, if we don't have to count it, it isn't real.' Well, believe me, it's real." *Munger:* "Just because it hasn't happened yet doesn't mean that the problem isn't real. If you jump out of the window on the 42nd floor, and you're still doing fine on the way down as you pass the 20th — it doesn't mean you don't have a serious problem." See [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md).

**A5 · Asbestos: "terrible behavior" at every level.** *Munger:* "terrible behavior by some lying doctors, terrible behavior by a bunch of lawyers who suborned perjury, and gutless behavior by certain important courts, and even more gutless behavior by politicians." Payments to people who haven't yet gotten sick, who "smoked two packs of cigarettes all their life and have one little spot on a lung" — "it never should have been allowed. But once you get a powerful political force, even judges fear consequences. And it's very easy just to drift along with an evil system." The pattern of institutional capture by vested interests extends well beyond asbestos. See [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

**★ A6 · Successors and "wonderful lack of oversight."** *Buffett:* the successor will come from within Berkshire, will have seen how the system works, and won't need to create it. "Three successors" are being discussed with the board Monday. The main risk: "the culture would get tampered with … people would try to oversteer." *Munger:* disagrees that Berkshire's success came from "skillful oversight" — "It's come from our lack of oversight of the subsidiary companies. And I think our successors will be able to provide the same wonderful lack of oversight that we have provided. … If you're not going to use a lot of oversight, you've got to be very careful in what you bring into your corporate family." *Supersession flag: Buffett referenced "three successors" in 2005. In 2010–2011, Todd Combs and Ted Weschler joined as investment managers. In 2021, Greg Abel was publicly named CEO successor.* See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md), [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md).

**★ A7 · Berkshire's best-ever investments: GEICO and finding Ajit Jain.** *Buffett:* GEICO (first half acquired for $40M) is probably the single best investment by dollar terms — "that 40 million will turn out very well." Now more than 6M customers, entering New Jersey in 2004, run by "a superb human being and businessman, Tony Nicely." See's Candy was "enormously important" as a teacher and cash generator. *Munger:* "I can think of no higher return investment that we've ever made than that one" — referring not to GEICO but to finding Ajit Jain. "Getting the right people into your system can frequently be more important than anything else." See [geico](../entities/geico.md), [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md).

**★ A8 · NYSE corporatization: the exchange should not try to earn a profit.** *Buffett:* "The enemy of investment performance is activity. And the creator of profit in a profit-minded exchange is activity." A for-profit NYSE would try to increase volume, earning more from frictional costs of capitalism. "IBM or General Motors or General Electric will not earn more money because their stock turns over more frequently. But a for-profit exchange will earn more money." Prefers the "exchange of yesterday" — not-for-big-profit. *Munger:* "I think we have totally lost our way on this stuff … I certainly don't think you want to turn the major stock exchange of the country into even more of a casino than it is already." See [investment-friction](../concepts/investment-friction.md), [passive-investing](../concepts/passive-investing.md).

**A10 · Financial company opacity: complexity breeds error and fraud.** *Buffett:* financial institutions are harder to analyze than operating businesses because "the biggest single element that is very difficult to evaluate … is the loss and loss adjustment expense reserve." Small changes in a $45B reserve produce massive reported-earnings swings. Derivatives compound it: "it's unbelievable what you can find" when you try to value a derivatives book at market. Buffett spent three years unwinding a 23,000-contract Gen Re book in a benign environment and doubts any regulator can "get their minds around evaluation" of an extensive derivative book. *Munger:* "Where you've got complexity … you're going to get more fraud and mistakes than you are if you're selling sand by the truckload." See [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md), [earnings-quality](../concepts/earnings-quality.md).

**★ A11 · Existing holdings: the "neither buyer nor seller" zone at scale.** *Buffett:* Berkshire owns Coca-Cola, American Express, Wells Fargo, Moody's — "very, very good businesses." Some would be repurchased at today's prices; some wouldn't; none are being sold. "We're in a zone in some of those securities — where we wouldn't buy and we wouldn't sell." At scale, moving large positions (taxes plus market impact) creates a "neither/nor" band that doesn't exist for smaller investors. Common stock percentage of Berkshire intrinsic value is at a near-historical low — no bullish market call is implied. *Munger:* in recent smaller equity moves, Berkshire's record "is much like it used to be in some of the best days." See [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md), [concentration-vs-diversification](../concepts/concentration-vs-diversification.md).

**★ A13 · Market valuation: no bubble, no bargain — equities preferred to bonds over 20 years.** *Buffett:* won't speak definitively about markets unless conditions are extreme (as in summer 1999). Current reading: "We're not in bubble-type, at all, valuations in equities. And I don't think we're anywhere close to … bargain valuations." Forced choice between equity index and 20-year bonds (yielding just over 4.5%): equities win. But "people that have expectations that they can earn more than 6 or 7 percent in equities … are making a big mistake." Expects a "screamingly intelligent" opportunity "in not too many years." *Munger:* "I can't improve on that at all." See [equity-return-expectations](../concepts/equity-return-expectations.md), [valuation-framework](../concepts/valuation-framework.md).

**A14 · REITs: unattractive now, attractive five years ago.** *Buffett:* at the NASDAQ peak (2000), his tiny non-Berkshire portfolio was all in small REITs selling at discounts to conservatively valued property. Now REITs trade at premiums to inflated property values. "The situation changed dramatically from five or six years ago." The right move: "pay attention to something that is being scorned rather than something that's being championed." *Munger:* for a C-corporation, real estate is "grossly disadvantaged" tax-wise vs. individual ownership; and "real estate investment real estate is having bubble valuation problems of its own right now." See [volatility-as-opportunity](../concepts/volatility-as-opportunity.md).

**A16 · Unwise policies, but don't bet against the country.** *Buffett:* acknowledges consumer debt and trade deficit as real problems. "Peter Lynch has always said, 'Buy a business that's so good that an idiot can run it, because sooner or later one will.'" Applied to the country: "We've got a country that's so good that we can have policies that are counterproductive — and we'll still come out OK." At worst: "very severe dislocations in financial markets from time to time … But we've had them historically. And sometimes there's great opportunity in those dislocations." *Munger:* "Apart from knowing that, we have no contribution to make." See [american-tailwind](../concepts/american-tailwind.md).

**★ A17 · Berkshire's board: rich, smart, fully invested, no liability insurance.** *Buffett:* "Every one of them has significant money invested in Berkshire. They all bought it in the market just like you did." No director gets D&O insurance from Berkshire. The systemic problem: many directors "need" their $100–200K annual fee and would lose it if they challenged management. "I have never seen a director, where the directors' fees were important to them, object to an acquisition proposal, object to a compensation arrangement of the CEO. It's just never happened … in my experience." *Munger:* "A director getting $150,000 a year from a company, who needs it, is not an independent director. That director automatically becomes an inside director." Notes one Berkshire director was "removed twice from compensation committees of other corporations because he had the temerity to actually question whether the compensation arrangement being suggested was the appropriate one." See [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md), [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).

**A19 · Gates is smart — we still stick to our circle.** *Buffett:* Gates on the board; Buffett will "listen to any idea Bill has … he is not only a smart manager, but he's a smart investor." But: "It's still Charlie and I that have the responsibility for managing the money. And we'll stick in what we consider to be our circle of competence. And the fact that somebody else's circle is wider or different, you know, that's the way the world is." Self-deprecating close: "I still wish I'd bought a little Microsoft when I first met him." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ A20 · Director independence: the Elihu Root test.** *Munger:* standard governance reform conflates diversity-box checks with independence. The correct standard: "No man was fit to hold public office who wasn't perfectly willing to leave it at any time." Elihu Root had three cabinet appointments and could always return to being the most sought-after lawyer in the world — his identity didn't depend on the government salary. "Is a man really fit to make tough calls who isn't perfectly willing to leave the office at any time? My answer is no." *Buffett:* comp committees want "Chihuahuas, not Great Danes and Dobermans." See [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

**★ A21 · "Gin-rummy management" — why Berkshire keeps underperforming companies.** *Buffett:* Berkshire won't sell a subsidiary that has disappointed expectations as long as it won't permanently lose money, doesn't have major labor issues, and doesn't require incessant capital. "We do not engage in what we call gin-rummy type management where we pick up one card and discard another." Acknowledges this "may produce slightly suboptimal returns" — a conscious choice to honor commitments to managers and employees who sold on the premise of permanent ownership. "Every business decision or investment decision isn't going to work out perfectly." *Munger:* frames it also as a moral distinction — operating a business carries different responsibility from owning its stock. See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md), [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**A22 · Moral distinction: owning stock vs. operating a business.** *Buffett:* "We would not have trouble owning stock in a cigarette company. We wouldn't want to manufacture cigarettes." Berkshire flew ~1,000 miles to look at a business — "probably one of the best businesses I've ever seen, in terms of the economics of it" — and declined because they didn't want to be responsible for that product. Didn't disclose what it was. *Munger:* they were "young and poor then by modern standards" — could see it was "like putting $100 million in a bushel basket and setting it on fire … We made the decision all right and with no difficulty. But there was a certain twinge." See [acquisition-criteria](../concepts/acquisition-criteria.md).

**A24 · Pharmaceuticals: a "too hard" pile.** *Buffett:* great historical returns on invested capital; now in "a state of flux" with much of the uncertainty in the political realm — "my judgment about the … what politicians will do is probably not better than yours." *Munger:* "We just throw some decisions into the 'too hard' pile and go on to others." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ A25 · No degree of difficulty in investing — step over 1-foot bars.** *Buffett:* "In terms of investing, there is no degree of difficulty. If something is staring you right in the face and the easiest decision in the world, the payoff, can be huge." Olympic divers get more credit for harder dives; investors don't. "We get paid, not for jumping over 7-foot bars, but for stepping over 1-foot bars. And the biggest thing we have to do is decide which ones are the 1-foot bars and which ones are the 7-foot bars." Err toward a narrower universe rather than a wider one. *Munger:* "Obviously." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md), [margin-of-safety](../concepts/margin-of-safety.md).

**A26 · Psychological traps: recognition first.** *Buffett:* on "anchoring" (stopped buying PetroChina after the price popped on disclosure): "The first step is in recognition of the fact that they can be traps and that you will be affected by them." Buffett and Munger are "a little less prone to some of those mistakes than other people are" by personality, but still make them and will again. *Munger:* "You don't have to have perfect wisdom to get very rich. All you've got to do is have slightly more than other people, on average, over a long time." See [mr-market](../concepts/mr-market.md).

**A27 · Long-term Treasury yields: a genuine puzzle.** *Buffett:* the 10-year Treasury at 4.2% amid commodity inflation is a "conundrum" — he looked up what the word meant after Greenspan used it and "decided I agreed with him." Keeping money short is implicitly a decision not to be long bonds — not a bet that rates will rise. *Munger:* "There won't be some automatic and rational correlation between inflation and interest rates. There will be weird diversions." See [bonds-and-inflation](../concepts/bonds-and-inflation.md).

**★ A28 · "Finite" reinsurance: risk transfer is the test, not the label.** *Buffett:* "Almost all insurance is finite" — a $200,000 homeowner's policy is finite. The term has become "convenient to use without anybody totally describing it well." The ACE/INA retroactive contract: ~$1.25B premium for up to $2.5B of old liabilities. Real risks: (1) whether the full $2.5B would be paid, (2) how fast, (3) what could be earned on the float in between. "In the first quarter of last year in our 10-Q, you will see that we recorded a loss of $100 million because the payment pattern on that contract turned out to be faster than we anticipated. I mean, there was risk involved." "Non-traditional" is a better term than "finite." *Munger:* "you could hardly invent a worse word" than finite reinsurance; it is "just a meaningless rubric." See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md), [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md).

**★ A31 · Derivatives accounting: "stupid and dishonorable accountants."** *Buffett:* even with 23,000 contracts in the Gen Re book (not among the largest in the world), three years into unwinding it in a benign environment, "I don't think any regulator, and I'm not sure any auditor … can get their minds around evaluation." Both sides of a derivative contract often simultaneously show a profit — for something that "essentially will wash out as a zero sum." *Munger:* "Stupid and dishonorable accountants allowed the genie of totally improper accounting to come out of the bottle and descend in the derivative books of the world. Once that has happened and people have used it to create masses of assets and masses of earnings reports and bonuses and status … getting the genie back in the bottle is no small task because you have these huge vested interests who are fighting you. … What ordinary housewife, as she puts the toast in in the morning, is thinking, 'My god, I've got to do something about derivatives?'" See [derivatives](../concepts/derivatives.md), [gaap-distortion](../concepts/gaap-distortion.md).

## Connections to other sources

- The derivatives systemic-risk warning (M27, A31) continues directly from [berkshire-2003-meeting](berkshire-2003-meeting.md)'s "financial weapons of mass destruction" framing and the six-sigma-event argument; 2005 adds the specific GSE derivative-misuse mechanism, the "both sides simultaneously show a profit" accounting critique, and the forward supersession that AIG's own CDS exposure fulfilled the warning's thesis in 2008.
- The finite reinsurance investigation (M4, M26, M28) is the legal aftermath of a Gen Re/AIG transaction; the underlying product category was discussed at prior meetings; the 2005 meeting defines legitimate vs. abusive contracts most precisely because Buffett is simultaneously constrained from discussing specifics.
- The pricing-power agony test (M14) is the most compressed operational shorthand for the franchise economics argument Buffett developed across [berkshire-1991-letter](berkshire-1991-letter.md) and the 1995 meeting's moat-and-castle framework ([berkshire-1995-meeting](berkshire-1995-meeting.md) M25): the publisher who agonizes over a 20-cent increase is exactly the operator whose moat has eroded.
- "Wonderful lack of oversight" (A6) is the most direct statement of what [berkshire-1994-meeting](berkshire-1994-meeting.md) A24 called "mail all the money to Omaha" — the conglomerate model works because Berkshire adds capital mobility without adding management friction; 2005 adds the successor transmission-of-culture argument.
- The gold-vs.-productive-assets framework (M22) extends the inflation analysis from [berkshire-1977-letter](berkshire-1977-letter.md) and subsequent letters; 2005 sharpens it to a single diagnostic: can the asset earn in real terms regardless of currency denomination?
- "Patient capital" and the cycle-of-opportunity theme (M10) build forward from [berkshire-1994-meeting](berkshire-1994-meeting.md) M47 ("impossible to figure out when") — the 2005 version adds three concrete historical proof points (1973–74, 1998, 2002) that temporary market dysfunction rewards those who can wait, while acknowledging the current period is unfavorable.
- The earnings-expectations-as-fraud-root argument (M23) sharpens Munger's "conservative accounting protects business decision-making" thesis from [berkshire-2003-meeting](berkshire-2003-meeting.md) A16: the 2003 meeting focused on how over-optimistic accounting misleads managers; 2005 adds the psychological-pressure channel through which even honest managers get corrupted by communicated earnings targets.
