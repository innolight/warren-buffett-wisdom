# Berkshire Hathaway 2007 Annual Letter

The 2007 letter is best remembered for its three-tier business taxonomy — Great, Good, and Gruesome — which crystallizes Buffett's view of what separates a business worth owning forever from one that merely produces acceptable returns, and from one that destroys capital while growing. Backdrop: the U.S. housing bubble is bursting ("you only learn who has been swimming naked when the tide goes out"), but Berkshire's operating businesses are largely unaffected. Net worth gained $12.3B; book value per share up 11%.

## Key themes

- **Great vs. Good vs. Gruesome.** The central essay introduces a three-tier framework for business quality, using the savings-account analogy. A great business pays an extraordinarily high interest rate that *rises* over time — minimal reinvestment needed, earnings compound on a near-fixed capital base (See's Candies). A good business pays an attractive rate earned also on new deposits added — solid returns, but growth requires capital reinvestment (FlightSafety). A gruesome business pays an inadequate rate *and* forces you to keep adding money at those disappointing returns (airlines). The key corrective: **growth is not inherently good.** A business that grows rapidly but requires heavy capital to sustain that growth destroys value if returns on that capital are inadequate. See [great-good-gruesome](../concepts/great-good-gruesome.md).

- **Enduring moat as a requirement, not a nice-to-have.** A moat that must be continuously rebuilt will eventually be no moat at all. More distinctively: a business whose great results depend on a superstar manager does not have a great *business*. The medical practice led by your area's premier brain surgeon earns outsized returns while the surgeon is there — the moat goes when the surgeon goes. The Mayo Clinic, by contrast, has a moat that survives any individual. Long-term competitive advantage in a *stable* industry is the target. See [moat](../concepts/moat.md).

- **See's Candies: the 35-year arithmetic.** Bought for $25M in 1972. By 2007: sales $383M, pre-tax earnings $82M, tangible capital required $40M — with only $32M of additional capital invested over 35 years while cumulative pre-tax earnings reached $1.35B. The ratio of earnings generated to incremental capital deployed ($1.35B on $32M) is the empirical proof of the great-business archetype: a business that earns enormous returns without needing to reinvest those returns to produce next year's earnings. See [sees-candies](../entities/sees-candies.md).

- **Passive investing arithmetic.** The Dow advanced from 66 to 11,497 over the 20th century — only 5.3% compounded annually. To match that rate in the 21st century, the Dow would need to close at approximately 2,000,000 by December 31, 2099. Eight years in, it had gained fewer than 2,000 of those 1,988,000 needed points. Analysts forecasting 10% annual equity returns are implicitly projecting the Dow at 24,000,000 by 2100. The implication: helpers (active managers, consultants, intermediaries) as a class cannot outperform the market they collectively constitute; passive investors win by default because they avoid the fee drag. See [investment-friction](../concepts/investment-friction.md).

- **Dexter Shoe: worst deal confirmed.** Acquired in 1993 for $433M in Berkshire stock (25,203 A shares). By 2007, those shares were worth $3.5B, making the effective cost not $433M but $3.5B — a 1.6% slice of a business now valued at $220B exchanged for a worthless one. The compounding of a stock-for-acquisitions mistake is what makes it catastrophic: the buyer permanently surrenders ownership of a superior business to acquire an inferior one. See [dexter-shoe](../entities/dexter-shoe.md) and [stock-as-acquisition-currency](../concepts/stock-as-acquisition-currency.md).

- **Missed DFW television station.** Around the time of the See's purchase (1972), Tom Murphy offered Buffett the Dallas-Fort Worth NBC station for $35M. Buffett declined. By 2006 it earned $73M in a single year — cumulative earnings since of at least $1B — plus a capital value of ~$800M. Filed alongside the Dexter mistake under [mistakes-of-omission](../concepts/mistakes-of-omission.md).

## Notable quotes

> "A truly great business must have an enduring 'moat' that protects excellent returns on invested capital."

> "A moat that must be continuously rebuilt will eventually be no moat at all."

> "But if a business requires a superstar to produce great results, the business itself cannot be deemed great. A medical partnership led by your area's premier brain surgeon may enjoy outsized and growing earnings, but that tells little about its future. The partnership's moat will go when the surgeon goes. You can count, though, on the moat of the Mayo Clinic to endure, even though you can't name its CEO."

> "It's better to have a part interest in the Hope Diamond than to own all of a rhinestone."

> "You only learn who has been swimming naked when the tide goes out — and what we are witnessing at some of our largest financial institutions is an ugly sight."

> "Think now about this century. For investors to merely match that 5.3% market-value gain, the Dow — recently below 13,000 — would need to close at about 2,000,000 on December 31, 2099."

> "By using Berkshire stock, I compounded this error hugely. That move made the cost to Berkshire shareholders not $400 million, but rather $3.5 billion. In essence, I gave away 1.6% of a wonderful business — one now valued at $220 billion — to buy a worthless business."
