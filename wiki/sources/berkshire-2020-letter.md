# Berkshire Hathaway 2020 Annual Letter

The 2020 letter centres on two intellectual set pieces: the "Two Strings to Our Bow" essay (the clearest treatment in the letter series of why traditional conglomerates failed and how Berkshire's hybrid controlling/non-controlling structure escapes every failure mode) and the Apple repurchase compounding example (the most concrete illustration in the corpus of how a minority investor's ownership stake can grow without buying a single share). On the financial side, operating earnings fell 9% ($21.9B), the $11B PCC write-down is the series' most transparent acknowledgement of a valuation mistake, and share buybacks hit an all-time high ($24.7B, ~5% of shares).

## Key themes

- **Operating earnings vs. GAAP** — $42.5B GAAP ($21.9B operating + $4.9B realized gains + $26.7B unrealized gains − $11B PCC write-down); "operating earnings are what count most, even during periods when they are not the largest item in our GAAP total"; the two capital-gains components "fluctuate capriciously from year to year, reflecting swings in the stock market"
- **Look-through earnings restatement** — the letter's opening section restates the retained-earnings doctrine: investees' retained earnings are invisible under GAAP ("the huge sums that investees retain on our behalf become invisible") but build value for Berkshire through reinvestment, acquisitions, debt paydown, and repurchases; "those unrecorded retained earnings are usually building value – lots of value – for Berkshire"; Buffett explicitly cross-references the prior year's Smith/Keynes framing ("as we pointed out in these pages last year"); the Apple example below is the worked case of this effect compounding through dual-entity repurchases; context: $281B equity portfolio at yearend
- **PCC valuation mistake** — Berkshire purchased Precision Castparts in 2016 and took an $11B after-tax write-down in 2020; "No one misled me in any way – I was simply too optimistic about PCC's normalized profit potential"; Buffett explicitly separates the diagnosis: PCC is a fine business with a strong CEO (Mark Donegan), but the price paid was wrong; "I was right in concluding that PCC would, over time, earn good returns on the net tangible assets deployed… I was wrong, however, in judging the average amount of future earnings"; the write-down is a valuation error, not a quality-of-business error, and is distinct from the classic mistakes-of-omission framing elsewhere in the series
- **Two Strings to Our Bow** — the canonical explanation of traditional conglomerate failure: great businesses won't sell, so deal-hungry conglomerateurs were forced into mediocre ones → had to pay staggering control premiums → manufactured overvalued stock as acquisition currency ("I'll pay you $10,000 for your dog by giving you two of my $5,000 cats") → required deceptive or fraudulent accounting to sustain the illusion → eventual collapse; Berkshire's escape via dual controlling/non-controlling ownership: "owning a non-controlling portion of a wonderful business is more profitable, more enjoyable and far less work than struggling with 100% of a marginal enterprise"; capital is deployed into "whatever we believe makes the most sense… you are awarded no points in business endeavors for 'degree of difficulty'"
- **Apple repurchase compounding** — Berkshire began buying [apple](../entities/apple.md) in late 2016, owned 5.2% ($36B cost) by mid-2018; sold a portion in 2020 for $11B; despite that sale, now owns 5.4% because Apple has continuously repurchased its own shares; concurrent Berkshire buybacks mean investors own a full 10% more of Apple's assets and future earnings than in July 2018; "The math of repurchases grinds away slowly, but can be powerful over time"; the most concrete worked example in the corpus of compounding ownership through dual-entity buybacks
- **CEOs and repurchase timing** — "American CEOs have an embarrassing record of devoting more company funds to repurchases when prices have risen than when they have tanked. Our approach is exactly the reverse." Berkshire repurchased the equivalent of 80,998 "A" shares ($24.7B) in 2020, ~5.2% of shares outstanding — the all-time high for the series at that point; Buffett emphasises the dual criterion: intrinsic-value accretion and maintenance of ample funds for opportunities
- **Shareholder bucket taxonomy** — five buckets: (1) Buffett as founder, emptying annually via philanthropy; (2) index funds on autopilot, owning only because required to; (3) active institutional managers; (4) individual traders; (5) one million-plus long-term "partner" investors; Phil Fisher restaurant analogy: "At Berkshire, we have been serving hamburgers and Coke for 56 years. We cherish the clientele this fare has attracted." "We already have the investors we want and don't think that they, on balance, would be upgraded by replacements." Stan Truhlsen (Omaha ophthalmologist, Emdee Ltd. partner since 1959, turned 100 in 2020) is offered as the prototype fifth-bucket holder
- **Float and bond environment** — $138B float, described as "costless on a cumulative basis"; 10-year Treasury at 0.93% at yearend, "fallen 94% from the 15.8% yield available in September 1981"; bond investors face "a bleak future"; Berkshire's equity-heavy insurance investment strategy is a structural advantage unavailable to bond-constrained competitors for regulatory and credit-rating reasons; cautions that reaching for yield in shaky obligations is not the answer (savings-and-loan cautionary reference)
- **BNSF and BHE** — capital-intensive regulated infrastructure compounds durably through cycles when managed with discipline: combined earnings rose from $4.2B (2011, BNSF's first full year) to $8.3B (2020), even as BNSF absorbed a 7% volume decline — management expanded profit margin 2.9pp through cost control, demonstrating the social-compact model yields earnings growth regardless of cycle; BNSF carries ~15% of all U.S. non-local ton-miles by freight volume; BHE committed to an $18B grid modernisation project (2006–2030) — "a societal necessity" — and has paid no dividends in 21 years of Berkshire ownership, compounding earnings from $122M to $3.4B; both businesses expect decades of capital deployment at appropriate incremental returns (Katie Farmer succeeded Carl Ice as BNSF CEO at yearend 2020)
- **Never bet against America** — entrepreneurship stories (See's Candy founded by Mary See; GEICO started with $100K by Leo and Lillian Goodwin; National Indemnity from Jack Ringwalt's $125K; Nebraska Furniture Mart built by Rose Blumkin from $2,500; Clayton Homes and Pilot Travel Centers each from University of Tennessee graduates with negligible capital) frame Buffett's structural optimism; "In its brief 232 years of existence, however, there has been no incubator for unleashing human potential like America."
