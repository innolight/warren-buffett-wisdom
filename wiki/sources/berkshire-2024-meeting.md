# Berkshire Hathaway 2024 Annual Meeting

Held May 4, 2024, in Omaha — the first meeting since Charlie Munger's death on November 28, 2023 — the 2024 gathering is quieter and more reflective than any in the prior three decades, though still characteristically unsentimentally productive. The only book sold at the bookstore is the fourth edition of *Poor Charlie's Almanack* (~2,400 copies on the first day). Greg Abel and Ajit Jain flank Buffett on stage; no third voice follows Buffett's remarks, and the silence where Munger's one-liners used to land is the meeting's most eloquent running theme. The substantive load is heavy nonetheless: a critical succession update (Abel will control the entire investment portfolio, not just operations); the first explicit articulation of the tax logic behind Apple trimming; Buffett's deepest statement yet on AI risk; a frank accounting of GEICO's catch-up on data analytics; a warning that Berkshire Hathaway Energy will stop deploying capital wherever the regulatory compact breaks down; and a record cash balance ($182B at quarter-end, headed toward $200B) that Buffett refuses to apologize for.

## How to read the Q&A digest

- **★** marks a timeless, load-bearing exchange worth citing in the future; unmarked entries carry investor value but are secondary or period-specific.
- **Three voices: Buffett, Abel, Jain.** No Munger lines appear in this digest — this is the first post-Munger meeting, and his absence is noted explicitly throughout. Where Abel or Jain gave the substantive answer, their name leads the entry; where both speak, both are credited. Abel now functions as vice chairman for all non-insurance operations; Jain for all insurance.
- **Buffett slips and says "Charlie" once** (M6, mid-answer on BHE/utilities), catching himself with "Oh, Charlie. I'm so used to it." The crowd applauds. The moment is noted in that entry.
- **M#/A#** map to numbered sections in the raw morning/afternoon transcript.

## Key themes

### Abel will own the investment portfolio — a clarification that changes succession scope

The most consequential sentence of the 2024 meeting is buried in an afternoon answer: "I think the responsibility ought to be entirely with Greg." Buffett explains that he once thought the common-stock portfolio might be handled differently from business acquisitions, but has changed his mind as capital has grown. "If you understand businesses, you understand common stocks." Ted and Todd may continue in some assisting role the board decides on, but the *responsibility* — strategic allocation across stocks, whole businesses, and any crisis-era opportunities — should sit with the CEO. Greg Abel's response underscores the capital-allocation principles that will carry forward: surplus cash in Treasuries, a fortress balance sheet, and every equity investment viewed as a business stake. This is a material update to the succession picture established at the 2021 meeting and should inform how shareholders evaluate Berkshire's future investment posture. See [capital-allocation](../concepts/capital-allocation.md) and [greg-abel](../people/greg-abel.md).

### Record cash is a discipline signal, not a failure — but patience has a scale limit

Cash and T-bills stood at $182B at the March 31 quarter-end; Buffett expected roughly $200B by June. The logic has not changed since 1994: "we wouldn't use it if it was at 1 percent" — cash piles up only when he cannot find things that meet the standard of "very little risk and can make us a lot of money." But the scale constraint is now explicit: at the size Berkshire has reached, even a Japan-style trade in a $30–40B range barely moves the needle, and most equity markets look like "things aren't attractive." The implied lesson for investors is that high cash is not a buy-the-market signal; it is a signal that Berkshire's required return has not been met. When the next 2008-style dislocation arrives, Abel will be the one positioned to deploy it — and Buffett says Abel may "have even more fun than I had" because the world is more financially intertwined and therefore more episodically vulnerable. See [liquidity-fortress](../concepts/liquidity-fortress.md) and [size-as-anchor-on-returns](../concepts/size-as-anchor-on-returns.md).

### Apple trimming is explicitly tax-driven, not a conviction shift

Berkshire sold ~115M Apple shares in Q1 2024, but Buffett says the view of Apple's business has not changed: "we own Apple, which is an even better business" than Coca-Cola or American Express. The sale is driven by the expectation that the federal tax rate on gains — currently 21% — will rise. "With the present fiscal policies, I think that something has to give… higher taxes are quite likely." By locking in some gains at 21% now, Berkshire is accepting a smaller dollar loss per unit than it would accept if forced to sell at 35% or 52% (both historical rates). Buffett adds: "We always hope at Berkshire to pay substantial federal income taxes. We think it's appropriate." Apple was expected to remain the largest common-stock holding at year-end, and was. The argument for a higher-tax future also explains the record cash balance: "the federal government owns a part of the earnings of the business we make… they can change that percentage any year." See [apple](../entities/apple.md) and [tax-deferral](../concepts/tax-deferral.md).

### AI is a genie out of the bottle — scam potential is "the growth industry of all time"

Buffett disclaims expertise ("I don't know anything about AI") but offers the two analogies that carry: the nuclear genie (once released you cannot get it back, and it "has been doing some terrible things lately") and a personal experience — he saw a flawless AI-generated deepfake of himself delivering a message he never said, in his voice and clothes, undetectable even to a spouse. "When you think of the potential for scamming people… it's going to be the growth industry of all time." The atom-bomb parallel is tighter than the 2023 version: in 2023 Buffett described AI as "the genie coming out of the bottle"; here he explicitly says "scamming has always been part of the American scene. But this would make me, if I was interested in investing in scamming, it's going to be the growth industry of all time." Like the bomb, AI may accomplish enormous good and enormous harm, and "I just don't know how that plays out." Greg Abel's follow-up describes Berkshire's businesses as using AI for operational efficiency — "early innings." See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

### Insurance discipline: one-year repricing is the moat against climate uncertainty; cyber is rat poison

Ajit Jain frames Berkshire's durable advantage in climate-exposed insurance as structural, not predictive: "our contractual liabilities are limited to a year in most cases. So at the end of a year, we get the opportunity to reprice, including the decision to get out of the business altogether." This one-year option converts an unknowable long-run trend (climate severity) into a sequence of annual repriceable bets. Jain's counterintuitive corollary: "climate change, much like inflation, done right, can be a friend of the risk bearer" — inflation in claim severity grows the premium base if you stay ahead of the curve on pricing. Cyber insurance is handled differently: the aggregation potential (one event could hit thousands of policies simultaneously, with no contractual cap on correlation) makes it structurally uninsurable at scale. Jain instructs his underwriters to assume they are "losing money" on every cyber policy regardless of premium, treating profitability as unknowable until sufficient data exists. Buffett's summary: "as Charlie would say, it may be rat poison." GEICO's turnaround under Todd Combs is in progress: the underwriting expense ratio is below 10% (an industry-leading cost position), but rate-to-risk matching still trails Progressive; Jain expects parity by end of 2025. See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md), [super-cat-insurance](../concepts/super-cat-insurance.md), and [geico](../entities/geico.md).

### BHE/utilities: the regulatory compact must hold or Berkshire stops deploying capital

Berkshire Hathaway Energy faces two simultaneous pressures: wildfire litigation (PacifiCorp faces a $30B claim as of the meeting) and an enormous forward capital requirement to meet AI/data-center-driven demand doubling (Iowa) and tripling (Nevada) over the next decade. Buffett's stance is clear: "we won't throw good money after bad." Berkshire has the capital and the expertise to invest "a hundred billion or more" in the national grid build-out, but only under a regulatory compact that allows cost recovery. Abel's answer identifies Utah as "the gold standard" — it caps non-economic wildfire damages and creates a state wildfire fund. Oregon, by contrast, is awarding large non-economic damages despite case law suggesting otherwise. The principle generalizes: private capital will fund essential infrastructure at a reasonable return, but not at a negative one. "Whether you earn X or go broke is not an equation that works." See [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md) and [franchise-vs-business](../concepts/franchise-vs-business.md).

### The board's one job: pick the right CEO every twenty years

Asked about what happens when the "architect and general contractor" are gone, Buffett's answer is architectural: the board's entire job reduces to one decision made once every twenty years or so. "It would be absolutely crazy for anybody for our board of directors to ever pick anybody to run this business that thought you should retire at 65." The directors should resist conventional wisdom (which pressures boards to look busy and diversify their own network), pick from within when possible, and maintain a fast-correction mechanism. The real legacy isn't culture as slogan; it's an ownership structure, a balance sheet, and an acquisition pipeline that gives the right CEO more tools than anyone else in the world to act when others are paralyzed. Buffett's framing: "if they get lucky on Greg's mortality, they can do just what Rip van Winkle did and go to sleep for 20 years or so and then make another decision." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md) and [corporate-governance](../concepts/corporate-governance.md).

## Q&A digest — Morning session

**M3 · Q1 results: $182B in cash, goal is simple to describe.** *Buffett:* Q1 operating earnings of $11.2B, boosted by higher insurance income and investment income. "The economic goal at Berkshire is to increase the operating earnings and decrease the shares outstanding. It's that simple to describe." Cash and T-bills at $182B; probably ~$200B by Q2 end. Berkshire is generating "a little over a hundred million dollars per day." Shareholders' equity reached $574B at March 31; only JPMorgan ($338B) is in the same league, and it pays dividends. See [berkshire-hathaway](../entities/berkshire-hathaway.md).

**★ M4 · Apple trimming is tax-driven; fiscal policy makes higher rates likely.** *Buffett:* prefaces with Ben Graham's *The Intelligent Investor* as the turning point that reframed him from speculator to business-owner ("you've been wasting your time now… put it to better use"), then turns to Apple. Sold ~115M Apple shares in Q1. View of the business hasn't changed — "an even better business" than Coca-Cola or American Express. "At the end of the year, I would think it extremely likely that Apple is the largest common stock holding we have." Sale rationale: federal tax rate is 21% now, was 35% recently and 52% historically; "with the present fiscal policies, I think that something has to give, and that higher taxes are quite likely." Berkshire pays willingly: "if 800 other companies had done the same thing, no other person in the United States would have had to pay a dime of federal taxes." *Supersession note:* Apple did remain Berkshire's largest common-stock holding at year-end 2024 — Buffett's expectation proved correct. See [apple](../entities/apple.md), [tax-deferral](../concepts/tax-deferral.md).

**M5 · Japan: "overwhelmingly compelling"; American-oriented for big bets.** *Buffett:* Primary investments will always be in the U.S. Japan was the exception — "overwhelmingly, compellingly attractive." BYD and Costco were the two investments where Charlie "pounded the table… buy, buy, buy"; BYD was correct and he should have been more aggressive. Japan position: "Greg will be sitting with that at some point… I don't know how many years. We couldn't be happier." Will not make large commitments in most countries. See [japanese-trading-houses](../entities/japanese-trading-houses.md).

**★ M6 · BHE/utilities: "won't throw good money after bad"; Utah is the gold standard.** *Buffett:* Won't invest in utilities where costs cannot be recovered — "whether you earn X or go broke is not an equation that works." Could deploy "a hundred billion or more" in the national grid if the regulatory compact holds. *Abel:* Iowa utility demand doubles into the mid-2030s (AI/data centers); Nevada demand triples. PacifiCorp faces a $30B wildfire claim (an incremental filing on top of existing litigation) — Berkshire will challenge it. Utah passed legislation capping non-economic wildfire damages and creating a state wildfire fund: "the gold standard." Oregon is the counterexample. Culture change required: employees must now prioritize de-energizing lines during fire risk rather than keeping power on. *[Buffett mid-answer:] "Charlie? Oh, Charlie. I'm so used to it. (Laughter and applause)" — the one slip of the meeting.* See [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md).

**★ M7 · AI is a genie out of the bottle — scam potential is "the growth industry of all time."** *Buffett:* "I don't know anything about AI." But: "last year I said that we let the genie out of the bottle when we developed nuclear weapons, and that genie has been doing some terrible things lately." AI is analogous — partly out of the bottle and impossible to reverse. Personally saw a convincing AI-generated deepfake of himself, undetectable by family, delivering a message he never gave: "when you think of the potential for scamming people… scamming has always been part of the American scene. But this would make me, if I was interested in investing in scamming, it's going to be the growth industry of all time." Explicitly agnostic on outcomes: enormous potential for good and harm, no idea how it plays out. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ M8 · GEICO: cost advantage intact, rate-to-risk still catching up under Todd Combs.** *Jain:* GEICO has lagged Progressive in using data analytics to match rate with risk — "we are still behind." Underwriting expense ratio has been driven below 10% (a major competitive advantage), but rate-to-risk segmentation still trails. Expects parity "by the end of '25." *Buffett:* Cost advantage has "masked the fact that we could do without progressing as much as we should've." GEICO in March 2024 stopped losing policyholders; 16M+ in force. "It is not remotely a threat to survival." See [geico](../entities/geico.md), [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**M9 · Surround yourself with people who never deceive you.** *Buffett:* The most important trust lesson is from Charlie: "In all the years we worked together, not only never once lied to me ever but he didn't even shape things so that he told half-lies or quarter-lies." Asks the audience: figure out "who you'd want to start spending the last day of your life with, and then figure out a way to start meeting them tomorrow." On investments specifically: "if I didn't think I could do it myself, I wouldn't have done it… I talk to myself on investments." See [charlie-munger](../people/charlie-munger.md).

**★ M10 · Insurance and climate: one-year repricing converts uncertainty into manageable bets.** *Jain:* "Our contractual liabilities are limited to a year in most cases. So at the end of a year, we get the opportunity to reprice, including the decision to get out of the business altogether." This one-year option is why Berkshire can stay in climate-exposed lines others are abandoning. Prices need to go up "a lot" and Berkshire is trying to stay ahead of the curve. Regulators are "getting a little more realistic." Jain's insight: "climate change, much like inflation, done right, can be a friend of the risk bearer" — severity inflation grows the premium base if you price ahead of it. *Buffett:* GEICO grew from 7M in premium in 1950 (40 bucks a car) to 40B today; inflation and technology drove that, not volume alone. See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md), [super-cat-insurance](../concepts/super-cat-insurance.md).

**M11 · Canada: comfortable, "looking at one thing now."** *Abel:* Canada operates like an extension of the U.S. environment — comfortable with it, significant operations there. *Buffett:* "We do not feel uncomfortable in any way, shape, or form putting our money into Canada. In fact, we're actually looking at one thing now." But still must meet return standards. Secondary — no enduring principle beyond the circle-of-competence geography point. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ M12 · Ajit's succession: "won't find another Ajit" but the structure he built endures.** *Buffett:* "We won't find another Ajit. But… we've created a structure that didn't exist when he came in 1986. Nothing close to it existed with us or with anybody else… insurance is the most important business at Berkshire." The institutionalized advantages (scale, underwriting culture, float) outlast any individual. *Jain:* Nobody is irreplaceable — cites Tim Cook as proof; briefs the board annually, including naming a named successor he would hand the business to. "At the end of the day, as Tim Cook has proved to us, it'll be the biggest non-issue of the day." See [ajit-jain](../people/ajit-jain.md), [float](../concepts/float.md).

**M13 · Munger tribute: "peaking at 99."** *Buffett:* Charlie and he never had doubts about each other; found equal fun in success and in digging out of foxholes together. "I told him, in the last few years, I'd never seen anybody that was peaking at 99." Elon Musk and many others made the trek to visit him on his own. Charlie met "all the great people" by reading their books — "he didn't have to go have lunch with them." Message to audience: "who you'd want to start spending the last day of your life with — start meeting them tomorrow." *[Primary value in the personal domain; included for completeness.]*

**★ M14 · Cyber insurance: aggregation makes "worst-case cap" unknowable; Jain's "losing money" mindset.** *Jain:* Cyber is a $10B global market with ~20% profit margins — but Berkshire writes very little because the aggregation potential is unbounded. If one cloud outage hits thousands of policies simultaneously, the correlation is unlimited. "Each time you write a cyber insurance policy, you're losing money. We can argue about how much money you're losing, but the mindset should be, you're not making money on it, you're losing money." *Buffett:* The old question from 1968 urban riots applies: is a correlated loss one event or a thousand events? In cyber, the court could decide one event — wiping out any per-policy limit. "As Charlie would say, it may be rat poison." See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md), [model-risk](../concepts/model-risk.md).

**M15 · Solar/wind: intermittent without breakthrough storage; gas bridges remain necessary.** *Abel:* Iowa utility already hits 100% wind on some days (Earth Day example), but the next day gas is required. Battery technology is currently a "four-hour battery" — cannot bridge multi-day intermittency economically. Nevada's last two coal units retiring, replaced by gas. Full decarbonization requires storage breakthroughs that are not yet available. *Buffett:* "You can't create a baby in one month by getting nine women pregnant." Secondary — operational detail of BHE. See [midamerican-energy](../entities/midamerican-energy.md).

**M16 · Florida insurance: legal reform improving a distorted market.** *Jain:* Florida property and auto insurance has been impaired by legal costs ("the amount of corruption") and storm frequency/severity. Florida "believes in the free market more than California or New York" — more likely to find balance. Secondary — period-specific market color. See [underwriting-discipline](../concepts/underwriting-discipline.md).

**M17 · Life advice: find the work you'd do without needing it.** *Buffett:* "Find the job that you would like to have if you didn't need a job." Think backward from how you'd want your obituary to read (Charlie's advice), then choose the educational and social paths that lead there. Secondary — life-philosophy tangent. See [warren-buffett](../people/warren-buffett.md).

"This place, if anything happened to me, it would be working extremely well the next day." *Jain:* Buffett managed the transition skillfully by redirecting managers to Abel without making them feel rebuffed. *Abel:* Credits the quality of managers for making the handoff easy. "There are times when… Greg will know how to handle them as well or better than I have over time." Commission negotiation will now be explicit. Agents remain essential for most buyers: "it's the one time in our lives where we make these massive investments, and having that counsel and guidance is critical." *Buffett:* "I have not negotiated down the commission." Wants to grow the brokerage business. Secondary — operational update. See [berkshire-hathaway](../entities/berkshire-hathaway.md).

**M26 · No attempt to pick EV or climate winners.** *Buffett:* "I would certainly not know how to pick the winners in an industry like" EVs. ## Q&A digest — Afternoon session

"What is unusual about Berkshire is that a very significant number of Berkshire shareholders… have contributed a hundred million dollars or more to their local charities… I think is many multiples of any other public company." "I used to think differently about how that would be handled. But I think the responsibility should be that of the CEO." **★ A5 · Apple and "apperceptive mass": consumer behavior crystallizes into conviction.** *Buffett:* The Apple investment was preceded by years of observation — the Furniture Mart customers walking out to go to Best Buy rather than leave without an Apple product; learning about consumer behavior from the Baltimore furniture chain mistake; See's Candy as a study in brand psychology. Intellectual honesty and self-attribution of mistakes. See [mistakes-of-omission](../concepts/mistakes-of-omission.md).

**A7 · BNSF: cost reset needed; margins behind the other five Class I railroads.** *Abel:* BNSF made excellent operating progress in 2021, then "called 2022 a reset year" due to supply chain disruptions. Moving into 2023 without resetting the cost structure to match lower actual demand was a mistake. Now working to right-size costs against flat underlying rail demand: "the rail industry, if you go back many, many years, it's flat." Competition is not just other railroads but trucks. Precision scheduled railroading at competing roads is a benchmark. *Buffett:* UP had the same problem under prior management; BNSF is an essential-infrastructure asset, impossible to replicate. "We love owning a business like that." See [bnsf](../entities/bnsf.md), [commodity-business-economics](../concepts/commodity-business-economics.md).

**★ A8 · Small-cap conviction: read 20,000 pages of Moody's; love the game, not the money.** *Buffett:* With $1M he could earn 50% per year by going through Moody's manuals (he found interesting things on the Green Bay and Western Railroad as a young man — the bond that functioned like equity, the equity that functioned like a bond). "You have to be in love with the subject. You can't just be in love with the money." Charlie was impressed when they first met because Buffett knew obscure West Coast companies Charlie thought only he knew. "You try and know everything about everything small. And you'd find something." The method doesn't scale to Berkshire's capital — it's an early-career, small-capital method. Connects to: mind-set, not size, determines whether the method is deployed. See [cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses](../concepts/cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses.md), [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ A9 · Principal-agent problem: everybody who approaches you has an interest in your capital.** *Buffett:* On passive-fund managers and proxy-voting agents who forward non-shareholder-interest proposals: "almost everybody that approaches you, if you have tons of resources, has got some interest in figuring out how to use your resources to their advantage." Investment managers collecting 1% a year on Berkshire stock would be extracting $8B annually from shareholders who aren't getting dividends. The correct response: pay a commission once, own the stock. On Charlie's unusual trait: he understood all the psychological techniques for manipulating people but considered it "beneath him" to use them. "He got very rich by understanding the weaknesses of others, to some extent, and then decided it was very important to recognize these when they occur… but you don't have to stoop to using them yourself." See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md), [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md).

**A11 · Pilot dispute: "All's well that ends."** *Buffett:* On the Pilot Flying J accounting dispute and settlement: "A couple of the directors had their doubts about going in." Settlement is done; Pilot working out well. Quotes Sam Butler: "All's well that ends." No elaboration on lessons. See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**A12 · Pilot/Adam Wright: "only in America" story.** *Buffett:* New CEO Adam Wright grew up in Omaha, raised by a single mother, went to public high school (same as Buffett's wife), graduated from University of Omaha with all-time rushing record, was drafted by the New York Giants, suffered an injury, became a MidAmerican trainee, and now runs a 25,000-employee company. Jim Haslam (founder, University of Tennessee player) turned a single gas station into a national chain. "If you don't think this is a great country and has a lot of great people in it, all you've got to do is read [Wright's] podcast." See [berkshire-conglomerate-model](../concepts/berkshire-conglomerate-model.md).

**★ A13 · Luck: "anybody who says I did it all myself is just delusional."** *Buffett:* "You have to be just plain lucky. There's no question about it that there's a hundred or a thousand… [times] some drunk could've pulled out a car and broadsided me." Would not have bet on himself to live to 93 in high school. Luck in birth year, birth country, birth sex (acknowledges his sisters got different messages than he did; gender discrimination persisted deep into his lifetime). "You should make the most of your luck when you get it." The corollary: "anybody that says I did it all myself is just kidding. It's just — you're delusional." See [american-tailwind](../concepts/american-tailwind.md).

**A15 · AI revisited: too early to assess impact on Berkshire's businesses.** *Abel:* At the business unit level, AI is being explored for efficiency — displacing labor but hopefully creating internal opportunities. "Early innings." *Buffett:* Keynes in ~1930 correctly predicted per-capita output growth but failed to predict what humans would do with the resulting leisure time: "he was right about what was going to go into the equation, but he didn't have it figured out exactly what would be the result." AI is in the same category. See [circle-of-competence](../concepts/circle-of-competence.md).

**★ A16 · Fiscal deficit, not debt quantity, is the risk; Volcker as the template.** *Buffett:* U.S. debt will remain acceptable for a long time — no real alternative to the dollar as reserve currency. "It won't be the quantity. It'll be whether, in any way, inflation would get let loose in a way that really threatened the whole world economic situation." Paul Volcker is the example: willing to act decisively against inflation despite threats on his life; "the man for that crisis." Jay Powell understands fiscal is the real issue but does not control it: he sends "a kind of disguised plea for, please — pay attention to this." Buffett: "I don't worry about the quantity. I worry about the fiscal deficit." See [bonds-and-inflation](../concepts/bonds-and-inflation.md).

**A17 · Be kind.** *Buffett:* Asked if he would change his priorities if starting over: "I don't believe in lots of self-criticism." On the one aspiration that's within everyone's reach: "you can be kind. And then the world's better off." Brief — life-philosophy addendum. See [warren-buffett](../people/warren-buffett.md).

**★ A18 · Charlie's ethical bequest / philanthropy: "if you're lucky, make sure a bunch of other people are lucky, too."** *Buffett:* Charlie's will contained an unusual provision — "an ethical bequest" imposing duty, not property, as the primary inheritance. Asked what his own ethical bequest to Berkshire shareholders would be: "I'd probably say read Charlie." Then: "If you're lucky in life, make sure a bunch of other people are lucky, too." The Ruth Gottesman donation is the concrete example: turning deferred consumption accumulated over decades into 150 future doctors who start debt-free. "That's why Charlie and I have had such fun running Berkshire." See [owner-orientation](../concepts/owner-orientation.md), [charlie-munger](../people/charlie-munger.md).
