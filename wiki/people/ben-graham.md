# Ben Graham

Author of *Security Analysis* (1934, with David Dodd) and *The Intelligent Investor* (1949). Buffett's teacher at Columbia and the intellectual progenitor of value investing as a discipline. Does not appear directly in any of the 1977–1984 Berkshire letters in person, but is the implicit anchor for many of Buffett's framings and is explicitly invoked at two pivotal moments in this stretch.

## Views and contributions

Graham's central contributions, as they show up in Buffett's letters:

- **Investment as business-ownership.** Treat a share of stock as a pro-rata portion of an underlying business; value it by what the business can produce, not by what other people will pay for it. This is the foundation of [[acquisition-criteria]] and of the "businesslike" framing Buffett extends even to bonds in [[bonds-as-a-business]].
- **Margin of safety.** Buy at a meaningful discount to estimated value so that errors of estimation and bad luck do not destroy you.
- **Mr. Market.** The market as a manic-depressive business partner who offers you a price daily; the right response is to use his moods, not to be governed by them. The 1983 stock-splits / shareholder-eugenics essay and the 1982 "manic-depressive lemmings" framing are Graham's voice through Buffett.

## The evolution beyond Graham

The 1983 letter and its Goodwill appendix mark the most explicit acknowledgment in the early letters that Buffett's thinking has moved beyond Graham's original tangible-asset preference:

> My own thinking has changed drastically from 35 years ago when I was taught to favor tangible assets and to shun businesses whose value depended largely upon economic Goodwill. This bias caused me to make many important business mistakes of omission, although relatively few of commission. ([[berkshire-1983-letter]])

Buffett's appraisal of why the escape was hard: "most of what I had been taught by the same teacher had been (and continues to be) so extraordinarily valuable." Keynes's line, also quoted in 1983, captures the dynamic: "The difficulty lies not in the new ideas but in escaping from the old ones."

See [[economic-goodwill]] for the substantive content of the shift.

## Direct citations

> Investment is most intelligent when it is most businesslike. — Graham, *The Intelligent Investor*, final chapter; quoted in [[berkshire-1984-letter]] alongside Buffett's appraisal that *The Intelligent Investor* is "by far the best book on investing ever written."
