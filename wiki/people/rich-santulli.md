# Rich Santulli

Founder and CEO of Executive Jet Aviation (later branded [[netjets]]), and the inventor of the fractional aircraft ownership industry. Santulli created the category from scratch in 1986, built it into the dominant global operator, and continued running the business after [[berkshire-hathaway]] acquired it for $725M in August 1998. He had no financial need to work — like most Berkshire managers, he stayed because the business was his creation and he wanted to see how far he could take it.

## Views and contributions

**Inventing the category.** Santulli did not improve an existing service; he imagined a new one. The 1998 letter describes him as having "created the fractional ownership industry in 1986, by visualizing an important new way of using planes. Then he combined guts and talent to turn his idea into a major business." By 1997 — eleven years after founding — EJA accounted for 31% of all corporate jets ordered globally. Buffett and Santulli believed in 1998 that the potential had "barely been scratched."

**The multi-manufacturer model.** The competitive architecture Santulli built was deliberately open. EJA offered aircraft from Boeing, Gulfstream, Falcon, Cessna, and Raytheon, while its two main competitors were subsidiaries of single manufacturers restricted to selling only their parent's planes. Buffett's formulation in the 1998 letter: "NetJets is like a physician who can recommend whatever medicine best fits the needs of each patient; our competitors, in contrast, are producers of a 'house' brand that they must prescribe for one and all." Santulli designed this structural advantage from the start — product breadth as a durable moat.

**Service obsession as organizational DNA.** Buffett's most direct characterization of Santulli as an operator appears in the 2001 letter: "An uncompromising insistence on delivering only the best to his customers is embedded in the DNA of Rich Santulli, CEO of the company and the inventor of fractional ownership." Santulli's service commitments translated into concrete operational choices: pilots received an average of 23 days of training per year, each flew only a single aircraft model (eliminating cockpit-switching risk), and each logged roughly 800 hours of flying per year. Buffett endorsed these choices both on principle and from self-interest — he, his wife, his children, his sisters, his 94-year-old aunt, and most Berkshire directors flew the NetJets program.

**Scale as competitive strategy.** Santulli understood early that the fractional business has a compounding service advantage for the largest operator: more planes in the air means lower dead-head positioning costs and faster availability for any customer anywhere. "The company with the most planes in the air worldwide will be able to offer its customers the best service," per the 1999 letter's articulation of this principle. By 2001, NetJets had more than 300 planes constantly aloft in the U.S., producing positioning-cost advantages smaller competitors could not match.

**European expansion as conviction.** When competitors abandoned Europe, Santulli did not. By 2003, European management-and-flying revenues had grown 88%, 61%, and 77% in consecutive years, even as the operation remained unprofitable. Buffett backed the commitment: "Any company that forsakes Europe, as all of our competitors have done, is destined for second-tier status."

**Founder-manager retained.** The Berkshire acquisition preserved Santulli's autonomy completely. The 1998 letter notes he had no economic need to work and "spends his time at EJA because it's his baby — and he wants to see how far he can take it." In the 2000 letter, Buffett describes him simply as "the dynamo behind this business." This is the [[acquisition-criteria]] pattern at its cleanest: Berkshire as permanent capital providing the balance sheet for a founder-manager who can run without quarterly-earnings pressure or board interference.

## Personal relationship with Buffett

Buffett was a NetJets customer before he was an owner. Frank Rooney of H.H. Brown introduced him to Santulli; by 1995, Buffett had purchased a quarter share of a Hawker 1000 (200 hours per year) — which Santulli reportedly sold him in about 15 minutes. By 1998, the Buffett family had flown 900 hours on 300 trips. Buffett did a testimonial advertisement for EJA before any thought of acquisition arose — then asked Santulli to call if he ever considered selling. Santulli called in May 1998. The deal closed in August.

By 1999, two Berkshire directors had signed up for the program. Charlie Munger enrolled the same year — which Buffett described as "the ultimate test" of the service's value.
