# Lorimer Davidson

"Davy." Long-time GEICO executive (Assistant to the President in 1951, eventually CEO). The man who, on a single Saturday afternoon in January 1951, taught the 20-year-old Warren Buffett "how the insurance industry functions" and pointed him toward what became — across four decades — Berkshire's largest and most consequential single subsidiary acquisition.

## Why he matters

The 1995 letter (written in March 1996, after Berkshire's announcement that it would acquire the remaining 50% of [geico](../entities/geico.md) for $2.3B) places Davidson at the origin of the entire Berkshire-GEICO arc:

> Davy has been one of my heroes for the 45 years I've known him, and he's never let me down. You should understand that Berkshire would not be where it is today if Davy had not been so generous with his time on a cold Saturday in 1951. ([berkshire-1995-letter](../sources/berkshire-1995-letter.md))

Three threads from that 1951 Saturday recur in Buffett's framework for the rest of his career:

1. **The low-cost-operator advantage as the structural moat.** Davidson explained how GEICO's direct-marketing model gave it a cost advantage no agent-based competitor could replicate without destroying its own distribution. This becomes Buffett's canonical case study of [moat](../concepts/moat.md) in financial services — restated in the 1980 "franchise vs. turnaround" reframing and again in the 1995 100%-acquisition essay.

2. **Where to learn the insurance industry.** Davidson's afternoon was, per Buffett, the equivalent of a graduate-level course in insurance economics. The willingness of an unknown senior executive to spend four hours teaching a Columbia student with no credentials beyond his connection to [ben-graham](ben-graham.md) (then GEICO's chairman) is the kind of unrequited generosity Buffett returns to as a recurring theme about character.

3. **The link to Graham.** Buffett's path to Davidson ran through Graham — Buffett looked up Graham in *Who's Who in America*, discovered Graham chaired GEICO, took a train to Washington on a Saturday, found the offices closed, pounded on the door until a custodian appeared, and was directed to Davidson on the sixth floor. The chain of personal connection is itself part of the moral of the story: cold-call diligence by a 20-year-old eventually produces an investment outcome of historic scale, but the mechanism is unrushed personal relationship rather than transactional access.

## The 1951 Buffett-GEICO sequence

The story Buffett tells in the 1995 letter:

- **September 26, 1951:** the date of Buffett's fourth and final personal purchase of GEICO shares that year. By yearend 1951 his 350 shares had cost $10,282 and were worth $13,125 — more than 65% of his net worth. (Buffett notes that he "kept copies of every tax return I filed, starting with the return for 1944," allowing him to reconstruct the dates.)
- **Late 1951:** Buffett writes a short report on GEICO for "The Security I Like Best" column in *The Commercial and Financial Chronicle*.
- **1952:** Buffett sells the entire GEICO position for $15,259, primarily to switch into Western Insurance Securities ("a p/e ratio that for some reason caught my eye").
- **By the early 1970s:** the GEICO stock Buffett had sold "grew in value to about $1.3 million" — the canonical lesson "about the inadvisability of selling a stake in an identifiably-wonderful business" (see [mistakes-of-omission](../concepts/mistakes-of-omission.md)).

## The 1995 acquisition and the personal cost

The 1995 letter's final note on Davidson:

> Our acquisition of 100% of GEICO caused Davy to incur a large tax. Characteristically, he still warmly supported the transaction.

By 1995, Davidson was 93 and continued to "pay close attention to GEICO" and to be available to its successive CEOs — Jack Byrne, Bill Snyder, and Tony Nicely.

## The 1997 tribute and death (November 1999)

The 1997 letter found Davidson at 95 and offered a second personal tribute, spanning what was then a 47-year friendship: "I might never have grown to understand the whole field of insurance" without him ([berkshire-1997-letter](../sources/berkshire-1997-letter.md)).

Davidson died in November 1999, days after his 97th birthday. Buffett, Tony Nicely, and Lou Simpson had visited him in August. Buffett's tribute in the 1999 letter:

> For me, he was a friend, teacher and hero. Clearly, my life would have developed far differently had he not been a part of it. ([berkshire-1999-letter](../sources/berkshire-1999-letter.md))

His death closed a personal arc that began on that cold January 1951 Saturday in Washington and ran, through the 1976 GEICO crisis and the 1995 100%-acquisition, for nearly half a century. Davidson died just four years after Berkshire completed the acquisition whose origins he had set in motion.
