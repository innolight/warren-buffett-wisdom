# Tom Murphy

CEO of [[capital-cities]] (later Capital Cities/ABC) for most of the 1965–1995 period. Buffett's most frequently invoked exemplar of the operator-allocator combination — what the 1981 letter calls a "twofer." Joins the Disney board through the 1995 Cap Cities/Disney merger.

## Why he matters

The most concentrated Buffett tribute appears in the 1995 letter:

> To put it simply, he is as fine an executive as I have ever seen in my long exposure to business. Equally important, he possesses human qualities every bit the equal of his managerial qualities. He's an extraordinary friend, parent, husband and citizen. In those rare instances in which Murph's personal interests diverged from those of shareholders, he unfailingly favored the owners. When I say that I like to be associated with managers whom I would love to have as a sibling, in-law, or trustee of my will, Murph is the exemplar of what I mean.
>
> If Murph should elect to run another business, don't bother to study its value — just buy the stock. ([[berkshire-1995-letter]])

Murphy is the recurring reference point for what Buffett means by "ability and integrity" in the same person, and by the Category-1 + Category-2 manager — someone who both recognizes a mispriced business and operates it disciplinedly thereafter.

## Views and contributions

- **The repurchase discipline.** Murphy is repeatedly cited (starting in the 1981 letter) as among the managers who recognize that when no acquisition meets the standard, repurchases at sub-intrinsic-value prices are the right use of capital. This is the operational embodiment of [[capital-allocation]] discipline.
- **Cost discipline in good times as in bad.** The 1990 letter pairs Murphy with Carl Reichardt of Wells Fargo as the canonical pair-of-pairs: managers who "attack costs as vigorously when profits are at record levels as when they are under pressure." Cost discipline as a structural feature, not a cyclical response.
- **Head count discipline.** Same passage: "pay able people well, but abhor having a bigger head count than is needed." Lean staffing as a sign of operating clarity.
- **Letting ability rather than ego determine scope.** Same: "stick with what they understand and let their abilities, not their egos, determine what they attempt." The avoidance of empire-building.
- **Voting Berkshire's shares.** The 1985 Cap Cities/ABC deal includes Berkshire's delegation of voting rights to Murphy (and after him Dan Burke) for an extended period — the friendly-block-holder model that originated with [[geico]] and [[washington-post]] (see [[capital-cities]]). The arrangement requires the trustworthiness Buffett has personally observed in Murphy.

## Cap Cities operating record

Murphy's three-decade tenure at Cap Cities took the company from a regional broadcaster into one of the largest U.S. media companies through disciplined acquisitions (most consequentially ABC in 1985, partly financed by Berkshire) and operational excellence at each unit. The Berkshire premium paid for Cap Cities ($172.50/share in 1985) was framed at the time as expensive ("no field for bargains") and accepted as the cost of the Murphy/Burke management team.

By the time of the 1995 Disney merger, Murphy's 1986–95 stewardship had been validated by both operating results and the Disney exchange ratio, which delivered Berkshire roughly $1.27B of value for shares that 1993 sales had earlier valued at $635M (the Cap Cities sale Buffett returns to as one of his canonical mistakes — see [[mistakes-of-omission]]).
