# Carl Reichardt

CEO of [wells-fargo](../entities/wells-fargo.md) until his retirement in 1994. With Paul Hazen as President (and successor), led Wells Fargo through the 1980s and early 1990s with operating discipline that made it Buffett's exemplar of well-managed banking — and the principal reason Berkshire was comfortable entering banking as an industry.

## Why he matters

Reichardt is one half of the recurring Buffett pairing of operating partnerships. The 1990 letter introduces him alongside Hazen explicitly as analogous to [tom-murphy](tom-murphy.md) and Dan Burke at [capital-cities](../entities/capital-cities.md):

> With Wells Fargo, we think we have obtained the best managers in the business, Carl Reichardt and Paul Hazen. In many ways the combination of Carl and Paul reminds me of another — Tom Murphy and Dan Burke at Capital Cities/ABC. First, each pair is stronger than the sum of its parts because each partner understands, trusts and admires the other. Second, both managerial teams pay able people well, but abhor having a bigger head count than is needed. Third, both attack costs as vigorously when profits are at record levels as when they are under pressure. Finally, both stick with what they understand and let their abilities, not their egos, determine what they attempt. ([berkshire-1990-letter](../sources/berkshire-1990-letter.md))

The framing is Buffett's clearest articulation of what separates a great CEO from a mediocre one in any industry — and Reichardt is the banking-industry instance. The 1994 letter on retirement:

> Two CEO's who have done great things for Berkshire shareholders retired last year: Dan Burke of Capital Cities/ABC and Carl Reichardt of Wells Fargo. Dan and Carl encountered very tough industry conditions in recent years. But their skill as managers allowed the businesses they ran to emerge from these periods with record earnings, added luster, and bright prospects. Additionally, Dan and Carl prepared well for their departure and left their companies in outstanding hands. ([berkshire-1994-letter](../sources/berkshire-1994-letter.md))

## What Reichardt operationally did

Distilled from the 1990 letter:

- **Cost discipline in both directions.** Same intensity attacking costs when profits are at record levels as when under pressure. Discipline as structural, not as reactive.
- **Lean head count.** "Pay able people well, but abhor having a bigger head count than is needed." Quality-over-quantity staffing.
- **Stayed within understanding.** Wells avoided the speculative real-estate and LDC-lending excesses that destroyed many peers. When Wells was punished in the 1990 sell-off, it was on fears about California real estate — but Wells's actual loss experience proved manageable, vindicating the discipline.
- **High ROE on conservatively-leveraged banking.** "Wells Fargo is big — it has $56 billion in assets — and has been earning more than 20% on equity and 1.25% on assets."

The recruitment-difficulty point Buffett notes in 1990 is structurally telling: "no one … has been able to hire the dean." Wells Fargo executives had become the most sought-after in the banking business. The credit for that institutional culture is Reichardt's.

## Why Reichardt matters beyond Wells

Buffett's general thesis on banking — see [wells-fargo](../entities/wells-fargo.md) and [institutional-imperative](../concepts/institutional-imperative.md) — is that the industry's 20:1 leverage makes the difference between a good manager and a mediocre one structurally larger than in less-leveraged industries. Mistakes on a small percentage of assets destroy a large percentage of equity. Reichardt is the case study Buffett uses to demonstrate that good managers in banking *do* exist — and that the right response is not to avoid the industry but to insist on the few who can be trusted.

The pairing of Reichardt/Hazen with Murphy/Burke is also a piece of Buffett's broader framework about *pairs* of managers. The recurring observation: extraordinary operating outcomes correlate with pairs of CEOs/Presidents who genuinely complement each other and trust each other. The successful pair is itself an institutional fact, distinct from the individual brilliance of either member.
