# Lou Simpson

Investment manager. Joined [geico](../entities/geico.md) in late 1979; ran its common-stock portfolio from then onward; became Vice Chairman of GEICO in 1985 alongside Bill Snyder as Chairman. With Berkshire's 1996 acquisition of 100% of GEICO, Simpson becomes an integral part of Berkshire's investment-side capacity and an explicit successor plan for the role Buffett occupies.

## Why he matters

Three durable contributions to the Berkshire story:

1. **The cleanest "second Buffett" track record.** Simpson runs the GEICO equity portfolio as a concentrated, value-oriented operation with discipline and temperament closely aligned to Buffett's own. The 1986 letter, with characteristic understatement, prints the comparison table — Simpson's 1980-86 equity returns at GEICO averaged dramatically above the S&P 500 — and Buffett notes the embarrassment of having to report on a peer's superior record. The 1995 update:

   > Between 1980 and 1995, the equities under Lou's management returned an average of 22.8% annually vs. 15.7% for the S&P. Lou takes the same conservative, concentrated approach to investments that we do at Berkshire, and it is an enormous plus for us to have him on board. ([berkshire-1995-letter](../sources/berkshire-1995-letter.md))

2. **The succession solution.** Buffett's repeated articulation: Simpson's presence at Berkshire (post-GEICO acquisition) provides an immediate, available, philosophically-aligned investment manager should anything happen to Buffett and Munger. The 1995 letter: "His presence on the scene assures us that Berkshire would have an extraordinary professional immediately available to handle its investments if something were to happen to Charlie and me."

3. **Demonstrating the methodology in practice.** Simpson's record is a real-time refutation of Efficient Market Theory (compare the 1988 letter's argument). Same principles, same long horizon, same concentration discipline — applied independently — produce the same kind of outperformance. The Buffett method is not idiosyncratically Buffett-personal; it can be replicated by a different operator who shares the framework.

## Views and contributions

- **Concentrated value investing.** "Conservative, concentrated approach" (1995). Simpson's portfolio runs to a small number of high-conviction positions, similarly sized to be meaningful relative to the GEICO equity base.
- **Risk minimization at the individual-security level.** The 1986 framing: "Lou has consistently invested in undervalued common stocks that, individually, were unlikely to present him with a permanent loss and that, collectively, were close to risk-free." The risk frame is Buffett's: permanent loss of capital, not price volatility (see [risk-vs-volatility](../concepts/risk-vs-volatility.md)).
- **Temperamental fit with Berkshire culture.** The 1984 letter: "Lou has the rare combination of temperamental and intellectual characteristics that produce outstanding long-term investment performance."

## The GEICO Capital Operations portfolio (1996–)

With Berkshire's full ownership of [geico](../entities/geico.md) from 1996, Simpson's portfolio became an explicit component of Berkshire's total investment operation — though structurally separate from Buffett's own. The 1996 letter reports Simpson outperformed the S&P 500 by 6.2 percentage points that year. By 1997, the GEICO equity portfolio had grown to approximately $2 billion, independently managed with holdings that sometimes overlap and sometimes diverge from Buffett's own positions.

**Separate compensation, separate accountability.** Simpson's pay is tied exclusively to investment performance over a rolling four-year period — not to underwriting results, not to overall GEICO performance. The rationale, stated in [berkshire-1996-letter](../sources/berkshire-1996-letter.md): "We think it foolish for an insurance company to pay bonuses that are tied to overall corporate results when great work on one side of the business … could conceivably be completely neutralized by bad work on the other." This is the cleanest insurance-specific application of the [incentive-compensation](../concepts/incentive-compensation.md) principle that each function should be measured on what it can actually control.

The structural logic extends beyond fairness: it prevents the investment manager from being penalized for underwriting cycles outside his control, which could otherwise induce the wrong kind of conservatism (holding cash to offset underwriting losses rather than investing when prices are attractive).

**Operational independence.** Simpson makes investment decisions for the GEICO portfolio without Buffett's involvement. The independence is both structural (separate accounts, separate reporting) and epistemic (Buffett does not advise or second-guess). This makes the Simpson track record a clean parallel test of the same investing methodology applied by a different practitioner — empirically validating that the results are method-driven, not idiosyncratically person-specific.

**The 25-year definitive record.** The 2004 letter publishes the most complete performance table in the letters' history: Simpson's annual equity returns at GEICO for every year from 1980 through 2004. The result: **20.3% annually vs. S&P 13.5%** — a 6.8 percentage point annual edge sustained over 25 years. Buffett: "Take a look at the facing page to see why Lou is a cinch to be inducted into the investment Hall of Fame." ([berkshire-2004-letter](../sources/berkshire-2004-letter.md))

The portfolio by 2004: approximately $2.5 billion of GEICO equities, with individual purchases typically in the $200–$300 million range, concentrated in companies smaller than those Buffett himself focuses on. Full delegation in practice: "You may be surprised to learn that Lou does not necessarily inform me about what he is doing. When Charlie and I assign responsibility, we truly hand over the baton." Buffett receives Simpson's transactions approximately ten days after the end of each month. "Sometimes, it should be added, I silently disagree with his decisions. But he's usually right." ([berkshire-2004-letter](../sources/berkshire-2004-letter.md))
