# Walter Schloss

Value investor, 1916–2012. Ran a limited partnership from 1956 to 2002 using simple Graham-based statistical methods. His 47-year record — dramatically outperforming the S&P 500 with no inside information, no analysts, and minimal overhead — is the most extended empirical refutation of efficient-market theory (EMT) in the letters.

## Views and contributions

**Graham distilled to its essence.** Schloss worked directly for Ben Graham before starting his partnership. His method required no computer, no analysts, no economist's forecasts — just a simple filter: buy stocks that are statistically cheap. When asked in 1989 how he would summarize his approach, his son and sole associate Edwin replied: "We try to buy stocks cheap."

He used "outside information only sparingly" and never came within a mile of inside information. The office infrastructure never exceeded four file cabinets and no secretarial support. This was not poverty but discipline: the method did not require more.

**47 years and ~1,000 securities.** Schloss did not concentrate. He held roughly 1,000 securities over the partnership's life, "mostly of a lackluster type." A few big winners did not account for the record — the performance was broad-based and repeatable. This matters for the EMT argument: luck might produce a decade of outperformance in a concentrated portfolio; it cannot explain 47 years across 1,000 positions.

**No fee unless investors made money.** From 1956 to 2002, Schloss "took not a dime unless his investors made money" — a direct contrast to the 2-and-20 structure Buffett critiques in the same 2006 letter. The alignment was complete.

**The statistical impossibility of chance.** Buffett's test: if millions of investors had mirrored Schloss's every trade (buy when he bought, sell when he sold), the *luckiest* of them would not have matched his record. "There is simply no possibility that what Walter achieved over 47 years was due to chance."

## The EMT connection

Buffett first publicly discussed Schloss's record in 1984 (in what became known as "The Superinvestors of Graham and Doddsville," published in the Columbia Business School alumni magazine). At the time, EMT was the dominant paradigm in business school investment instruction. Buffett's argument: if markets are always efficiently priced, no investor relying only on publicly available information should systematically outperform. Schloss's record — built entirely on public information, using methods learned from Graham — directly contradicts the theory.

The academic community's response, as Buffett tells it, was not to update the theory but to ignore the evidence: "To my knowledge no business school teaching EMT made any attempt to study Walter's performance and what it meant for the school's cherished theory. Instead, the faculties … went merrily on their way presenting EMT as having the certainty of scripture." Buffett's conclusion: maybe it was a good thing for his investors that Walter didn't go to college.
