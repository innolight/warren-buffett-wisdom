# Ralph Schey

CEO of [[scott-fetzer]] from 1977 onward, including throughout Berkshire's ownership (1986 onward). The canonical Buffett example of a manager who runs a diverse conglomerate of unglamorous businesses at extraordinary returns on capital — and the case study used in the 1994 letter on the intrinsic-value-vs-book-value divergence and in the 1994 letter on incentive compensation.

## Why he matters

Three durable contributions to the Berkshire story:

1. **The conglomerate-can-be-well-run counterexample.** Schey manages 19 (later 22) distinct businesses inside Scott Fetzer — World Book, Kirby, Campbell Hausfeld, and many smaller — without losing the operational detail of any of them. Buffett's recurring framing: "Ralph's mastery of the 19 businesses for which he is responsible is truly amazing." This stands against the standard critique of conglomerates as inevitably mismanaged through diffuse attention; Schey's record demonstrates that the failure is usually managerial, not structural.

2. **The intrinsic-value-vs-book-value case study.** The 1994 letter's extended Scott Fetzer accounting walk-through (see [[intrinsic-value-vs-book-value]]) is constructed around Schey's results: book value declining over time through dividends-greater-than-earnings, while underlying earnings climbed steadily — return on equity rising from "exceptional" at purchase to "truly extraordinary" by 1993. The 1994 letter: "Scott Fetzer's success comes from the managerial expertise of CEO Ralph Schey."

3. **The five-minute compensation arrangement.** The 1994 letter on incentive compensation:

   > I can't resist mentioning that our compensation arrangement with Ralph Schey was worked out in about five minutes, immediately upon our purchase of Scott Fetzer and without the "help" of lawyers or compensation consultants. This arrangement embodies a few very simple ideas — not the kind of terms favored by consultants who cannot easily send a large bill. … Our agreement with Ralph has never been changed. It made sense to him and to me in 1986, and it makes sense now. ([[berkshire-1994-letter]])

   The structure: bonuses tied to Scott Fetzer's results (not Berkshire's), with a high charge for incremental capital employed and an equally high credit for capital released. Symmetric incentives. The architecture exemplifies the [[incentive-compensation]] doctrine.

## Views and contributions

- **Capital release as well as capital deployment.** Distinctive among operating CEOs: Schey actively works to release capital that cannot earn high incremental returns inside Scott Fetzer, sending the cash to Berkshire for redeployment. The 1992 letter notes Scott Fetzer's distribution of more than 100% of its earnings to Berkshire across seven years while *increasing* the company's earning power.
- **Operational basics, executed well.** The 1994 letter's distillation:

   > Ben Graham taught me 45 years ago that in investing it is not necessary to do extraordinary things to get extraordinary results. In later life, I have been surprised to find that this statement holds true in business management as well. What a manager must do is handle the basics well and not get diverted. That's precisely Ralph's formula.

- **Honest reporting.** Same passage: "He's forthright about problems and is self-confident without being self-important." The first part — honest reporting of problems — is the practical operating discipline that allows Buffett to manage him at a distance.

## Scott Fetzer record under Schey

| Year | Pre-tax earnings | Capital base | ROE comment |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| 1986 | $55M (approx., 1st Berkshire yr) | $172.6M book | Already exceptional |
| 1992 | $110M | $116M | Record year |
| 1993 | (continuing growth) | (declining book) | Would have ranked 4th in Fortune 500 ROE if standalone (1st excluding bankruptcy-emergence companies) |
| 1994 | (continuing) | ~$94M | Pre-tax earnings now roughly 2× 1986 on declining capital base |
| 1997 | $118.9M | (continuing decline) | Buffett: "just warming up" in 1994 — see [[berkshire-1998-letter]] |
| 1998 | $96.5M after-tax | $112M | Record after-tax year; no leverage beyond conservative finance subsidiary |

The "rocking-chair savings account" anti-pattern from [[economic-goodwill]] (1985) — earning more by *putting up more* — is the opposite of what Schey does. Schey earns more by putting up *less*.

## Final years and Buffett's tribute (1996–2000)

Scott Fetzer's compounding continued through the late 1990s. Pre-tax operating earnings were $118.9M in 1997; in 1998 the business earned a record $96.5M after-tax on $112M of net worth, operating without financial leverage. When Buffett reported these results in [[berkshire-1998-letter]], he revisited his 1994 assessment — that Schey had been "just warming up" — to show how far the trajectory had extended beyond even that optimistic benchmark.

When Schey retired at year-end 2000, after fifteen years under Berkshire ownership, Buffett published the full retrospective in [[berkshire-2000-letter]]:

> Fifteen years ago, when we bought Scott Fetzer, I predicted that Ralph would be a star. What I didn't know then was just how much of a star. … The bottom line: $1.03 billion of distributions [to Berkshire] against a net purchase price of $230 million, and we still have a business worth well more than the $230 million investment.

Schey was inducted into "Berkshire's Hall of Fame."

The portrait that emerges from fifteen years of letters is of the **archetypal Berkshire operating manager**: runs his businesses without headquarters involvement; delivers consistent, compounding returns on a declining capital base; actively releases surplus capital rather than redeploying it at inferior returns internally; reports honestly about problems; and never asks for strategic help. See [[acquisition-criteria]] and [[incentive-compensation]]. The $1.03B cumulative distribution against a $230M investment is the empirical record of what that pattern produces.
