# Warren E. Buffett

Chairman of [[berkshire-hathaway]] since the 1965 change in corporate control. Author of the annual shareholder letter that anchors this wiki. Student of [[ben-graham]] at Columbia, partner of [[charlie-munger]] since the late 1970s. By the 1984 letter, Buffett and Munger together own 47% of Berkshire stock — a deliberate structural commitment to acting as owners rather than managers.

## Views and contributions

The framework established in 1977 expands materially across 1980–1984. The major additions to Buffett's investment philosophy in this stretch:

- **Look-through earnings as the primary economic measure.** Reported dividend income understates the value of partial-equity stakes; what matters is attributable earnings. The 1980 "iceberg" and "tree in the forest" formulations are canonical. By 1983 this is codified as principle #6 of the owner's manual. See [[look-through-earnings]].
- **Share repurchases as canonical capital allocation.** Sub-intrinsic-value repurchases are both arithmetic (immediately accretive) and behavioral (signaling pro-shareholder management). See [[share-repurchases]].
- **The kissing-toads acquisition critique.** Three motivations behind value-destroying deals; two categories of acquisition success. The Noah principle: "predicting rain doesn't count, building arks does." (1981)
- **The passive-return crossbar.** Equity value-added requires business ROE above after-tax passive returns; in 1981's high-rate environment most American businesses no longer cleared the bar. Inflation as corporate tapeworm. See [[passive-return-crossbar]].
- **The stock-as-acquisition-currency essay.** Issuing undervalued stock is a partial sale at a discount. "Part of A sold to acquire B" — the linguistic reframe. (1982) See [[stock-as-acquisition-currency]].
- **The commodity-business framework.** Over-capacity + undifferentiated product = poor profitability, unless rescued by a wide and sustainable cost advantage. Insurance as textbook case; GEICO as exception. See [[commodity-business-economics]] and [[moat]].
- **Discarding the beginning-ROE yardstick** (1982) for Berkshire's own scorekeeping, in favor of book value as a proxy for intrinsic business value (1983). See [[intrinsic-value-vs-book-value]].
- **The owner's manual** (1983) — 13 governing principles for the manager-shareholder relationship. See [[owner-orientation]].
- **Economic Goodwill** — Buffett's explicit acknowledgment of evolving past Graham's tangible-asset bias toward businesses with enduring economic Goodwill and minimal tangible capital. See [[economic-goodwill]].
- **Dividend policy framework** (1984) — restricted vs. unrestricted earnings; the $1-of-market-value per $1-retained test. See [[dividend-policy]].
- **Bonds as a business** (1984) — applying business-valuation discipline to fixed income; the WPPSS case. See [[bonds-as-a-business]].
- **Concentration as policy** (1984) — "If you have a harem of forty women, you never get to know any of them very well."

The continuing-from-1977 framework remains operational:

- **Return on equity, not EPS, as the management scorecard** for ordinary businesses (though, per 1982, discarded as the dominant Berkshire measure due to look-through earnings becoming dominant). See [[return-on-equity-yardstick]].
- **The four-criteria acquisition test** (1977) becomes the six-point operational ad (first published 1982). See [[acquisition-criteria]].
- **Tailwinds matter more than effort.** See [[tailwinds-vs-headwinds]].
- **Counter-institutional discipline** — fully articulated as the [[institutional-imperative]] over time; 1984 ties Berkshire's resistance to it explicitly to the owner-manager 47% stake.

## The 1986-1995 framework expansion

The framework continues to deepen materially across this decade, with several canonical additions:

- **Owner earnings (1986).** The formal formula and the cash-flow-fallacy critique. See [[owner-earnings]].
- **Mr. Market (1987).** The Graham allegory canonized as Buffett's. See [[mr-market]].
- **Capital allocation as the CEO's central skill (1987).** The 60% statistic; the musician-to-Federal-Reserve analogy. See [[capital-allocation]].
- **Mistakes of the First Twenty-five Years (1989).** Cigar butts vs. wonderful businesses; institutional imperative formally named; mistakes of omission. See [[cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses]], [[institutional-imperative]], [[mistakes-of-omission]].
- **Cost-of-float framework (1990).** The recurring scorecard table. See [[float]].
- **Volatility-as-opportunity / food-prices analogy (1990).** See [[volatility-as-opportunity]].
- **Franchise vs. business (1991).** The three-condition test; the valuation math. See [[franchise-vs-business]].
- **Margin of safety reaffirmed (1990, 1992).** See [[margin-of-safety]].
- **Risk vs. beta (1993).** The five-factor framework. See [[risk-vs-volatility]].
- **Concentration vs. diversification (1993).** Know-nothing vs. know-something investor. See [[concentration-vs-diversification]].
- **Compensation philosophy (1994).** The Schey arrangement; "alignment in both directions." See [[incentive-compensation]].
- **Float-as-equity (1995).** See [[float]].

## Major operational events (1986-1995)

- **1986:** Scott Fetzer acquired; Fechheimer Bros. acquired.
- **1987:** $700M Salomon convertible preferred purchased.
- **1988:** First major Coca-Cola purchase; Berkshire NYSE-listed November 29; David Dodd dies (see [[david-dodd]]).
- **1989:** Coca-Cola enlarged; three convertible preferreds purchased (Gillette, USAir, Champion); $902.6M zero-coupon debentures issued.
- **1991:** August — Buffett becomes Interim Chairman of Salomon during the Treasury-bond-trading-scandal crisis; serves through June 1992. See [[salomon]].
- **1995:** GEICO acquired in full ($2.3B for the remaining 50%); Helzberg's and R.C. Willey acquired; Cap Cities/Disney merger initiated (Sun Valley encounter with Eisner).

## The Lorimer Davidson tribute (1995)

The 1995 letter contains Buffett's most extended personal tribute to anyone outside his immediate family: the retelling of the 1951 Saturday meeting with [[lorimer-davidson]] at GEICO. The four-hour conversation with a 20-year-old student is framed as the origin event of Berkshire's largest single subsidiary acquisition forty-five years later. "Berkshire would not be where it is today if Davy had not been so generous with his time on a cold Saturday in 1951."
