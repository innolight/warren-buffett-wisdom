# Charlie Munger

Vice Chairman of [[berkshire-hathaway]] and Chairman of [[wesco-financial]] (from late 1983, succeeding Louie Vincenti). [[warren-buffett]]'s partner in managing Berkshire since the early 1970s. Operates from Los Angeles; Buffett operates from Omaha — distance, Buffett notes, "impedes us not at all." The Munger family holds more than 90% of its net worth in Berkshire stock.

## Views and contributions

### The intellectual shift: from cigar-butts to wonderful businesses

Munger's most consequential influence on Buffett's investing career is the gradual displacement of Graham's tangible-asset bias with a preference for businesses with enduring competitive advantages and minimal incremental capital needs. The 1989 letter makes the credit explicit: Buffett bought Berkshire (the textile mill) and Hochschild Kohn (a Baltimore department store) as "cigar butts" — cheap on book value, structurally mediocre. "Charlie understood early; Buffett was a slow learner." The [[sees-candies]] purchase is the pivot: "In our See's purchase, Charlie and I had one important insight: we saw that the business had untapped pricing power." That lesson seeded Berkshire's equity portfolio — Coca-Cola, Gillette, American Express — built on economic moat, not tangible assets. See [[economic-goodwill]] and [[cigar-butts-vs-wonderful-businesses]].

### Partnership structure and the "two jobs" framework

The 1986 letter distills Buffett and Munger's roles to two: (1) attract and keep outstanding managers, (2) allocate capital. Everything else is delegated. The 1982 letter puts the operating model plainly: "Charlie and I are interchangeable in business decisions. Distance impedes us not at all: we've always found a telephone call to be more productive than a half-day committee meeting." By 1984: "With 47% of Berkshire's stock, Charlie and I don't worry about being fired, and we receive our rewards as owners, not managers." The [[owner-orientation]] principles (1983 letter) were co-authored by both.

### Mental models attributed specifically to Munger

Three frameworks in the letters are explicitly Munger's:

- **Invert, always invert.** Named directly in the 1996 letter as the planning principle behind Berkshire's risk management: "All I want to know is where I'm going to die so I'll never go there." Applied to why Berkshire caps super-cat exposures and avoids heavy borrowing — reason backwards from worst outcomes. See [[inversion]].
- **"Just tell me the bad news."** Cited in the 1995 letter as Charlie's dictum on management reporting. "The good news will take care of itself." A compression of the honest-information-flow principle central to how Berkshire operates with its managers.
- **Lumpy returns over smooth.** "Charlie and I would much rather earn a lumpy 15% over time than a smooth 12%." Repeated across multiple letters (1990, 1996). The framing rejects the conventional institutional preference for smoothed earnings and volatility-minimization. See [[volatility-as-opportunity]].

### Judgment as a check on Buffett

The most revealing Munger reference in the 1996–2003 period is the Gen Re Securities confession in the 2003 letter — Buffett's most explicit acknowledgment that Munger had the right call and he did not: "Charlie would have moved swiftly to close down Gen Re Securities — no question about that. I, however, dithered." The dithering cost shareholders $173M pre-tax in 2002 and $99M in 2003, with thousands of derivative tickets still open years after the merger. This is the sharpest instance across all letters of Buffett naming Munger as the better decision-maker on a specific, material call. See [[berkshire-2003-letter]].

### Operator trust and talent spotting

A small but characteristic 1987 episode: K&W Products (a small Berkshire automotive-compound subsidiary) had stumbled. Munger called Harry Bottle — then 68, who had earlier rescued Buffett's 1962 Dempster Mill crisis — and installed him as CEO. Profits rose more than 300% in 1987; capital employed fell 20%. The pattern: find the right operator, define the problem, get out of the way.

### Capital allocation philosophy

The 1998 letter provides the most explicit joint statement of Berkshire's capital allocation style: "Charlie and I have the easy jobs at Berkshire: We do very little except allocate capital. And, even then, we are not all that energetic. We have one excuse, though: In allocating capital, activity does not correlate with achievement. Indeed, in the fields of investments and acquisitions, frenetic behavior is often counterproductive. Therefore, Charlie and I mainly just wait for the phone to ring." The 1993 letter captures the distilled investment posture: "In an investment lifetime it's just too hard to make hundreds of smart decisions. … We'll now settle for one good idea a year. (Charlie says it's my turn.)"

### Derivatives

The 2002 letter gives the sharpest joint statement on derivatives: "Charlie and I are of one mind in how we feel about derivatives and the trading activities that go with them: We view them as time bombs, both for the parties that deal in them and the economic system." Written before the 2008 crisis, this became one of the most cited passages from the letters.
