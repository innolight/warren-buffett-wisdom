# Ajit Jain

Insurance underwriter. Joined Berkshire's reinsurance operation in 1986. Built and runs Berkshire's super-catastrophe ("super-cat") reinsurance franchise — the line of business that becomes one of the principal drivers of Berkshire float growth from the early 1990s onward.

## Why he matters

Jain is the operating architect of a business that *only Berkshire can do at scale*. The super-cat product requires:

- An underwriter whose judgment can be trusted on policies where no statistical base exists,
- A balance sheet large enough to retain rather than lay off,
- A willingness to accept lumpy reported results in exchange for higher long-term return.

Jain provides the first; Berkshire's capital provides the second; Buffett's communication discipline provides the third. The combination produced what the 1992 letter called Berkshire as "the largest U.S. writer of super-cat business" and the 1994 letter, with Jain, as the carrier capable of writing single $400M risks same-day. The 1995 letter elevates Jain to "the guiding genius of our super-cat business."

The underwriting judgment is the irreplaceable input. Buffett's 1992 articulation:

> Ajit Jain, who runs our reinsurance operation, is simply the best in this business. In combination, these strengths guarantee that we will stay a major factor in the super-cat business so long as prices are appropriate. ([[berkshire-1992-letter]])

## Beyond super-cat

The 1995 letter expands the description of Jain's range. In addition to super-cat (which by definition covers many small loss events from a single mega-catastrophe), Jain writes "important non-cat business" — large single-loss policies where the underwriting judgment is similarly judgment-bound rather than data-driven. The 1995 examples:

1. The life of Mike Tyson for a sum large initially and gradually declining to zero over several years (fight-by-fight).
2. Lloyd's against more than 225 of its "names" dying during the year.
3. The launch and a year of orbit of two Chinese satellites.

The unifying feature of these policies: each requires bottom-up judgment because no actuarial table provides a credible base rate. Berkshire's edge in this market is the willingness to think rather than to lay off.

## Views and contributions

- **Pricing on exposure, not on competition.** The Berkshire stance — accept volume only at rates that compensate for the risk — is operationalized in Jain's underwriting. Volume falls dramatically when prices weaken; volume swells dramatically when industry capacity withdraws.
- **Concentration of risk rather than spreading.** Most reinsurers lay off large positions to syndicates of other reinsurers. Jain's policies are retained on Berkshire's own balance sheet. The 1990 letter quantifies: a Berkshire super-cat book of ~$100M premium can produce a $200-300M loss in a bad year — concentrated rather than diversified.
- **Same-day quotation on enormous limits.** The structural promise that buyers cannot get elsewhere: a definitive price quote within hours on coverage too large for any other underwriter to match.
