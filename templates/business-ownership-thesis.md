# Business Ownership thesis

This investment thesis applies Warrant Buffet Investment philosophy - owning "wonderful companies at a fair price".
The thesis is shaped with Four-M framework, covering Meaning (understanding of economic engine), Moat, Management, Margin of safety.

## Meaning — Do I understand the economic engine?

> The question is about *my* knowledge boundary, not the business's simplicity. A simple business can sit outside my circle; a complex one inside it if I have genuine edge. If I can't forecast the economics ten years out, the honest answer is that this falls outside my circle.

- **What & how.** What do they sell? How do they make money? Who buys, and why?
- **Why it's stable.** Why does that buying behavior persist over time?
- **Industry.** What is the industry structure? What are the headwinds and tailwinds?
- **Operating history.** Is there a long, stable track record — or is it short and unproven?

## Moat — Is the competitive advantage durable?

> Name the *specific mechanism*, not a generic claim ("strong brand" is not enough). Argue whether it is widening or narrowing, and what would have to change for it to erode.

- **Mechanism.** What specifically protects this business — cost advantage, brand/intangible, network effects, or switching costs? Why can't customers easily defect?
- **Trajectory.** Is the moat widening or narrowing? How long can it compound?
- **Pricing power.** Can they raise prices without losing volume? (Buffett: "the single most important decision in evaluating a business.")
- **Economic evidence.** Does the moat show up in the numbers — high ROE *without* leverage, surplus cash rather than capital consumed, persistent FCF growth?
- **Long-term prospects.** What does this look like in 10–20 years? What forces could disrupt it?
- **Inversion.** If a rival had unlimited capital, where would they attack — and would it work? A moat that survives a well-funded attacker is real; one that doesn't is a story.

## Management — Are they able and trustworthy?

> Lead with capital allocation — the most important and least-discussed management quality. Owner alignment is a supporting signal, not the lead one.

- **Capital allocation.** Have they reinvested retained earnings wisely? Resisted value-destroying acquisitions?
- **Retained-earnings test.** For every dollar retained, has at least a dollar of market value been created?
- **Candor.** Do they acknowledge mistakes honestly, or only celebrate wins? Does the letter read like a frank owner's report or a PR document?
- **Institutional imperative.** Do they resist the structural forces that corrupt even good CEOs — strategy that perpetuates itself regardless of results, subordinates validating whatever the boss wants, peers as benchmark, acquisitions to feed a craving for action?
- **Owner alignment.** Does pay tie to long-term per-share value? Do they act like owners, not hired hands?

## Margin of Safety — Is the price fair enough?

> Answer Buffett's Aesop test, derive an intrinsic value, then compare to price. The required margin scales *inversely* with certainty. A thesis that cannot name a price at which it would *not* buy is not a value thesis.

- **Owner earnings.** What is the true cash for owners — net income + D&A − maintenance capex − incremental working capital?
- **Intrinsic value (Aesop).** How much cash will the business produce over its life, when will it arrive, and with what certainty?
- **Price vs. value.** What is the current price, my value estimate, and is the gap meaningful?
- **Cushion.** Is the discount large enough to absorb errors in my estimate, given the dispersion of outcomes?

## Pre-mortem — What would prove me wrong, and what then?

> Argue the bull case *and* pre-commit to what would disprove it — written down now, before I'm emotionally long. Risk here is *permanent loss of capital*, not estimate error or volatility.

- What are critical risks the business is facing?
- **What would make me wrong.** What specific, *observable* facts would prove the moat is narrowing, the economics misjudged, or management untrustworthy?
- **Sell / buy-more discipline.** I exit only on moat erosion or a broken thesis — never on a price move. If price falls on unchanged fundamentals, do I buy more?
- **Risk of permanent loss of capital.** Would the business endure a once-in-a-century shock?
- **What I don't understand well.** What remains genuinely uncertain or outside my circle?

## Check lists

### Research Questions

Disqualifiers:
- Does it compete primarily on price with no durable low-cost advantage?
- Is it a Turnaround story? "Turnarounds seldom turn". A hoped-for improvement is not a moat.
- Does the business require debt to produce an acceptable return on equity?

Business Tenets
- Understanding. Do I understand it? How does it makes money? Who buy the products and why?
- Circle of Competence: Do *I* understand this business well enough to forecast its economics 10 years from now with confidence? Do I understand it well enough to have a lasting edge in valuing it?
- Consistent Operating History. Does the business have a long, stable track record of performance?
- Pricing Power. Can this company raise prices without losing volume? Buffett called this "the single most important decision in evaluating a business."
- Moat Type & Trajectory. What is the specific mechanism protecting this business — cost advantage, brand/intangible, network effects, or switching costs? Is the moat widening or narrowing? 
- Long-Term Prospects. What will this business look like in 10–20 years? Does the industry have a long runway for growth? What forces could disrupt it or erode its advantage?
- Competitive Threat (inversion). If a rival had unlimited capital, where would they attack? Would it work? A moat that survives a well-funded attacker is real; one that doesn't is a story.

Management Tenets
- Capital Allocation Rationality. Does management reinvest earnings wisely? Have they resisted value-destroying acquisitions?
- Candor & Integrity. Does management acknowledge mistakes honestly? Does the annual letter read like a PR document or a frank owner's report?
- Institutional Imperative Resistance. Do managers show they can resist the structural forces that corrupt even well-intentioned CEOs: strategy perpetuating itself regardless of results, subordinates producing analysis validating whatever the boss wants, peers becoming the benchmark, and acquisitions happening because the CEO craves action?
- Owner Alignment. Does compensation tie to long-term per-share value creation? Do executives act like long-term owners, not hired hands?

Financial Tenets
- Owner Earnings. What are the actual owner earnings — net income + D&A − maintenance capex − incremental working capital needs? More accurate than reported net income; the true cash the business generates for owners.
- ROE Without Leverage. Does the company consistently earn above-average ROE without depending on debt to achieve it? High ROE propped by leverage is fragile; high ROE on equity alone reflects genuine business quality.
- Survivability. Can the balance sheet endure a once-in-a-century shock without a forced sale or rescue — debt sized for adversity, ample liquidity? Risk is permanent loss of capital, not volatility.
- Capital Intensity. How much reinvestment does the business require to *maintain* its competitive position? Great businesses generate surplus cash; mediocre ones consume it just staying in place.
- Retained Earnings Test. For every dollar retained, has at least one dollar of market value been created? The ultimate report card on management's ability to allocate capital.

Market Tenets
- Intrinsic Value Estimate. Aesop's three questions: how much cash will this business produce over its life, when will it arrive, and with what certainty?
- Price vs. Intrinsic Value. What is the current market price? What is my estimate of intrinsic value? Is the gap meaningful?
- Margin of Safety. Is the discount to intrinsic value large enough to absorb errors in the estimate?
- No Called Strikes. Is this opportunity compelling enough to act on now, or is the right answer to wait for a better pitch? Passing is always a valid decision — there are no called strikes in investing.
- Opportunity-Cost Bar. Is the discount meaningful *versus my alternatives* — the index, a cash yield, the cheapest thing I already own — not merely versus zero?

Behavioural Discipline Tenets
- Pre-mortem - What would make me wrong. What observable facts would prove the moat is narrowing, the economics misjudged, or management untrustworthy?
- When I'd Sell or Buy More. Sell only on moat erosion or a broken thesis, never on price. If price falls on unchanged fundamentals, do I buy more?
- What I don't understand?

### Relevant Metrics

Note: Buffett doesn't screen with rigid cutoffs. He uses numbers to *confirm* a judgment, not to *make* one. When a checklist item flash warning or red, you need to explicitly rationalize the decision.

Profitability & Efficiency:

- ⚠️🔴🟢 10-Year Average ROE > 20%. Confirms long-term capital efficiency and a durable moat.
- ⚠️🔴🟢 Minimum Annual ROE (10-Yr) > 15%. Ensures business resilience even during economic downturns Gross Profit Margin
- ⚠️🔴🟢 (Gross Profit / Rev) > 40%. Indicates pricing power and a non-commodity business. Not competing solely on price. Likely possesses a differentiated product, strong brand, or other competitive advantages.
- ⚠️🔴🟢 Net Profit / Income Margin (Net Income / Rev) > 20%. Demonstrates superior operational efficiency and cost control.
- ⚠️🔴🟢 SG&A Margin (SG&A / Gross Profit) < 30%. Wide-moat companies don’t need to spend much on overhead marketing.
- ⚠️🔴🟢 R&D Margin (R&D / Gross Profit) < 30%. R&D expenses don't always create value for shareholders.
- ⚠️🔴🟢 Depreciation Margin (Depreciation / Gross Profit) < 10%. Great businesses do not require massive, ongoing investments in depreciating assets to sustain their competitive advantage
- ⚠️🔴🟢 FCF growth. Persistent, reliable growth in free cash flow

Financial Health & Prudence:

- ⚠️🔴🟢 Debt-to-Equity Ratio < 0.5. Ensures solvency and low reliance on leverage.
- ⚠️🔴🟢 Adjusted Debt-to-Equity Total Liabilities / (Shareholder Equity + Treasury Stock) < 0.80. Confirm company is financed primarily with equity rather than debt
- ⚠️🔴🟢 Current Ratio (Current assets / Current liabilities) > 1.5. Guarantees short-term liquidity and operational stability.
- ⚠️🔴🟢 Interest Expense Margin (Interest Ex / Operating Income) < 15%. Debt service payments are not a significant burden on operating profitability
- ⚠️🔴🟢 Preferred Stock = None. No funding through preferred stock - a type of equity that combines features of both stocks and bonds. Typically: fixed dividend payment, and priority over common stockholders for both dividend distributions and company assets in liquidation; keep the debt-to-equity ratio lower than issuing bonds.
- ⚠️🔴🟢 Treasury Stock Share Count = Exists & Reducing & Creating values. Buyback happens belows intrinsic value. Tax-efficient ways to return value to shareholders.

Capital Allocation:

- ⚠️🔴🟢 Capex Margin (Capex / Net Income) < 25. Signals a non-capital-intensive business model.
- ⚠️🔴🟢 Retained Earnings Test. Market Value Gain > Retained Earnings. The ultimate report card on management's ability to create value.

It's important to note that Warran Buffet's best investments would fail several of these thresholds:
- **Capex/Net Income < 25%**: BNSF (his largest wholly-owned business) is a railroad. High capex isn't a red flag here; it *is* the moat.
- **R&D/Gross Profit < 30%**: Apple, his largest equity position for years, has meaningful R&D intensity.
- **Debt-to-Equity < 0.5**: Most financial businesses, insurance operations, and utilities he's owned would fail this.
- **Current Ratio > 1.5**: Insurance businesses structurally fail this — it's meaningless for that industry.
- **SG&A < 30% of Gross Profit**: Consumer brands with global marketing (Coca-Cola, Gillette) would fail this.

### Scope Limitations

These metrics are calibrated for **consumer brand and manufacturing businesses** with stable, predictable cash flows in non-financial industries. They would produce misleading signals for:

- **Insurance & financial businesses**: Float-funded balance sheets structurally fail debt and liquidity ratios. Current ratio and debt-to-equity are meaningless; focus instead on combined ratio, reserve adequacy, and underwriting discipline.
- **Railroads & utilities**: High capex is intrinsic to the business model and often *is* the moat (scale infrastructure competitors can't replicate). Capex/Net Income thresholds don't apply.
- **Banks**: Leverage is the product. Standard debt metrics are irrelevant; use return on equity, net interest margin, and loan quality instead.
- **Technology & software**: R&D intensity is expected and often the source of the moat. SG&A can be high during growth phases without signaling inefficiency.
- **Capital-light compounders (e.g. asset managers, marketplaces)**: The depreciation and capex metrics don't discriminate meaningfully — these businesses are already structurally capital-light.

When analyzing any of these industry types, replace inapplicable metrics with industry-specific proxies and apply qualitative judgment accordingly.
