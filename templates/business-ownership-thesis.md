# Business Ownership Thesis

This thesis applies Warren Buffett's investment philosophy — owning "wonderful companies at a fair price" — structured with the Four-M framework: **Meaning** (the economic engine), **Moat**, **Management**, and **Margin of Safety**, closing with a **Pre-mortem**.

Each section below is a set of prompts to answer, not boxes to tick. Write a few lines — or a few paragraphs — under each. The deeper reference (disqualifiers, metrics, scope) lives at the bottom.

---

## Meaning — Do I understand the economic engine?

> The question is about *my* knowledge boundary, not the business's simplicity. A simple business can sit outside my circle; a complex one inside it if I have genuine edge. If I can't forecast the economics ten years out, the honest answer is that this falls outside my circle.

- **What & how.** What do they sell? How do they make money? Who buys, and why?
- **Why it's stable.** Why does that buying behavior persist over time?
- **Industry.** What is the industry structure? What are the headwinds and tailwinds?
- **Operating history.** Is there a long, stable track record — or is it short and unproven?
- **Circle of competence.** Can *I* forecast its economics 10 years out with confidence and have a lasting edge in valuing it? If not, stop here.

## Moat — Is the competitive advantage durable?

> Name the *specific mechanism*, not a generic claim ("strong brand" is not enough). Argue whether it is widening or narrowing, and what would have to change for it to erode.

- **Mechanism.** What specifically protects this business — cost advantage, brand/intangible, network effects, or switching costs? Why can't customers easily defect?
- **Trajectory.** Is the moat widening or narrowing? How long can it compound?
- **Pricing power.** Can they raise prices without losing volume? (Buffett: "the single most important decision in evaluating a business.")
- **Economic evidence.** Does the moat show up in the numbers — high ROE *without* leverage, surplus cash rather than capital consumed, persistent FCF growth?
- **Long-term prospects.** What does this look like in 10–20 years? What forces could disrupt it?
- **Inversion.** If a rival had unlimited capital, where would they attack — and would it work? A moat that survives a well-funded attacker is real; one that doesn't is a story.

## Management — Are they able and trustworthy?

> Lead with capital allocation — the most important and least-discussed management quality. Owner alignment is a supporting signal, not the lead one.

- **Capital allocation.** Have they reinvested retained earnings wisely? Resisted value-destroying acquisitions?
- **Retained-earnings test.** For every dollar retained, has at least a dollar of market value been created?
- **Candor.** Do they acknowledge mistakes honestly, or only celebrate wins? Does the letter read like a frank owner's report or a PR document?
- **Institutional imperative.** Do they resist the structural forces that corrupt even good CEOs — strategy that perpetuates itself regardless of results, subordinates validating whatever the boss wants, peers as benchmark, acquisitions to feed a craving for action?
- **Owner alignment.** Does pay tie to long-term per-share value? Do they act like owners, not hired hands?

## Margin of Safety — Is the price fair enough?

> Answer Buffett's Aesop test, derive an intrinsic value, then compare to price. The required margin scales *inversely* with certainty. A thesis that cannot name a price at which it would *not* buy is not a value thesis.

- **Owner earnings.** What is the true cash for owners — net income + D&A − maintenance capex − incremental working capital?
- **Intrinsic value (Aesop).** How much cash will the business produce over its life, when will it arrive, and with what certainty?
- **Price vs. value.** What is the current price, my value estimate, and is the gap meaningful?
- **Cushion.** Is the discount large enough to absorb errors in my estimate, given the dispersion of outcomes?
- **Opportunity-cost bar.** Is it cheap *versus my alternatives* — the index, a cash yield, the cheapest thing I already own — not merely versus zero?
- **No called strikes.** Compelling enough to act now, or is the right answer to wait for a better pitch? Passing is always valid.

## Pre-mortem — What would prove me wrong, and what then?

> Argue the bull case *and* pre-commit to what would disprove it — written down now, before I'm emotionally long. Risk here is *permanent loss of capital*, not estimate error or volatility.

- **What would make me wrong.** What specific, *observable* facts would prove the moat is narrowing, the economics misjudged, or management untrustworthy?
- **Sell / buy-more discipline.** I exit only on moat erosion or a broken thesis — never on a price move. If price falls on unchanged fundamentals, do I buy more?
- **Survivability.** Does the business endure a once-in-a-century shock without depending on the kindness of strangers — debt sized for adversity, liquidity that never forces a sale at the bottom?
- **What I don't understand.** What remains genuinely uncertain or outside my circle?

## Reference

### Disqualifiers — screen these out first

- Does it compete primarily on price with no durable low-cost advantage?
- Is it a turnaround story? "Turnarounds seldom turn." A hoped-for improvement is not a moat.
- Does the business require debt to produce an acceptable return on equity?

### Relevant Metrics

> Buffett doesn't screen with rigid cutoffs — he uses numbers to *confirm* a judgment, not to *make* one. When an item flashes ⚠️ or 🔴, explicitly rationalize the decision rather than auto-rejecting.

**Profitability & efficiency**

- ⚠️🔴🟢 10-Year Average ROE > 20%. Confirms long-term capital efficiency and a durable moat.
- ⚠️🔴🟢 Minimum Annual ROE (10-Yr) > 15%. Ensures resilience even during downturns.
- ⚠️🔴🟢 Gross Margin (Gross Profit / Rev) > 40%. Indicates pricing power and a non-commodity business — differentiated product, brand, or other advantage.
- ⚠️🔴🟢 Net Margin (Net Income / Rev) > 20%. Demonstrates operational efficiency and cost control.
- ⚠️🔴🟢 SG&A Margin (SG&A / Gross Profit) < 30%. Wide-moat companies don't need heavy overhead marketing.
- ⚠️🔴🟢 R&D Margin (R&D / Gross Profit) < 30%. R&D doesn't always create shareholder value.
- ⚠️🔴🟢 Depreciation Margin (Depreciation / Gross Profit) < 10%. Great businesses don't need massive ongoing investment in depreciating assets.
- ⚠️🔴🟢 FCF growth. Persistent, reliable growth in free cash flow.

**Financial health & prudence**

- ⚠️🔴🟢 Debt-to-Equity < 0.5. Ensures solvency and low reliance on leverage.
- ⚠️🔴🟢 Adjusted Debt-to-Equity — Total Liabilities / (Shareholder Equity + Treasury Stock) < 0.80. Confirms primarily equity-financed.
- ⚠️🔴🟢 Current Ratio (Current Assets / Current Liabilities) > 1.5. Short-term liquidity and operational stability.
- ⚠️🔴🟢 Interest Expense Margin (Interest Ex / Operating Income) < 15%. Debt service isn't a meaningful burden.
- ⚠️🔴🟢 Preferred Stock = None. No funding via preferred stock.
- ⚠️🔴🟢 Treasury Stock = Exists & Reducing & Creating Value. Buybacks below intrinsic value; tax-efficient return of capital.

**Capital allocation**

- ⚠️🔴🟢 Capex Margin (Capex / Net Income) < 25%. Signals a non-capital-intensive model.
- ⚠️🔴🟢 Retained-Earnings Test. Market Value Gain > Retained Earnings. The ultimate report card on management's value creation.

Note that several of Buffett's *best* investments fail these thresholds — the metric is a prompt for judgment, not a verdict:

- **Capex/Net Income < 25%**: BNSF is a railroad; high capex *is* the moat.
- **R&D/Gross Profit < 30%**: Apple, long his largest equity position, has meaningful R&D intensity.
- **Debt-to-Equity < 0.5**: Most insurance, financial, and utility businesses fail this.
- **Current Ratio > 1.5**: Insurance businesses structurally fail it — meaningless for that industry.
- **SG&A < 30% of Gross Profit**: Global consumer brands (Coca-Cola, Gillette) fail this.

### Scope Limitations

These metrics are calibrated for **consumer-brand and manufacturing businesses** with stable, predictable cash flows in non-financial industries. They mislead for:

- **Insurance & financial businesses**: Float-funded balance sheets structurally fail debt and liquidity ratios. Use combined ratio, reserve adequacy, and underwriting discipline.
- **Railroads & utilities**: High capex is intrinsic and often *is* the moat. Capex/Net Income thresholds don't apply.
- **Banks**: Leverage is the product. Use return on equity, net interest margin, and loan quality.
- **Technology & software**: R&D intensity is expected and often the source of the moat. SG&A can run high during growth without signaling inefficiency.
- **Capital-light compounders (asset managers, marketplaces)**: Depreciation and capex metrics don't discriminate — these are already structurally capital-light.

When analyzing these types, replace inapplicable metrics with industry-specific proxies and apply qualitative judgment.
